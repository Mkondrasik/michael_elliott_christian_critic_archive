“Deadpan” must have been the actors’ order of the day in The Life Aquatic with Steve Zissou. Finding an expression on any of these characters faces proved to be as difficult as the central character’s quest to find the elusive “jaguar shark.”

The film follows the misadventures of Steve Zissou (Bill Murray), a cut-rate Cousteau who, despite his flagging popularity, continues to make maritime documentaries with the help of his ragtag crew and a broken down research vessel called “The Belafonte.”

After the aforementioned jaguar shark eats a member of the Belafonte, Steve’s focus turns from research to a kind of passionless revenge. It is an obsession that causes his wife (and the true brains of the operation) Eleanor (Angelica Huston) to abandon ship. Steve must also adjust to two newcomers aboard his vessel: Ned (Owen Wilson), a Kentuckian pilot who may or may not be Steve’s 30-year-old son and Jane (Cate Blanchett), a pregnant journalist looking for a story.

Director Wes Anderson (The Royal Tenenbaums) pads the story by layering a number of subplots upon this Moby Dick story but none of them manage to hold our interest. The love triangle between Ned, Steve and Jane doesn’t play that effectively; the competitive relationship between Steve and his chief rival, Alistair (Jeff Goldblum) is hit and miss; the rest of his crew (including an eager-to-please German played by Willem Dafoe, a Brazilian who croons David Bowie tunes, and a script girl who parades topless half the time) are largely forgettable; and there’s a pirate attack halfway into the film that is so tonally out of place it seems as if it belongs to a different movie.

Admittedly, Anderson’s style doesn’t appeal to some (myself included), but within his films are usually some elements worthy of note. I failed to find much of interest in The Life Aquatic. The story is rather aimless, the performances are listless and perfunctory, and the camera work is simply adequate. If a filmmaker expects to keep his audience in the theater for close to two hours, he really should have a good reason for them to do so. In The Life Aquatic all Anderson gives us is a waterlogged bore.

While it is hard to get a handle on any of these characters, Zissou is probably the most enigmatic of all. We get the sense that he’s not all that interested in new maritime discoveries or exploring the sea for scientific purposes. Instead he seems driven by fame and the many perqs that come with it.

It is in his selfish quest to make a name for himself that he fails to build anything that would make that name meaningful. It certainly wasn’t for lack of opportunity. He simply didn’t care enough for others to look beyond himself.

Living one’s final days filled with regrets of what could have been is a miserable way to end one’s life. It is far better to give of ourselves fully so in our twilight years we might make the same confession as the apostle Paul.

I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith. 2 Timothy 4:7 (KJV)

Paul knew that the end of his days was drawing near. He also knew that he had done all that he could to make a difference in his world. Knowing that he had successfully struggled against temptation, accomplished the God-inspired goals that he had set for himself, and maintained his integrity and his believing in the face of all opposition allowed him the peace of mind to face death with no regrets and great hope for the rewards of the future.

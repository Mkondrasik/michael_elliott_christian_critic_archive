Author-verified transcription

"Are you willing to get your hands dirty?" That is the question that developing magician Robert Angier is asked in the Victorian thriller The Prestige. It informs us that sacrifice is a crucial ingredient to maintaining an illusion.

The title is derived on the latter of three acts that comprises every magic trick: Pledge, where the magician assures the audience that all is normal; The Turn, where something extraordinary occurs; and The Prestige, which restores the world to its normal state and is the final payoff of every illusion. "It's not enough to make something disappear," we are told. "You have to bring it back."

Angier (Hugh Jackman) is a master showman with a major problem. He has a rival. Alfred Borden (Christian Bale), who once worked together with Angier, is every bit the magician he is (though his presentation is somewhat drier.) But he does have a trick that Angier covets because he can't figure out how it is done.

Both magicians try to outsmart or outplay each other as their friendship turns to rivalry; curiosity turns to obsession; and their desire to be the best takes a lethal turn.

Director and co-writer Christopher Nolan is no stranger to the sleight-of-hand or the art of misdirection. He crafts an intriguing story with plenty of twists and turns all wrapped up in a Victorian ribbon that perfectly sets the time, mood and place of his tale. He allows the second act to drag a bit with an over-extended segment set in the Americas as Angier visits Nikola Tesla (David Bowie), a historical figure who, like his contemporary and rival Thomas Edison, experimented with the new science of electricity.

Nolan also takes the third act in a direction that is not well suited for all that came before. It is a disappointment that Nolan could not finish his tale in the same manner that he began it but there is still much to The Prestige that is worth seeing.

There is decent acting across the board with Hugh Jackman and Christian Bale both developing believable characters whose love of their shared craft turns into an obsession. Scarlett Johansson has a small but integral part as the woman who bounces between them. Michael Caine turns in another charming performance as Cutter, entrepreneur and aid to Angier.

The art direction, costumes, and set direction are also superb and help to set the tone for the film. But it is the story itself that will draw us into this world of illusion and keep us guessing until the end.

Both main characters develop obsessions that prove to be their downfall. Life is designed to be balanced. If our lives are out of whack, it is typically because we have elevated a single element to be more important to us than anything else. It is a dangerous and ultimately unsatisfying way to live.

God promotes a more moderate mindset. There's nothing wrong with a desire to be the best, but our competition is not with others... it lies within us. We need not try to outdo the guy next door. We simply should endeavor to develop ourselves to the best we can be and gain our satisfaction in the knowledge that we gave it our all.

For we dare not make ourselves of the number, or compare ourselves with some that commend themselves: but they measuring themselves by themselves, and comparing themselves among themselves, are not wise. 2 Corinthians 10:12 (KJV)

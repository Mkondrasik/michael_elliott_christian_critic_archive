Author-verified transcription

In The Perfect Man, director Mark Rosman makes some great demands of his audience. We're asked to believe that Hilary Duff has never had a date and that men keep walking out on Heather Locklear. We're obviously dealing with a movie about an alternate universe because neither situation is that believable in this world.

Holly Hamilton (Hilary Duff) calls her blog "Girl on the Move" because she is never in one place long enough to call it home. It seems her mother, Jean (Heather Locklear), has a bad habit of running away from every bad dating experience she has ever had. And she has had a lot. After being dumped yet again, Jean starts playing her Patsy Cline records and packs her bags yet again. This time Brooklyn NY is the destination and start of their "new adventure."

Holly sees her mom begin her same pattern of behavior and, hoping to interrupt the cycle, decides to invent a secret admirer to keep her mother from falling into the depression that leads to bubble wrap and packing tape.

Using the dating advice of a new friend's uncle (Chris Noth), Holly begins anonymously wooing her mother. Stupid idea? You bet! Stupid movie? Ditto.

The Perfect Man is yet another thinly constructed vehicle for Hilary Duff who has yet to show much of an acting range. She cries real tears and can look either shy or embarrassed. All these talents are on full display in this film.

Heather Locklear is given the difficult job of trying to rationalize the inconceivably stupid behavior of her character. That she doesn't succeed is not her fault. Blame the screenwriter (The Princess Diaries' Gina Wendkos).

Chris Noth, as the title character, is little more than an empty suit which, I suppose, is how some might describe the "perfect man." Mike O'Malley, as one of the losers who Jean seems to attract, has more to work with although his performance is overly broad and exaggerated.

The best lines belong to the film's stereotypically gay character Lance (Carson Kressley, Queer Eye for the Straight Guy), who preens and prances across the screen in a manner I thought was now considered politically incorrect.

Director Mark Rosman (Cinderella Story) goes through the motions of telling this insipid by-the-numbers story as if he knows that nothing he does will matter in the long run. If the tracks are laid improperly, it doesn't matter how fast you drive the engine. There's no avoiding the train wreck.

Although the film is silly and slight, there is a message worth receiving. Jean and Holly both have to learn that running from problems is not the same as solving them. There is no such thing as a "perfect life" - only a life that is perfectly lived.

We all face problems or obstacles that try to prevent us from accomplishing our goals. The key is not to avoid them as they occur but to work through them while keeping our eyes on the goals we have set.

Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but one thing forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus. Philippians 3:13-14 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

To receive the full experience, The Widow of Saint-Pierre is a film that should not be seen alone. The after film discussion that will take place between those who have seen it should promise to be as stimulating and thought-provoking as the film itself.

Based on a true event and set in a remote French territory off the coast of Newfoundland during 1850, The Widow of Saint-Pierre is a rather simple film which deals with rather grand concepts: crime and punishment; forgiveness and redemption; love and sacrifice; and holding onto one’s ideals regardless of the consequences.

Auguste Neel (played by Yugoslavian director Emir Kusturica in his acting debut) is a fisherman who blows into Saint-Pierre one foggy night. While drunk, he carries a stupid prank too far which results in his brutally stabbing a man to death. Lawfully convicted and condemned to death, he is placed into the custody of the community’s military leader who is known simply as “The Captain” (Daniel Auteuil, Jean de Florette).

French law requires execution by guillotine (AKA “the widow”) and as the small island has no such device, they must request one from the French government. With no overnight service available in 1850, it is going to take a great deal of time to get a guillotine to the small island community, not to mention finding someone among the reluctant townspeople to agree to serve as executioner.

The Captain’s wife, Madame La (Juliette Binoche, Chocolat) opposed to the very idea of the death penalty, decides to use this delay to allow the prisoner to redeem himself in the eyes of the community. As her protégé, Neel demonstrates great nobility and courage, becoming a popular and productive member of their small society. When the guillotine finally arrives, no one wants to see it used, including the Captain who, under the threat of being charged with sedition, refuses to involve his troops.

Juliette Binoche and Daniel Auteuil pair well together, displaying a love and passion for each other’s characters which makes the tragic end even more devastating. Emir Kusturica is a study in quiet dignity and integrity. All three characters are tragically noble and idealistic. By remaining stoically true to their own ideals: Madame La, that the execution serves no good purpose; the Captain, that his beloved wife’s happiness is more important than anything else; and Neel, that no one else suffer because of his actions; they seal their respective fates even as they rush to the inevitable tragedy that ends the tale.

Director Patrice Leconte (Girl On The Bridge) stacks the deck heavily in favor of the anti-capital punishment side but he does so in a non-preachy manner. He simply tells the tale, biased as it may be, leaving it to the audience to draw their own conclusions. The danger, of course, would be to take this heavily romanticized film and try to treat its scenario as if it illustrated the rule instead of the exception.

For the law to be just, punishment should be meted out soon after a conviction. Failure to do so will only lead to confusion and the eventual perversion of justice. The standard that God set in the Old Testament is clear and should serve as a basis for our judicial system today.

And whosoever will not do the law of thy God, and the law of the king, let judgment be executed speedily upon him, whether it be unto death, or to banishment, or to confiscation of goods, or to imprisonment. Ezra 7:26 (KJV)

Obviously, the scenario as presented in the film would lead us to disagree with the above scripture. After all, did “justice” prevail in The Widow of Saint-Pierre? Hardly. Does it mean that the death penalty should never be invoked in our day and time? We can’t infer that. Life doesn’t allow us to paint with such a broad brush. Godly wisdom, which is the application of divine knowledge, enables us to make a proper decision when each troublesome and complex situation arises.

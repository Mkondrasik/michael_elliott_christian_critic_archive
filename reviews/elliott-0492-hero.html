Author-verified transcription

My favorite film of 2001 was a simple Chinese tale told by a brilliant filmmaker, Zhang Yimou. The Road Home told an eloquent and emotional story of romantic love and the honor a son demonstrates for his parents.

Zhang is back with a different kind of film: the long-awaited US release of Hero. Actually, it has been around for a while, even winning an Oscar nomination for best foreign language picture in 2003. But despite the nomination, Miramax has kept the film on its shelf for over a year as it inexplicably waited for the "right time." Thanks to the prodding by Asian film fan Quentin Tarantino who will be presenting the film in its LA/NY limited release, Hero will be given its due screen time and cineastes will be the better for it.

A Wuxian martial arts adventure, Hero fictionalizes the events surrounding the unification of the Chinese territories some 2,000 years ago. Back then, the land was divided amongst seven different kingdoms. The king of Qin (Chen Daoming) is waging war in an attempt to conquer the other six kingdoms. By doing so he hopes to form a Chinese empire and provide peace to all under his rule. His efforts have made him many enemies who would not think twice about assassinating him.

When the news that three powerful assassins have been defeated by one of his warriors, he summons the nameless hero (Jet Li) to his court so that he might hear firsthand how the victory was achieved. The movie then flashes back as the swordsman relates his strategy and tales of combat.

Though the story is an intriguing take on a fascinating period in Chinese history, the artistry of the film is what takes center stage. Like Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon, Hero's martial arts sequences are magnificently choreographed and filled with grace and beauty.

Zhang's use of color, aided by Christopher Doyle's stunning cinematography, help to delineate between each of the film's many stories. As the movie progresses, we are told different versions of the same event. The color-coding scheme keeps us and the film on track while providing an aesthetic quality that is quite remarkable. One sequence among many which stand out in my mind is an extended fight between two females clad in flowing silk and set within autumnal woods. The golden leaves which swirl around the combatants with every movement of the sword are immediately eye-catching.

From a story-telling perspective, Zhang has expressed his desire to take the genre in a new direction. He notes that most martial arts films deal with revenge. Using history as his template, Zhang has crafted a martial arts tale where all the martial artists are driven by noble and selfless desires. While there may be some flaws in the ultimate logic used by these characters, we can reach a level of respect for everyone in the film because their intentions and motivations are honorable.

In one scene, Nameless tries to learn about one of the assassins, Broken Sword (Tony Cheung Chiu Wai), by studying his calligraphy. He claims that the power of his sword is tied to the power of his written word. It is a claim that may strike some of us as being familiar. Scripture often figuratively compares the Word of God to a sword.

For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit, and of the joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart. Hebrews 4:12 (KJV)

No matter how sharp the blade or how skilled the one who wields it, no sword on this earth can penetrate the heart more quickly or accurately than the truth which is contained in God's own Word.

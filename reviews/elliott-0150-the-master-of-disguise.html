The Master of Disguise

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 2, 2002
Rating
1½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

1 Corinthians 11:11 Thessalonians 1:6Philippians 3:17

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Imitation; example; following Christ; role models; Christian conduct

Author-verified transcription

There are some films that tip their hands early on. This is the first film that actually did so before the movie got under way. The studio provided promotional gifts to the screening audience for The Master of Disguise. Everyone in the house was given a whoopee cushion. That was the only clue needed to determine the level of humor that would be in the film we were about to see.

Dana Carvey (Opportunity Knocks) stars as Pistachio Disguisey. He's a dimwitted Italian waiter who was raised without knowing the Disguisey family secret. When his father (James Brolin, Traffic) and mother (Edie McClurg, Ferris Bueller's Day Off) are kidnapped, Pistachio must be told the truth. He comes from a long line of masters of disguise.

With help from his grandfather (Harold Gould, Stuart Little) and his new assistant (Jennifer Esposito, Don't Say A Word) Pistachio goes undercover to rescue his "papa and his mama." It won't be easy. First off, he's not all that smart. Secondly, his opponent is the dastardly Devlin Bowman (Brent Spiner, Star Trek: Generations), who is greedily stealing all the treasures of the world and hoarding them in his underground lair.

Will Pistachio find and rescue his parents? Will Jennifer fall prey to the irresistible Disguisey charms? Will Devlin Bowman succeed in his evil plans? Will the audience ever stop playing with their whoopee cushions?

The Master of Disguise was designed to be a kiddie film and its vapid silliness will most probably inspire a tittering of grade school giggles up and down the theater rows.

This is first and foremost a vehicle for the talented Dana Carvey. After excelling as a sketch comedian on Saturday Night Live, he never quite found the right film offer to showcase his impersonation skills. After a well-publicized medical nightmare, it is nice to see that Carvey is fully recovered and working again. The role of Pistachio Disguisey allows him to drift in and out of one outrageous character after another. His characters are both overplayed and exaggerated, but then again, subtlety has never been his trademark.

Brent Spiner makes for an appropriately villainous villain, although he's been saddled with a "pull my finger" physical ailment... which explains why the studio ordered those whoopee cushions to use as promotional items. Jennifer Esposito is appropriately sexy, in a PG kind of way, as Pistachio's love interest, Jennifer. All other characters are there primarily as set dressing or plot devices.

The filmmakers seem to have made an error in judgment by including jokes that are inappropriate for their target audience as well references that won't be understood. It's doubtful that a theater full of seven and eight year olds will pick up on the allusions to The Exorcist and Jaws. They may laugh at the big-bottomed women to whom Pistachio is attracted but it is a laugh that might make some parents a tad bit uncomfortable.

Pistachio's greatest attribute is his ability to imitate people. It is a quality that all of us have to some degree. God, through His Word, even encourages us to use our ability to mimic. The apostle Paul, speaking by revelation, says:

Be ye followers [imitators] of me, even as I also am of Christ. 1 Corinthians 11:1 (KJV)

It is sometimes easier to adopt a desired behavior when we imitate someone else. We just have to be wise in who we choose to emulate. There isn't anything or anyone worthy of imitating in The Master of Disguise... although that won't prevent many of the young people from trying. Something tells me that those whoopee cushions will be getting quite a workout.

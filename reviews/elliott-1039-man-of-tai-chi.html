Author-verified transcription

Passive meets aggressive in Man of Tai-Chi, Keanu Reeves’ directorial debut. In it, Reeves plays Donaka Mark, a wealthy man providing a service for his even wealthier clients. By running an underground fight ring with a philosophical twist, his purpose is to take an innocent, pure-hearted man practiced in the peaceful disciplines of one of the martial arts and corrupt his chi (or life energy) through temptation and manipulation. He records the journey and broadcasts it to all those who can afford the price. The endgame, of course, will be a fight to the death.

His latest project is Tiger Chen (Tiger Hu Chen), a student of Tai-Chi, whose master (Yu Hai) encourages meditation as being instrumental in keeping his students’ chi in balance. Tiger sees the power potential in Tai Chi as an equal if not more effective means of solving problems. This makes him a prime candidate for Donaka’s illegal activities.

It isn’t long before Tiger is fighting (and winning) against increasingly stronger and more brutal opponents. But at the same time, he’s changing within. He’s becoming harder, less compassionate, less balanced. Can Tiger stop himself from traveling too far down this path before there’s no hope for return?

Keanu Reeves has often been criticized for his wooden acting skills and in Tiger Hu Chen, who was a stunt player for two of the three Matrix films, he has found a protégé to mold in his image. Fortunately the blank-faced stoicism of the lead character fits with this storyline and the fight sequences are expertly done which makes this martial arts film work.

Tiger, in the film, is presented with a crossroads. Should he follow the path being taught by his Tai Chi master who uses the martial arts as a discipline to be controlled and thus bring one’s life into harmony with all other living creatures? Or should he follow his new master who promises wealth and glory by exploring the lethal power that can be unleashed through the techniques already learned?

For a long time, Tiger tries to do both. But he eventually learns the truth that God revealed to us many years ago:

“No servant can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon.” Luke 16:13 (KJV)

As we move through this life, we eventually discover that we cannot straddle a fence for very long before realizing that in order to move anywhere, forward or back, we have to decide which side of the fence we want to be on. Until we choose, we remain stuck on that fence. Once we choose, we have limitless possibilities of what we can do and where we can go.

Author-verified transcription

Political satire works best when those attempting it have a deep or full understanding of the political activity that is being spoofed. Chris Rock's Head of State takes a lazy man's way out by settling for a shotgun approach to humor when a more focused or targeted aim is needed.

Funnyman Chris Rock (Down to Earth) plays Mays Gilliam, a black DC alderman who becomes an unlikely choice for the office of US president after the untimely deaths of his party's candidates just a few weeks before the national election. With so little time remaining before the electorate must cast their votes, no savvy politician is going to throw his hat in the ring to run against an incumbent vice president who also happens to be a war hero. It would be political suicide. And so the party turns to Gilliam who is blissfully ignorant of this fact.

Gilliam quickly learns that his particular appeal does not lie in parroting the party line being fed to him by his handlers but rather in being his "own man," addressing with candor the societal ills facing us all. His fresh "politically incorrect" stances strike a chord with the voters and his popularity begins to rise. The problem is that the leaders of his own party don't actually want him to win. He was merely set up to be a sheep for the slaughter - a token candidate to help gain favor with the black community without actually having to do something for them.

Unfortunately, Chris Rock is rather bland as Gilliam, pulling back quite a bit on his edgy stand-up persona. In this film, the edge and the energy comes from Bernie Mac (The Bernie Mac Show) who brings a little life to the project as Gilliam's brother and eventual running mate. But his appearance is a good example of something that is too little too late.

Lynn Whitfield (Eve's Bayou) and Dylan Baker (Road to Perdition) are the political brains behind Gilliam's losing campaign who become befuddled when his street smarts and straight (if somewhat superficial) talk starts winning over their large white middle class constituents.

Tamala Jones (The Brothers) is given very little to do as Gilliam's love interest while Robin Givens (Book of Love) plays his opportunistic ex-girlfriend who now wants to be first lady in the worst way.

Most of the humor is based on stereotypical observations. While some of the jokes hit the mark, none of them do so with any force. Nothing real deep or meaningful is being attempted here and so it remains a rather shallow experience. This film is not helped by the multitasking Rock who, in addition to writing, acting, and producing Head of State, is making his unimpressive directorial debut.

His character is keen to point out much that is wrong with the status quo and his catch phrase, "That ain't right" can be applied to any number of problems facing our nation. But pointing out what is wrong is not that helpful unless it is followed up with a suggestion on how to make it right.

To look at how it can be done effectively, we need only to look at the teachings of Jesus Christ. Not only did he point out the errors of man's ways, he led us to the correct action that would deliver us from those errors. Reproof by itself has its benefits but they are limited. Reproof followed by correction will lead to deliverance and righteousness.

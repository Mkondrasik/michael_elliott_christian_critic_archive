Author-verified transcription

It is said that money makes the world go 'round. It also makes it go crazy. In Millions, a small boy learns this lesson first hand when a bag of English pounds falls from the sky and into his lap.

Following the death of their mother Damien (Alex Etel) and Anthony (Lewis McGibbon) have moved to a new home in Northern England with their father (James Nesbitt). All three are in the process of learning how to adjust to their loss.

Damien has a strong fascination with Catholic saints, even imagining that they can appear to him long enough to hold a conversation. When the bag of money literally drops from nowhere and lands at his feet, he naturally believes that it is a gift from God which will enable him to emulate his beloved saints and help the poor.

His brother is not as altruistic. When shown the bag of money, Anthony convinces Damien not to tell anyone because of the taxes they'll have to pay. He then proceeds to tell a core group of his school friends eliciting their sworn secrecy not to tell anyone else and thus elevating his status to BMOC (big man on campus).

There is a sense of urgency added to the film through two devices: First, the bag of money arrives on the eve of Britain's conversion to the Euro. The boys are going to have to figure out a way to spend or convert all their British pounds to euros before the deadline or else the money will become worthless. Secondly, there is a big, threatening brute of a man (Christopher Fulford) looking for the cash which he claims is rightfully his.

A straightforward approach would have worked best for the film that is largely told from the naïve perspective of the children. Unfortunately director Danny Boyle allows his directorial hand to get a bit too heavy for my taste. He has a distinctive style which, for me, distracts from the simplicity of the characters and the story they tell.

Alex Etel and Lewis McGibbon do surprisingly well for youngsters making their film debuts. McGibbon as the older boy, who sees the windfall as a way to win friends and influence people, has a rakish quality about him that is both amusing and real. But it is Etel that provides the movie's heart. The earnestness of his faith is extremely touching even if it is based upon Roman Catholic doctrine and tradition rather than being grounded in scriptural accuracy.

Much of the film has Damien talking to the various patron saints of Catholic lore. His inquiring mind wants to know whether or not they know his newly deceased mother. And why shouldn't they? To a small boy, one's mum always qualifies for sainthood.

The film leads us to believe that to become a saint it must first be documented that one has performed a miracle. The truth is that this has never been one of God's requirements. His Word tells us that anyone who has been "born again" and received His spirit can be called a saint. And anyone can be born again if they simply follow God's directions in Romans 10:9:

That if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved. Romans 10:9 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

Would we recognize Jesus Christ if he were walking among us… working with us… standing before us? Such is the question asked in the newest Christian-made film for mainstream audiences, Joshua.

Tony Goldwyn (Ghost) stars as the title character, a drifter who walks into the small town of Auburn and turns things upside down by the simple act of doing good and being genuine. A woodcarver by trade, he lives amongst the townspeople and acts whenever and wherever he sees a need. A church is destroyed by a storm, he starts rebuilding it; a teen feels isolated and disenfranchised, he gives encouragement; a visiting evangelist pretends to heal in the name of God, Joshua, without condemnation, lovingly shows him and his “congregation” how true faith needs no pretense. In no time at all, he attracts followers… people who are drawn to his goodness and the hope that he represents.

Joshua’s genuine, selfless acts and growing popularity are, of course, viewed as a threat to the spiritual hold the religious leaders of the community claim they maintain over their flock. The Roman Catholic representative, Father Tardone (F. Murray Abraham, Amadeus) is especially threatened and is ready to denounce Joshua as a fraud before the Pope himself (Giancarlo Giannini, Hannibal) if need be.

Despite Father Tardone’s apprehension, Joshua’s popularity swells, attracting even members of his own congregation and staff, in particular Father Pat (Kurt Fuller, Scary Movie) who finds himself at odds with his own parish priest because of the signs, miracles, and wonders which follow the young stranger.

At the risk of minimizing the efforts done by those involved with Joshua, the film does seem to most resemble a made-for-TV movie. It is structured in a way that appears like it was almost designed for commercial interruption. Additionally, many of the secondary characters have a superficial, or two-dimensional feel to them instead of the fuller, more substantial depth that might have added to the overall production quality of the film.

Still, the major characters are well acted. Tony Goldwyn does a superb job by keeping his character grounded on a spiritually solid foundation while avoiding the sanctimonious religiosity that often accompanies a movie depiction of a Christ figure.

Director Jon Purdy (Star Portal), while not giving us a stellar example of cinematic artistry, still succeeds because he remains true to the film’s simple and yet poignant message: God is love and we need more love in our lives. It is a vital message in our day and time and one that will touch a nerve in many a heart.

For me, a weakness in the script is in the very inference that Joshua represents the second coming of Christ. Scripture prophecies tell us that the next time Christ treads upon this earth, he’ll be arriving as Lord of Lords and King of Kings. There will be no mistaking who he is or why he has come.

That doesn’t mean that the story of Joshua does not resonate with me. I see Joshua representing the best of all born again Christians. Each of us have the ability to be Christ-like, should we choose to believe the promises of God.

Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me, the works that I do shall he do also; and greater works than these shall he do; because I go unto my Father. John 14:12 (KJV)

You and I can be the Joshua of our communities. We only need to manifest the power of God’s love to all we know. As we see a need, we simply step in to help meet it. We need not hang our heads in shame at the hatred and evil that exists in this world… we can hold our heads high above this world so others will know that there is a better way to live life. It really is as simple as Joshua says. There is too much hate in this world. We need to love more. And no matter what anyone has told us in the past, we can do that.

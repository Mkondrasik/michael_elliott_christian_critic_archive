Cinderella Man

Author-verified transcription

Fairy tales can come true. Director Ron Howard turns the tables on what has thus far been a lackluster year for movies with Cinderella Man - the story of depression era boxer James J. Braddock. This inspirational biopic is the best film I've seen this year and a sure contender for an award or two come season's end.

Russell Crowe stars as Braddock. a heavyweight boxer whose glory days appear to be behind him. Once in line for a title shot by the time the Great Depression hit America Braddock had become something of a sports joke - a guy who has seen one fight too many. On the other hand, he could still boast that after 80 fights. he had never been knocked out.

Early in the film we see him in a bout so unimpressive that the boxing commissioner revoked his license, saying that he was no longer fit to box in sanctioned events. Braddock lost his main source of income at a time when he could ill afford to lose it. He turns to the docks looking for a day's wage as a longshoreman but, during the Depression, he was just one more broke and unemployed guy among the masses. Jobs were scarce and hard to come by.

His debts and financial troubles continued to mount. Just as he found himself against the ropes, unable to provide food or warmth for his family, he miraculously got a second chance to redeem himself.

Allowed to reenter the ring against a highly ranked fighter due to a last minute scheduling emergency, Braddock shows the boxing world that "there's still some juice in [his] legs." What he shows to the rest of the world is measurably more important - that even if a man is down and out, he can still fight his way back to the top. Braddock becomes the working man's hero and a symbol of hope to the downtrodden.

It is a great story and Ron Howard layers it with genuine warmth and emotional depth. As good as Crowe is as Braddock the fighter. he really delivers the goods as Braddock the man. There are many moments packed with the emotional punch of a heavyweight's uppercut. Whether he is teaching his young son that stealing is wrong or humbling himself in order to ask for financial help to keep his family together, Crowe is honest and respectful in his handling of the role.

Renee Zellweger provides solid support as Braddock's loving but concerned wife. Paul Giamatti, as Braddock's trainer, gives a well rounded and fully realized characterization.

Howard is meticulous in his recreation of New York and New Jersey of the 1930s. He builds a clear sense of time and place that adds considerably to our understanding of Braddock's life and accomplishments.

Through this film we come to appreciate so much more than Braddock's skill in the ring. A man of strong moral and ethical standards, he demonstrated many of the qualities that God exhorts us to adopt as our own. His example galvanized a nation and gave its people, at a time of their deepest depression, something to celebrate.

Often, when we are in the midst of a difficult time, what gets us through it is seeing the glimmer of hope that suggests a better day. Braddock, through his determination and force of his will, provided a kind of hope for his generation. Jesus Christ, through his sacrifice, provides hope for all mankind and for all generations.

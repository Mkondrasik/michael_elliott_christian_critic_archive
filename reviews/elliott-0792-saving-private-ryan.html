Author-verified transcription

The horrors of war have never before been so vividly and graphically depicted as they are in Saving Private Ryan, Steven Spielberg's welcome homage to WWII veterans. After a short prelude, the film flashes back to the casualty-laden invasion at Omaha Beach. For the next 28 minutes, we are assaulted with such a cacophony of aural and visual stimuli as to have a visceral effect upon us.

This film is not for the squeamish. War amply demonstrates the brutality that men are capable of inflicting upon one another. Saving Private Ryan does not sanitize that brutality for us. It fully displays the unmistakable ugliness of war at the same time exalting the courage and dedication to duty of the men that lived through it (or died trying).

The movie succeeds in condensing (and therefore personalizing) the war for us via its story line. Three of the four Ryan brothers were killed in military action within days of each other. The military powers-that-be decide to find the sole remaining brother, Private James Frances Ryan (Matt Damon, Good Will Hunting), and send him home to his grieving mother. Trouble is, with all the confusion of the multi-tiered invasion, no one knows exactly where young Pvt. Ryan is. The task of finding him falls on Captain Miller (Tom Hanks, Apollo 13) who accurately describes the mission as looking for "a needle in a stack of needles."

He and his squadron set off through the battle-scarred countryside of France, encountering skirmish after skirmish as they search for Pvt. Ryan. The inevitable question is, of course, raised: "Is the life of one man worth risking the life of another... or eight others?"

The squadron asking that question of themselves consists of Tom Sizemore (The Relic), Edward Burns (She's The One), Barry Pepper (Enemy of the State), Adam Goldberg (Higher Learning), Vin Diesel (Strays), Giovanni Ribisi (That Thing You Do!), and Jeremy Davis (Going All The Way). All of these actors are deserving of more praise than the length of this column will allow. They capture the camaraderie between soldiers that builds in wartime and the various conflicting emotions that course through a man's soul in such a hellish environment. As an acting ensemble, there isn't a weak link among them.

Tom Hanks turns in a bravura performance as Captain Miller, whose military facade is only betrayed by an uncontrollable tremor in his hand. This average schoolteacher and decent family man who is thrust into and excels in an unfamiliar and ungodly arena strives to maintain his humanity, even as he makes decisions that will cause other men to die.

He worries about how he may be changing. He worries about the effects his wartime experiences will have on him. "With every man I kill, the farther away from home I feel." And yet he maintains an enormous respect for his duty and responsibility as an American soldier.

Director Steven Spielberg (Shindler's List) has crafted the best and most socially important film of the year. By taking us on this journey, he leads us to an appreciation and understanding of the sacrifices made by those who participated in World War II.

Was Pvt. Ryan worthy of the sacrifices made by those on the mission to save him? That really isn't the issue. After all, Jesus Christ sacrificed his life to save ours. Who among us were worthy of that sacrifice?

For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God. Ephesians 2:8 [KJV]

The question is not "what did Ryan do to deserve his salvation?" The question is: "What will Ryan do after he has been saved?" That, my friends, is also the key question for each one of us. We know what the Scriptures say:

That ye might walk worthy of the Lord unto all pleasing, being fruitful in every good work, and increasing in the knowledge of God; Colossians 1:10 [KJV]

The mission to save us has been successfully completed. God has given each of us a gift. At the end of our lives, will stand worthy of receiving that gift? Will we be able to say that we earned it?

Author-verified transcription

One man's feast is another man's famine. While some may look at Soul Plane as a biting satire on modern day black culture, others will see only a crude attempt at immature humor based upon sexual and racial stereotypes. Count me as being among the latter group.

After a bad and embarrassing experience with a major airline, Nashawn Wade (Kevin Hart) wins a court case and is awarded one hundred million dollars in damages. He decides to use his newfound wealth to open his own airline -- one catering specifically to the hip hop culture in which he was raised.

His airline, NWA (also the name of the 'Niggaz with Attitude' group who helped to pioneer gangsta rap), is born and prepares to launch its first flight from Malcolm X Terminal in Los Angeles to New York City.

The plane is a pimped out jetliner complete with purple skin, kickin' rims, and a hydraulic system which gives it a little bounce as it muscles its way down the runway. Divided into two classes -- first and low -- the plane is boarded by every type of black person known in the hood. For comic "relief," also along for the ride is a token "honkey" family, the Hunkees, headed up by Elvis Hunkee (Tom Arnold).

Some of the plane's unusual features include coin operated overhead baggage compartments in low class (as well as television monitors connected to foil wrapped rabbit ears); a safety video done MTV style; a bathroom attendant; a drink service consisting primarily of Colt 45 tall boys and "shorty forties;" and an invitation only passenger lounge/rap club.

The stereotypes come at us fast and furious: the sassy security guard "sista" with attitude; the tall muscular black buck who immediately steals the one white woman from the milquetoast white guy; the shiftless, lazy black man always looking for an easy ride; the stoned, ex-con of a pilot who is afraid of heights; the oversexed members of the mile-high club; the white kid pretending to be black; the overweight and flamboyantly gay flight attendant; and a wide assortment of provocatively dressed women willing to be sexually exploited.

There's not much of a story behind the film. Soul Plane consists of a series of Airplane-like sketches and sight gags. Some work. Most don't. And more than a few are downright offensive. The one concession to a semblance of a plot line deals with a girl from Nashawn's past being on board the plane. The filmmakers need not have bothered. It's tacked on, obtrusive and completely irrelevant to the rest of the movie.

NWA's motto is "We Fly, We Party, We Land." The same could be said for the film except, for this reviewer, it lands with a thud. I found little enjoyment and nothing redeeming about this little excursion into a crude urban fantasy.

A satire or a spoof needs to be more specific about how it feels about its targets. Too many of the gags in this film were exploitative. Instead of making a comedic commentary on some aspect, the filmmakers simply went for cheap, salacious laughs. There was no insight offered -- no excuse for enduring what was, for the most part, an exercise in bad taste.

Relatively speaking, it wasn't too long ago that the only roles that black actors could get in the movie industry were "shuck and jive" caricatures. I see very little difference between those depictions and the modern caricatures on display in Soul Plane. Instead of laughing with the actors, I found myself feeling sorry for them.

Like the crackling of thorns under the pot, so is the laughter of fools. This too is meaningless. Ecclesiastes 7:6 (NIV)

Author-verified transcription

As I left for the theater, I was warned that Syriana would be the type of film to separate the intellectuals from the non-intellectuals. Well, someone better put me in the class with the other dummies because this movie totally lost me... when it wasn't putting me to sleep.

It was written and directed by Stephen Gaghan (Traffic), who is no stranger to multi-layered, intricately-woven plots but, with Syriana, he has thrown so much into the mix that none of the ingredients actually have time to set. (I feel like the emperor in Amadeus who complained to Mozart that his newest work had "too many notes.")

In a nutshell, in Syriana, oil is the name of the game that everybody wants to play. Governments are simply pawns for those with real power to use. The politicians will either do the bidding of big business or else they will pay the consequences. The oil rich country of Kazakhstan is the playing field.

The various personalities caught up in the maelstrom of plots and counterplots are: George Clooney as a CIA black operative who has become expendable; Matt Damon as an energy consultant experiencing a personal tragedy at the same time expanding his career; Jeffrey Wright as a lawyer investigating a proposed merger between two oil companies; Alexander Siddig as their heir apparent to the Kazakhstan throne; and a veritable who's who of smaller roles filled by big names (Chris Cooper, Christopher Plummer, William Hurt, Tim Blake Nelson, Amanda Peet).

Following who's doing what to whom and how the multitude of stories are interrelated turns out to be a strenuous exercise that many filmgoers (myself included) will no doubt fail to complete. They say that if one stays plugged into the film and manages to keep the connections clear in one's head, the payoff is satisfactory. Sorry, but they can't prove it by my experience.

Gaghan clearly has much to say about politics and the global industrial/military complex that apparently rules the world but his message is obfuscated by his inability and/or unwillingness to tell his story through a single perspective or point of view. Thus we bounce from character to character with little information about each person's history or back story.

What is clear (and a little scary) is the plausibility with which Gaghan makes his case for how easily governments are manipulated and policies formed to serve the interests of a few.

Americans should bear in mind that all their politicians serve at the bequest of the constituents. To put it simply, we are the boss... they are the employee. It is when this relationship is forgotten that problems begin to mount and corruption enters the picture.

Leadership is, first and foremost, a call to serve. The best leaders are the ones who act according to the needs of those they lead. When self-interest becomes the primary motivation, the leader's service is compromised and he ceases to be effective or desirable in the office he holds.

The tenets of leadership hold true whether we speaking of a political office or a spiritual office.

The Brave One

Author-verified transcription

The tagline for the new pro-vigilante film, The Brave One, begs the question: "How many wrongs to make it right?" Technically speaking, the film may actually answer its own question but it sure doesn't satisfy the questions that will linger in the minds of those who watch it.

Jodie Foster stars as Erica Bain, a NY radio social commentator, who, along with her fiancé (Naveen Andrews), is brutally assaulted while walking in Central Park. She survives. He doesn't. She, no longer feeling safe in the city she loves, buys a gun. That same gun becomes the first step on her journey into vigilante justice.

As Erica is transformed into someone that she herself doesn't know - a faceless, nameless executor of vigilante justice - Detective Sean Mercer (Terrence Howard) is trying to figure out who is killing all the lowlifes in NY and why.

The Brave One is a morally confused and socially dangerous film - starting with the title itself. There's nothing brave about picking up a gun and killing someone with it... no matter how much you believe they may deserve it. But seeing and hearing how the audience responded when Erica the victim became Erica the avenger I fear that my opinion may be the minority one.

Foster gives yet another intense performance but one that never completely rings true. She's at her best in the latter third of the picture when her criminal anonymity (as well as her former sense of self) is beginning to unravel.

Terrence Howard is fine as the sensitive cop Mercer. He is there to serve as the film's moral compass which is why some of his character's decisions were so disappointing to this reviewer. Nicky Katt as a wise-cracking detective provides most of the film's infrequent lighter moments.

Director Neil Jordan has done a bit more than simply make a female version of Death Wish. He seems to be more interested in the transformation of the central character and how she interprets her own journey into the dark side of vigilante justice than he is in the journey itself. What's confusing is what he appears to be saying through her self-analysis.

At the end of the day, no amount of rationalization or justification makes what Erica does right. If Jesus Christ's message to the world teaches us anything it teaches us that one does not overcome evil by taking on its attributes. The second that we cross over the line that separates good from evil, we lose and evil wins... regardless of the temporal outcome.

Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord. Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink: for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head. Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good. Romans 12:19-21 (KJV)

Erica seems to be arguing that her fear is what turned her into the vigilante crusader she was to become. This decision didn't rid her of her fear. It simply channeled that negative energy into something just as destructive. There's only one remedy or cure for fear and had the filmmakers chosen to depict it, The Brave One would have been worthy of its title.

There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love. 1 John 4:18 (KJV)

The Legend of 1900

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 29, 1999
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Proverbs 29:25Matthew 6:25-34Isaiah 45:18

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Fear; trust; freedom; overcoming limitations; courage

Author-verified transcription

A true legend must have, at its center, a figure of mythical proportions. Giuseppe Tornatore (Cinema Paradiso) presents to us such a figure in his new film, The Legend of 1900 (also known as La Leggenda del pianista sull'oceano).

A baby boy is discovered on board the luxury liner Virginian following its New Year's Eve party which celebrated the turn of the century and the beginning of a new millennium. The crew member who found the child, names him after this new year and decides to raise him on board the ship.

1900 grows to maturity without ever leaving the Virginian to discover what "land life" is like. He also discovers a talent for the piano which he develops until he becomes inarguably the finest pianist never heard on land.

The Legend of 1900 appropriately begins with Max (Pruitt Taylor Vince, Mumford), a musician who once played with 1900, telling the story of his friend to the proprietor of a music store (Peter Vaughan, Les Miserables). The film then follows two story lines: flashbacks which describe 1900's life on board the Virginian; and Max's search for 1900 as the ship, now gutted, is scheduled for demolition.

Tim Roth (Hoodlum) adopts a calm but quirky manner for the role of 1900. Bemused by life and people, he is at his most eloquent when he allows his music to speak for him. In one particularly effective scene, 1900 shows Max where he gets the inspiration for his improvised music. Casting his gaze upon the passengers in the music hall, he "analyzes" them by guessing where they have come from and where they might be going. With each passenger, there's a different story and with each story, the music changes.

Exquisitely shot, the film features two memorable scenes. The first, is the initial meeting of Max and 1900 during a violent storm. As the ship rolls and pitches, Max, who can hardly keep his feet beneath him, follows 1900, apparently unaffected by the moving ship, to the grand piano. Round and round the ballroom they travel as 1900 plays. Cinematically, it is a wonderful sequence.

The other is a three round piano duel between Jelly Roll Morton (Clarence Williams III, The General's Daughter) and 1900. Two actors thoroughly enjoying their roles, an unconventional duel which maintains an air of suspense, and some excellent music make this scene one of the year's best.

The score by Ennio Morricone (Bulworth) and Roger Waters (Pink Floyd: The Wall) is magnificent. Ranging from wistful and yearning ballads to jazz that bubbles over with enthusiastic optimism, the music represents the hopes, dreams, and desires of the various passengers traveling for the first time to the promise of America. As 1900 surmised, each passenger does have a story and each story is worth the telling.

The only negative is the aura of sadness that permeates 1900's story. As interesting and talented as his character is, he limits himself and the impact his life can make by remaining on board a ship that only allows him to experience life "2000 people at a time." His expressive face shows his conflicting emotions as he watches the passengers disembark to begin a new and exciting life in a new and exciting country.

Those passengers exhibit a fearlessness that 1900 wishes he had. Looking at the huge, seemingly endless expanse of New York City is all too overwhelming for a man whose entire universe has always fit between stem and stern. In other words, he became comfortable within his box and too bound by a fear of the unknown to leave it.

As the old joke goes, an animal, caught in a trap, will gnaw off his own leg to procure his freedom. Man, in the same trap, will simply hang curtains and call it home.

The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the Lord shall be safe. Proverbs 29:25 (KJV)

The story of 1900 teaches us a valuable lesson. Unless we learn to overcome our fears and venture out to experience all that God has made available to us, we may live our entire lives in prisons of our own making.

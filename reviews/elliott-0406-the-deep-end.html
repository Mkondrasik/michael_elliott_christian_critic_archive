The Deep End

Michael Elliott

Rating

1 hr: 40 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Parenting; teaching children; communication; responsibility

Author-verified transcription

How far will a mother go to protect her son? In The Deep End, that question is answered by Tilda Swinton (The Beach) when her character is faced with an untenable situation with no resolution in sight.

Swinton plays Margaret Hall, a Navy wife/mother of three whose entire life revolves around the activities of her children. Looking for lost baseball gloves, carpooling the neighborhood children, and attending ballet recitals are all part of her normal, ordered routine. When her oldest boy's activities expand to include drinking, driving, and homosexual sex, Margaret finds herself embroiled in a murder investigation and blackmail scheme for which she is ill-equipped to handle. Not that it stops her from trying.

It begins when Darcy Reese (Josh Lucas, American Psycho) the 30 year old "lover" of 15 year old Beau Hall (Jonathan Tucker, The Virgin Suicides) turns up dead. Jumping to a logical conclusion, Margaret, without hesitating, takes extreme action to protect her son from being implicated in the apparent murder. Things become further complicated when thug Alek Spera (Goran Visnjic, TV's ER ) shows up with a graphic sex video of Beau and Darcy and an offer to "sell" it for $50,000. With her husband at sea and unable to be reached, it falls upon Margaret to satisfy the blackmailer's demands and keep the police investigation away from her son, all the while keeping up with the never-ending motherly chores that are a part of her daily life.

Swinton does yeoman work in keeping our interest despite a melodramatic screenplay which ultimately shows its flaws after a strong and engrossing opening. We feel for her character and applaud her unquestioned commitment to her children. She is particularly effective during her scenes with Tucker who plays her teen aged son.

Goran Visnjic, who plays the compassionate blackmailer Alek Spera, is saddled with a character arc that is not explained in the dialogue. Perhaps the book by Elisabeth Sanxay Holding, from which this film is adapted, handles Alek's behavioral choices through exposition. In the film itself, we never get a read on why Alek makes some of his decisions which take him down a rather unexpected path.

Part of the dilemma in which Margaret finds herself stems from the lack of communication which exists between her and her son regarding his sexual experimentation. He's unwilling to talk about it. She's uncertain as to how to approach him. This adds a believable level of tension to the scenario and forces the characters to assume certain facts rather than getting to the truth of the matter. Not dealing with the sex question openly also does the boy no good at al.

As parents, we are always going to face certain situations that might make us feel uncomfortable. Ignoring them, hoping they'll go away, or praying that our children will figure it out for themselves does them a disservice. It is our influence as their parents that must counterbalance the mixed signals our children receive from all other sources.

And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up. Deuteronomy 6:6-7 (KJV)

Parenting is a full time job and entails much more than being a glorified cook or chauffeur. First and foremost, we are teachers.

There isn't any subject too taboo for a parent to address with his or her child when they reach an appropriate age. How else will they be prepared for what the world might try to throw at them later? If we relinquish our responsibility to teach our children right from wrong because the subject matter might be a bit awkward, we leave it to the world to do so... and we know how messed up this world can be.

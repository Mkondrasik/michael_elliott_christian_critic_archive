Digimon: The Movie

Michael Elliott

October 6, 2000

Author-verified transcription

Just when you thought the cinematic waters might be free of those pesky Pokemon, "Digimon: The Movie" invades our theaters to assault our senses with its nearly nonstop digital monster battles.

A cross between Pokemon and Transformers, Digimon are born of digital eggs which suddenly appear in select computers. After extracting them from their computers, the human children, hereafter referred to as the "digi-destined", keep the hatched Digimon and use them to save the world from digital destruction.

Each Digimon has the ability to "digivolve" or transform themselves from the cute and cuddly throw pillows they resemble to armor plated, plasma blasting warriors. This they do at every digi-opportunity.

The movies traces the origin of the Digimon and carries the story through to the present day crisis: a renegade Digimon, infected with a computer virus, who threatens to take over the Internet unless the digi-destined can stop it. And apparently they can only stop it by having their Digimon transform themselves into giant mutants with warlike super powers.

An unfamiliarity with the "Digimon" series may make it difficult to follow the story or keep the characters, who are blandly similar to one another, distinguishable. The story, such as it is, is rather muddled and poorly related. This may be caused by the fact that the film consists of three "Digimon" episodes that have been spliced together.

There is a wry wit to the dialogue in "Digimon" that rarely appears in the "Pokemon" series, but it is overwhelmed by the digi-deluge of animated violence and bass-heavy rock music that underscores the action.

The logic presented in the film also borders on the inane. "Sometimes you've got to save the world, even if you're the one that caused the problem," one of the young characters profoundly intones.

A short animated piece called "Angela Anaconda Goes to the Movies" is a more effective piece in its oddly amusing way. In it, Angela and her friends are excited to see the "greatest movie in the universe"... Digimon: The Movie, of course. Funny, we don't get to see their reactions coming out of the theater.

Despite all the obvious commercial tie-ins which expose the true motivation behind the film's existence; despite the overuse of animated violence; and despite the awful behavioral examples of these digi-destined characters, should your children be exposed to this film, there is a biblical principle that can be taught using the film as an example:

The latter part of the film deals with Willis and his digimon, who live in America while all the other characters live in Japan. Willis must learn the benefit of being part of a team and the additional strength that can come from not acting or being alone.

Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up. Again, if two lie together, then they have heat: but how can one be warm alone? And if one prevail against him, two shall withstand him; and a threefold cord is not quickly broken. Ecclesiastes 4:9-12 (KJV)

The principle of two by two is one that was used by Jesus Christ when he sent his apostles out into the world.

And he called unto him the twelve, and began to send them forth by two and two; and gave them power over unclean spirits; Mark 6:7 (KJV)

Likewise, we are strengthened whenever we choose to act alongside a like-minded individual. Who knows, perhaps the person with whom we are paired will have enough sense to want to see a better movie.

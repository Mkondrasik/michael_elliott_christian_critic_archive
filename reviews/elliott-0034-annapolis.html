Annapolis

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 27, 2006
Rating
1½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

1 Corinthians 12:25-26 Ephesians 4:16 Ecclesiastes 4:12

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Unity; teamwork; one body; mutual care; accepting help

Author-verified transcription

Take a little Top Gun; add a healthy dose of An Officer and a Gentleman; sprinkle a dash of Rocky for good measure; and then remove all traces of a spine that made those films memorable. What is left is Annapolis, an instantly mediocre version of life at the Naval Academy.

Jake Huard (James Franco), aka “Rivet,” grew up across the river from the Academy with dreams of one day wearing the uniform. A poor student from a working class family, the likelihood of his being accepted into the elite institution is almost non-existent... an opinion that he has heard for his entire life.

Through his persistence, Huard is granted a last minute acceptance. He enters the hallowed ground where he immediately is placed at the bottom of the class of plebes.

Singled out as being unworthy to wear the uniform of an officer, Huard is driven unmercifully by Midshipman Cole (Tyrese Gibson) who has made it his personal mission to drive Huard out of the Navy.

Fortunately for Huard, there’s a covergirl of a cadet (Jordana Brewster) who has taken a liking to his form and an Academy tradition of the Brigades... a boxing competition open to all – officers and plebes alike... in which Jake shows some promise. Perhaps by beating up some of his classmates, Huard will earn the respect he hasn’t gained through his disciplined learning.

I’m sorry if I appear to be somewhat dismissive regarding the film. I have the utmost respect for the Navy and for the Academy which trains its officers. Perhaps that is why Annapolis is a bit of a disappointment. There is precious little to respect in this film or its characters.

Huard is written in a superficial, one-dimensional manner and Franco does little to plumb the depths of this character. Brewster is appallingly miscast and totally unbelievable unless Navy recruiters have begun hanging out at local Hooters restaurants to find officer candidates.

Tyrese Gibson does a decent job filling in for An Officer and a Gentleman’s Louis Gossett Jr. as the “drill sergeant” who has it in for the new recruit. But it is Vicellous Reon Shannon, as an overweight plebe trying to live up to his small town’s expectations, who does the best job in connecting emotionally with the audience.

It is a connection that is badly needed. Most of the film is filled with stock characters and predictable situations that we’ve seen in countless movies (both good and bad). Annapolis is not one of the bad ones but neither does it do anything to elevate itself above the middle of the pack..

Huard learns a valuable lesson during his plebe year. Because nobody seemed to think he was worthy to be a naval officer, he set out to prove them all wrong – neither seeking nor accepting help from others. In an environment like the Academy, that is a recipe for failure. As John Donne so eloquently wrote, “No man is an island.” Perhaps that is why God called us to stand together as one body. As we unite and pool our talents and abilities, we increase in strength and might. It is a lesson Huard needed to learn. It is a lesson that would serve us all well to know.

That there should be no schism in the body; but that the members should have the same care one for another. And whether one member suffer, all the members suffer with it; or one member be honoured, all the members rejoice with it. 1 Corinthians 12:25-26 (KJV)

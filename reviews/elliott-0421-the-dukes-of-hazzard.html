Let's face it. The Dukes of Hazzard was never intended to be high brow entertainment for the intelligentsia. But, by the looks of the new movie version of the 1980s iconic TV show, someone thinks that audiences have gotten a whole lot dumber in 20 years.

Seann William Scott and Johnny Knoxville have taken Luke and Bo Duke from being a couple of fun loving, fast driving good ole boys to being a pair of fun loving, fast driving morons. In other words, there's nothing "cool" about them.

The plot, as ridiculously simple as it is, is actually more complicated than it needs to be. While running moonshine for their Uncle Jesse (Willie Nelson) in a souped-up Dodge Charger affectionately known as General Lee, the Duke boys stumble upon a plot by the wealthy but unscrupulous Boss Hogg (Burt Reynolds) who has confiscated their land on trumped-up charges.

They try to unravel the plot while being pursued by Hazzard's woefully inept sheriff's department which includes Roscoe P. Coltraine (M.C. Gainey) and Deputy Enos Strate (Michael Weston). Their "research" takes them from a nearby university comprised of female coeds in various degrees of undress to a countrified road rally that pits the General against NASCAR drivers.

The filmmakers take full advantage of Jessica Simpson's talents. She plays the micro-short wearing Daisy Duke whose cleavage pops up whenever the boys need a diversion (which seems to be about every 7 minutes).

Whatever it was that led to the appeal of the TV series, and there is no denying the appeal factor of the show, it did not make the leap to the big screen. This film is as enjoyable as a flat tire and moves along about as smoothly.

The major problem, apart from the general direction of the project (helmed by Super Troopers' Jay Chandrasekhar), can be found in the casting or perhaps in the attitude of the cast. There just doesn't seem to be much respect given to any of the original characters. The two exceptions are the voice over narration provided by someone who sounds an awfully like the late Waylon Jennings, and, of course, the car itself.

Scott overplays his affection for the Dodge Charger and gives new meaning to the term "auto-erotica." Knoxville goes from Jackass to "dumb-ass" by turning Luke into a sex-crazed, juvenile-minded hillbilly.

Back in the 80s, Catherine Bach's Daisy managed to be sexy without letting the audience know that she knew that she was. Simpson's character simply tries too hard to be sexy relying on her curves instead of her charisma to get attention. And Burt Reynolds, as Boss Hogg, was simply a major miscalculation.

None of these "movie stars" bring anything to erase or compliment the memory of the actors who originated the roles.

Chandrasekhar does give us a few touches that bring to mind the original show: clips of the General sailing impossibly high over a ravine; and freeze frames which allow the narrator to insert a wry country comment or two. But his approach is too crude and his execution is too muddy for the film to do anything more than spin its wheels and go nowhere.

The film uses diversions throughout the story line. Boss Hogg uses the road rally to keep people away from the courthouse. Daisy uses her feminine wiles to keep the cops away from her cousins. The Duke boys use their reckless driving to keep the cops away from Daisy.

Misdirection has long been a ploy used by those who wish to manipulate people and keep them from discovering something that is important. Our spiritual adversary is a master at this technique.

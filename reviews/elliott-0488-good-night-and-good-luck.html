Author-verified transcription

It may be hard for today's younger generation to imagine, but there was a time when true intellectual discourse could be found on television. A time when news anchors were not just whitened teeth in empty suits reading from a teleprompter but serious journalists to be respected and trusted. Such a time is brilliantly reconstructed in George Clooney's Good Night and Good Luck.

The title refers to the familiar sign off phrase of broadcaster Edward R. Murrow and the film takes us back to his famed standoff with US Senator Joe McCarthy who in the 1950s, as chairman of the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations used threats, innuendos, and fear based tactics to uncover Communists in our midst. Murrow and his CBS See It Now producer, Fred Friendly, risked their careers and reputations by publicly taking a stand against McCarthy, using the new power of television to state their case.

Now, as then, McCarthy is his own worst enemy and writer/director George Clooney takes full advantage of it, not casting an actor to play the junior senator from Wisconsin but to let archival footage of McCarthy in action speak for him. It is as damning now as it was then.

David Strathairn is wonderful in the role of Murrow. He captures the cadence of the well known newsman in a performance so controlled and studied that at times we'd be forgiven if we forgot that we weren't watching a documentary on the event.

Clooney adds his much needed good humor and charm by casting himself as Murrow's producer Fred Friendly. His affability lightens an otherwise somber and humorless film. Also in a supporting role is Frank Langella, as CBS chairman William Paley, who sees CBS as a business first and recognizes the backlash that may harm the company as a result of Murrow's unrelenting pursuit of the truth.

Robert Downey Jr. and Patricia Clarkson play a married couple who must keep their union a secret from their co-workers due to a company policy. Theirs is the only real "fluff" to an otherwise economically constructed piece. There aren't a lot of other extraneous subplots cluttering up the core element of the film - Murrow's determination to take a stand against what he perceives as a wrong.

Hindsight being what it is it is easy to forget just how much of a risk Murrow and Friendly were willing to take. McCarthy was a powerful man and his actions had powerful support. Those who did oppose him, subjected themselves to ugly allegations, public and personal attacks of character and motivation. Lives and careers were destroyed. For this reason, most were willing to let McCarthyism die a natural death instead of acting to end it sooner.

Murrow was unwilling to stand by silently. His was an act of courage and integrity that reminds us of the courage displayed by the leaders of the first century church who were warned, under threat of death, not to preach the gospel of Jesus Christ.

And they called them, and commanded them not to speak at all nor teach in the name of Jesus. But Peter and John answered and said unto them, Whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye. For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard. Acts 4:18-20 (KJV)

It takes no courage to do what is right when there is nothing standing in one's way. The mark of integrity is to stand by one's beliefs even when the risk of personal loss is great.

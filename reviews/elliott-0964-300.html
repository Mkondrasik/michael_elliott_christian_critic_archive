Sermon / spiritual topic: Sacrifice

Author-verified transcription

One thing is certain about Zack Snyder's new film 300 - no one could ever confuse it for a "chick flick." This testosterone-filled sword and sandal epic tells (in a very loose sense) the story of how a small band of Spartan warriors held off the entire Persian army for days before being betrayed into their eventual defeat.

Based on the Frank Miller graphic novels that liberally relate the tale, Snyder employs the same visual tricks and CGI-candy of Miller's other adapted work, Sin City.

In 480BC, Xerxes (Rodrigo Santoro), a Persian king who likens himself to a god, is on the march with an army so powerful that victory is all but guaranteed. When the leprous elders of Spartan pay too much attention to the young naked oracle who prophecies against war, they rule that the army may not be marshaled against the invading horde. Spartan king Leonidas (Gerard Butler) decides to take matters into his own hand. He and 300 of his most loyal and brave followers go off to stop the 100,000 plus soldiers of Persia. And they almost pull it off.

The movie is visually amazing, historically suspect, and a bit slim when it comes to narrative details. If you like watching ripped torsos and muscled thighs as they charge slo-mo into battle... if flailing swords and thrusting spears accompany the constant pounding from a music track that tries to surround-sound you into submission is your cup of tea... this may be the film you've been waiting for. It's been a long time since Steve Reeves donned a toga but he would have felt at home among the 300.

Instead of Reeves, we have Gerard Butler (The Phantom of the Opera) as Leonidas overflowing with the machismo we'd expect from Spartan warriors. While not flexing his predominantly featured pecs or abs, he's bellowing out inspirational platitudes to his men.

Rodrigo Santoro as Xerxes is the victim of a production designer's failed vision. Less a god/king than a 80s rock star has been, his performance is lost amid the ridiculous garb and makeup that defines his character.

Other actors fare better but even the best among them is unmemorable in comparison to the visual effects and stylized action that is featured in the film. Unfortunately, our interest in the special effects waned after the first twenty minutes or so and without anything else of substance to hang onto, we were left disappointed and dissatisfied.

But there is a saving grace - a spiritual message buried amid the carnage and bloodletting. Leonidas, his defeat certain, dispatched a messenger home to tell word of the victory he and his men had wrought. He was a wise man and one that saw beyond his own circumstances.

Yes, he knew that he and his 300 men were defeated. But he also knew that his sacrifice would awaken the Greek city states into unified action against a common threat. This was the victory to which he referred. It was not the battle that resulted in his death but rather the action that would arise from his death.

Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ had the same vision and mindset - even as he allowed himself to be led to slaughter.

Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God. Hebrews 12:2 (KJV)

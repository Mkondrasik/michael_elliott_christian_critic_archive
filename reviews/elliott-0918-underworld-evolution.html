Underworld: Evolution

Sermon / spiritual topic: Reproof

Author-verified transcription

Correct me if I am wrong but doesn't the word evolution suggest that something must grow or develop into something else? Then why is it that Underworld: Evolution bears all the markings of the original without showing any signs of improvement?

As in the original, there exists in this world a brutal and raging war between Vampires and Lychens (or werewolves). Underworld: Evolution first takes us back to the beginning where, in the 13th century, two brothers, Marcus (bitten by a bat) and William (bitten by a wolf), each became the first of a new, frightening species which immediately were set at odds against each other. William is captured and (against his brother's wishes) sentenced to eternal imprisonment. No points for guessing who the villain of this film will be.

We fast forward eight centuries to find that forces are now trying to free William from his hidden living tomb. Standing in the way are vampire "death-dealer" Selene (Kate Beckinsale), and a hybrid (a creature who is both Lychen and vampire) named Michael (Scott Speedman).

Lots of blood, gore, and dismemberment later, we arrive at a final showdown. After that is done, we can leave the theater... thankfully.

As in the original film, much of the movie takes place in darkness since, as we should know, vampires and Lychens are nocturnal. Director Len Wiseman uses a great deal of special effects, gallons of fake blood, and little logic to craft this pseudo horror/action picture.

At the core of film's problem is a script by Danny McBride who penned the original and has apparently not learned anything in the process. The movie is poorly constructed, confusing to follow, and lacks the heart that might encourage us to care about its outcome.

Kate Beckinsale wears her leather outfit well; Scott Speedman looks like he's been working out with a trainer; and all the CGI creatures bleed on cue. That about sums up the film.

We do learn that centuries worth of death and warfare might have been avoided had the father of Marcus and William taken action back when they first started to get uncontrollable. He chose not to act because he could not bear to take action against his own sons. Sadly, that is no excuse.

We see, in a less graphic manner, this principle reflected in biblical times. When king Samuel was old, he appointed his sons to be judges over Israel. His sons were corrupt and used their position for their own personal gain. It was within Samuel's power to take action against his sons and protect his people but he could not bring himself to do so. The nation suffered as a result.

Reproof and correction are the ways of life. When we withhold corrective measures from our children who may be misbehaving, we are, in effect, condoning their actions and ensuring that they will continue down a path of disobedience.

The rod and reproof give wisdom: but a child left to himself bringeth his mother to shame. Proverbs 29:15 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

The tagline of Matrix: Revolutions is “Everything that has a beginning has an end.” And thus it is with the film series itself which first launched into public awareness in 1999. Four years later, the Wachowski brothers are back for a third and final visit to the strange sci-fi world of The Matrix.

When the story last ended, Neo (Keanu Reeves) was lying unconscious on a medical table after somehow using his Matrix powers in the real world; the machines were beginning an assault on the human city of Zion; Mr. Smith (Hugo Weaving) had become a rogue program able to replicate itself and we (the audience) had more unanswered questions than the Wachowski Brothers had bullets to spare.

Those questions do get answered in Revolutions. How satisfactorily we find the answers may depend upon one’s level of involvement into this tale. For those viewing the film as mere sci-fi entertainment, the explanations will suffice although the first act of the film may drag a bit. For those looking for deep spiritual or philosophical meaning, the explanation might be found a bit wanting.

After Neo awakens, he knows he must revisit the Oracle (Mary Alice) to ask specific questions regarding who he is and what he must do. At the same time, Zion is under increasing pressure from the machines whose invasion force will undoubtedly overwhelm the hopelessly outmatched humans.

As Trinity (Carrie-Anne Moss) and Neo head off in one direction to find a solution in the above ground Machine City, Morpheus (Laurence Fishburne) and Niobe (Jada Pinkett Smith) race back to Zion to help defend against the invading hordes.

The second film (Reloaded) seemed to me to be weighted down and a bit pretentious, lost in its own patchwork quilt of philosophical and spiritual concepts. It didn’t help that the film seemed intended to be a build up to the finale rather than as a stand alone part of a trilogy. Half a story is never very satisfying.

The third film has the benefit of wrapping the story up and so the narrative is far stronger and much more interesting to watch.

Common to all Matrix films has been stunning special effects work and Revolutions is no exception. Morpheus, Trinity and Seraph (Collin Chou) have a “bullet-time” battle as they pay the Merovingian (Lambert Wilson) another visit; Neo and Agent Smith outdo the “Burly Brawl” of the second film with a climactic clash of the titans “Super Burly Brawl;” and the machine’s siege on Zion is as remarkable a sci-fi war as we’ve ever seen.

Because much of the film is set in the human world, the violence is less stylized and more framed in the reality. A fight that takes place aboard one of Zion’s ships is particularly gruesome and graphic, more than justifying the R rating.

Matrix: Revolutions revisits four main themes - all of which are elements of life that God emphasizes as being particularly important: The energizing power of love; the activating power of faith or believing; the strengthening power of hope; and the God-given right of choice.

Throughout the three films we see characters motivated by love, acting on what they believe, and resting in or relying on hope. God, in His infinite wisdom, even tells us which of those three is most important.

“And so faith, hope, love abide (faith - conviction and belief respecting man’s relation to God and divine things: hope - joyful and confident expectation of eternal salvation: love - true affection for God and man, growing out of God’s love for and in us), these three; but the greatest of these is love.” 1 Corinthians 13:13 (Amplified)

Equipped with faith, hope, and, most especially, love, man can accomplish great and wonderful things. It is all dependent upon whether or not he makes the free will decision to do them.

Author-verified transcription

Mike McDermott (Matt Damon, Good Will Hunting) is a law student with a promising career ahead of him, a beautiful girlfriend who will stand by him, professors who are beginning to take notice of him... If he plays his cards right, he can go right to the top. And therein lies the problem. Mike McDermott knows his cards only too well. He is a poker player... a very good poker player. And in the underground world of high stakes poker, being very good can get you into very big trouble.

After losing everything in an underground game run by Teddy KGB (John Malkovich, The Man In The Iron Mask), Mike decides to retire from the game. All is going well until his childhood friend, Worm, is released from prison. Worm (Edward Norton, Primal Fear) has accumulated some debts while behind bars and the person to whom he owes is not the forgiving type. In order to help Worm, Mike returns to the tables.

Mr. Damon does a fine job as Mike although, as the character with whom the audience is to associate, he plays him as a "straight arrow". An arrow who has developed an uncanny ability to read people, thereby reading their poker hands.

Mr. Norton is far more interesting to watch, playing Worm as a loser who cannot stop manipulating the card deck, using skills perfected while in prison, in order to deal himself or Mike winning hands.

These two characters are obviously constructed to illustrate the two opposing approaches to the game of poker. Worm views the game as merely an easy way to make money. The pot is his only motivation. The other players are only "marks" to be separated from their dollars.

Mike, on the other hand, sees the game in a purer form. While money is still the yardstick by which one's poker abilities are measured, the motivation for Mike is in the playing of the game. He sees himself able to sit at the same table with the best in the world. For Mike, it is a game of skill, not greed.

Director John Dahl (Unforgettable) keeps the movie running at a brisk pace. The screenplay by newcomers David Levien and Brian Koppelman doesn't always explain the poker terminology that peppers the dialogue, but they never seem to lose us, even in their unfamiliar and unusual world of high stakes (and illegal) gambling.

Martin Landau (The X-Files) is cast as one of Mike's law school professors who, in one scene, seems to be talking Mike out of law school and back into the world of poker. His point, in summation, was: "To thine own self be true."

Spiritually speaking, pretty bad advice. The blessings of life come when we are true to something that lies outside of our own selves... The Word of God.

Gambling, whether it is poker, the race track, a state lottery, or even a church raffle, is wrong. One person wins at the expense of many others. This redistribution of wealth is not a godly increase. With God, there can be no losers for God is no respecter of persons. In a godly transaction, all parties must benefit.

We take money from our employer, but in exchange, we give him the labor for which he's paying. We give money to the grocer, but in exchange, we receive food and dry goods of equal value. We give gifts of love to someone about whom we care, but we receive the warmth and joy of knowing we blessed that person.

In gambling, everyone wants to win but within its structure, that is impossible. Regardless of Mike's love of the "pure" game, gambling appeals to the basic greed in man which, if encouraged, can lead to major spiritual problems. All our spiritual opponent needs is a toehold in our minds... a crack in the armor that he can exploit. It is better not to play that hand. Fold the cards and walk away from the table. The devil can't win if we don't play his games.

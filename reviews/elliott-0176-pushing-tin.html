Pushing Tin

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 23, 1999
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Pride; competition; comparison; humility; self-improvement

Author-verified transcription

Pushing Tin gets its title from the highest compliment that can be given to an air traffic controller: "He pushes tin." This means that he moves the planes through his assigned air space quickly and efficiently (without them running into each other.)

In 1996, Darcy Frey wrote a widely respected article for The New York Times Sunday Magazine. In it he examined the stress levels of a job where mistakes or indecision carry dire consequences. Pushing Tin takes the findings of the article and uses it as the framework for its fictionalized story.

Nick Falzone (John Cusack, Grosse Pointe Blank) is the best of the best. Nicknamed "The Zone" for his uncanny ability to mentally focus solely on the screen and its blips in front of him, he operates in the rarefied air of the busiest air traffic control room in the country (TRACON). Brash, empirically confident, and highly competitive, Nick excels in a world unfamiliar to most of us. A world which calls for clear thinking and quick decisions during situations where hundreds of lives literally hang in the balance.

Enter Russell Bell (Billy Bob Thornton, A Simple Plan), an enigma of a character with a quiet manner, a sexy young wife, and an ability to push tin like no other. Nick's type-A personality revs into overdrive as he reacts to Russell as a threat to his position as top dog.

The testosterone flows freely as Nick and Russell engage in a series of increasingly stupid contests vying for that elusive title of being "the better man." From "who can hold a lit match the longest?" to a free throw contest at a neighborhood cook-out, these silly games are taking their toll on Nick's ego. The contest gets personal as Nick seduces Russell's wife (Angelina Jolie, Playing by Heart). It isn't long before the competition moves to the control room as safety and reason take a back seat to ego mania.

John Cusack is excellent as a man whose own self image is destructively linked to how well he compares himself to others. Billy Bob Thornton is an effective counterpoint as the weird but unflappable Russell. The scene where he stands beneath a landing airliner to "experience" wind turbulence is a hoot. (Note: Do not try this at home.)

Australian actress Cate Blanchette (Elizabeth) as Connie Falzone shows that she has the ability to "do American," but Angelina Jolie is a disappointment as the shallow and one-dimensional Mary Bell.

I had the pleasure of attending a screening of Pushing Tin with an invited audience of local air traffic controllers. You could cut the machismo with a knife. Prior to the film, the excitement and anticipation emanating from those around me was palpable.

"About time they made a movie about us," I heard, "This is going to be our Top Gun!"

As the movie opened, the screen flashed a quote from one of their own. It received the biggest laugh of the evening: "You land a million planes safely. Then you have one little mid air and you never hear the end of it."

By the end of the movie, which was admittedly contrived and improbable, their mood had shifted. Gone was the expectation of "Top Gun pride" and I detected a slight twinge of resentment (slightly unjustified) at the way their profession was depicted.

What they should recognize is that the movie didn't take a dig at their profession per se. Ego and pride exist in all areas and arenas of life.

The measure of a man is not in being THE best, but in being HIS best. Making comparisons will invariably lead to envies, jealousies and destructively negative emotions.

Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory; but in lowliness of mind let each esteem other better than themselves. Philippians 2:3 [KJV]

Competition is only valuable in how it helps us to improve ourselves, pushing us to do better. When our focus becomes the competition itself, with winning being the only prize, we have lost sight of the ultimate benefit of the race. Instead of strengthening ourselves, we have begun a course of self-destruction. A course with which Nick has become all too familiar.

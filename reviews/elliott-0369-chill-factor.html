Chill Factor

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
September 1, 1999
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Wisdom; prudence; responsibility

Author-verified transcription

Fifty seems to be the magic number when making a bomb. In Speed, the bus had to stay above 50 mph or it would explode. In Chill Factor, the bomb has to stay under 50 degrees, or kA-boom. Good thing our heroes have an ice cream truck.

Sounds like a great idea for a spoof on Speed, doesn't it? It's too bad the filmmakers played it straight instead of for laughs.

David Paymer (Payback) is Dr. Richard Long, the scientist who developed the weapon, nicknamed "Elvis" for no apparent reason other than to provide the screenwriters with the opportunity to throw in some fairly lame jokes: "Where's Elvis?" "He's dead, last I heard."

It has been ten years since Elvis' first "sighting," a test which resulted in the death of eighteen servicemen, the imprisonment of Maj. Andrew Brenner (Peter Firth, Mighty Joe Young), the disgraced military commander in charge of the test site, and a guilt complex the size of Montana for Dr. Long. Upon his release from Leavenworth, Maj. Brenner assembles a tactical assault team in order to steal the device and sell it to the highest international bidder.

Dr. Long, to keep Elvis from falling into the wrong hands, decides to entrust it to Tim Mason (Skeet Ulrich, Scream), a convenience store clerk with a guilty secret of his own and Arlo (Cuba Gooding, Jr., What Dreams May Come), a wisecracking ice cream vendor who just happened to be there.

What follows is a predictable, mindless chase movie dotted with explosions, helicopters, squabbling heroes and lots of ice.

Director Hugh Johnson, in his first outing at the helm of a feature film, has not deviated too far from the Speed template obviously used during the constructing of Chill Factor. But the gimmick of needing the bomb to be kept temperature-controlled does not carry the same tension or sense of urgency that worked so well for the speed-controlled bomb in the other movie.

The relationship between Arlo and Tim also doesn't connect. Individually, Mr. Gooding and Mr. Ulrich do respectable jobs, (although Mr. Gooding does have a tendency to hit the same shrill note throughout the film) but there isn't a chemistry between them that we've come to expect in "buddy" pictures. David Paymer, as the guilt-ridden scientist, has some nice scenes but he disappears early in the film.

Peter Firth plays the traitorous Maj. Brennan as an emotionless machine. An interesting choice which ultimately becomes uninteresting to watch. On his team of bad guys is Hudson Leick, familiar to TV audiences as the villainous Callisto on Xena: Warrior Princess). She plays a gorgeous blonde turned professional mercenary, an incredulous combination we see all too frequently in bad movies which cast in order to appeal to audience demographics rather than to serve a story.

Of course, with this story, which has numerous holes in logic, such a decision may be excusable. This is not a film that demands a great deal of introspective analysis. If you like explosions, Chill Factor has a bunch of them. Be advised, the flesh-melting effects of "Elvis" are needlessly graphic.

The first disastrous test of the device was a result of an impetuous decision. The lesson learned is, "Power without caution is death." With power comes a responsibility to exercise that power with wisdom.

For wisdom is better than rubies; and all the things that may be desired are not to be compared to it. I, wisdom, dwell with prudence, and find out knowledge of witty inventions. Proverbs 8:11-12 [KJV]

Acting without knowledge is acting unwisely, resulting in undesirable effects. A wise person will consider his ways and then take action accordingly. Dr. Long acted presumptuously by proceeding with a test before receiving all the data by which to make a sound decision. This proved to be a fatal error.

To sum up, Chill Factor may not pack the explosive power of Elvis, but neither is it a complete dud. But, armed with this knowledge, you may now use wisdom in choosing whether it is a film that you wish to spend your money to see.

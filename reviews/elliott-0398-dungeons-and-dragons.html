Sermon / spiritual topic: Imagination; spiritual error; parenting; good and evil

Author-verified transcription

I suppose it was inevitable that an enterprising young filmmaker would latch onto the title of the mystical and magical role-playing fantasy "game" known as Dungeons & Dragons, converting it to a loud, dark and mind-numbingly idiotic movie. Now in theaters, the film may prove to exceed even those low expectations, being more childish, spiritually dark, and amateurishly crafted than originally thought.

What is most odd about Dungeons & Dragons is that it seems to want to cater towards the preteen set. Either that, or the filmmakers severely underestimate the average intelligence of today's teens. The problem with the former is that they've made the film so violent that most parents would hesitate (rightfully so) to allow their young children to attend. And this, of course, will not be the only parental objection towards a film which is based upon a role-playing game set amidst a fantasy world of wizards, demons, and supernatural powers.

The kingdom of Izmer is ruled by the benevolent child empress, Savina (Thora Birch, American Beauty) who cannot tolerate to see the unjust disparity of her people. The powerful elite known as Mages use their magic to prosper while the magic-less commoners struggle for their very existence. She aims to unite her people so that all will be considered as equals.

This sets up a political struggle between her and the evil Mage, Profion (Jeremy Irons, The Man in the Iron Mask) who plots to dethrone her and seize control of the empire for himself. But as long as she holds the scepter which allows her to command Izmer's Golden Dragons, he does not have the power to usurp her authority.

When word leaks out about the existence of the Rod of Savrille, which gives whoever holds it power over the stronger Red Dragons, Profion sees his opportunity and the race between good and evil is underway. Everything builds to the final climatic battle between the two color squads of dragons.

Operating on the side of good are two kindhearted thieves, Ridley (Justin Whalin, Jimmy Zip) and Snails (Marlon Wayans, Requiem for a Dream), a low-level Mage named Marina (Zoe McLellan, Stranger in My House), a giant red-haired dwarf named Elwood, and Norda, an elf tracker.

On the side of evil is Damodar (Bruce Payne, Highlander: Endgame), Profion's number one henchman, and an army of faceless, nameless, and apparently expendable soldiers.

The film suffers from more than the premise - the dialogue is stilted and sounds rather awkward coming from the mouths of these young actors. The dragons are too loud and the dungeons are too dark. Jeremy Irons works very hard at trying to convey the evil intent of his character. The rest of the cast seems content to get by on their appearance and the film's magical special effects. However, the only magic that works in this film is the disappearing act which it should perform a few weeks after its release.

Parental caution is certainly called for when it comes to films such as these. It is not that this film is inherently evil. But when such spiritual error goes unchecked, evil does have free rein. Many games, TV shows, or movies which choose to target a children's audience may contain elements which on the surface might seem innocuous but serve nonetheless to raise spiritual doubts or questions while, at the same time, titillating a child's fanciful imagination.

We, as parents, must find the proper balance whereby we may continue to encourage our children in their imagination but lead them toward the source where their imagination can be fueled by godly truth.

We should always remember that imagination, left undirected, merely leads to a worthless end:

This evil people, which refuse to hear my words, which walk in the imagination of their heart, and walk after other gods, to serve them, and to worship them, shall even be as this girdle, which is good for nothing. Jeremiah 13:10 (KJV)

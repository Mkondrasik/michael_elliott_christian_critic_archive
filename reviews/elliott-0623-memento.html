Author-verified transcription

Anyone interested in a different movie-going experience really ought to take a look at Memento. It is one of the more innovative film concepts to have come along in quite a while.

Leonard Shelby (Guy Pearce, LA Confidential) is a man on a mission. He just can't always remember what that mission is. During an attack in which his wife was raped and murdered, he suffered an injury causing him to lose his ability to form new memories. Everything before the injury, he can remember. Anything he does now, he'll forget within a few minutes. It makes revenge almost impossible.

Out to avenge his wife's murder, but unable to retain the results of yesterday's investigation, he resorts to unusual practices to help leave himself clues which will keep him on the trail of the murderer. He takes snapshots with his trusty Polaroid camera and he leaves himself notes, the most important ones are tattooed on his own body.

All of this would make an interesting back story for a film noir murder mystery but writer/director Christopher Nolan (Following) adds a bizarre twist. Wanting to place us in the same untenable situation as Leonard, Nolan tells this story in reverse. Starting with the final scene, we jump cut to the scene which would have occurred moments before. This “leapfrog” effect continues until we reach the beginning of the film where all is made clear... at least to those paying close attention.

In other words, we see Leonard taking action but don't know what prompted that action. The only explanation to what is happening are the very snapshots and cryptic notes that he leaves himself. We have no more awareness of what transpired before than does Leonard. It is immediately frustrating and unnerving, requiring us to continually rethink what is happening and why. This unusual convention, while disconcerting, does demonstrate the constant disorientation that the main character might be feeling.

The production is greatly aided by the casting of Guy Pierce as Leonard. He does a magnificent job in capturing the various emotional ups and downs of a character resigned to living a life of fragmented and unconnected moments.

Carrie-Anne Moss and Joe Pantoliano, who last were seen together in The Matrix, appear here as Natalie and Teddy, the two people who are closest to Leonard. As more scenes are played out, contradictions begin to occur and we can't be sure if they are what they appear to be or if they are using Leonard's lapses of memory for some ulterior motive.

As a fill between scenes, we get an extended look at one of Leonard's remaining memories. It's an incident which occurred before the accident and has such meaning for Leonard that it is one of the tattooed reminders he has placed on his body.

In addition to the mystery aspect of the film, director Nolan has also built in layers of comedy. During a chase sequence, Leonard forgets whether he is doing the chasing or if he is the one being chased. A hotel clerk rents him a second room when Leonard forgets that he had already checked in. He readily admits it when caught because he knows Leonard won't remember the confession.

Memento does get us thinking about our ability to remember and how integral that ability is to the functioning of our lives. God, who designed our minds, built into them the capacity to retain data. Most importantly, and something which He reiterates throughout the Scriptures, is our ability to remember the Word. To remember the Word requires that we put that Word into practice.

For if any be a hearer of the word, and not a doer, he is like unto a man beholding his natural face in a glass: For he beholdeth himself, and goeth his way, and straightway forgetteth what manner of man he was. James 1:23-24 (KJV)

Short term memory loss is something that we all, at one time or another, have experienced. It is no wonder that manufacturers of post it notes and daytimers are doing such a brisk business. One way to move a memory from short term to something more permanent is to put into application that which we want to remember. Being a faithful doer of the Word will cause it to remain a part of our heart, soul, and mind.

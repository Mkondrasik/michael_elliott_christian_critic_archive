Author-verified transcription

Sitting down to write a holiday classic is not as easy as one may think. Theodore Geisel (AKA Dr. Seuss) managed it with his delightful tale of a Grinch whose heart was two sizes too small and the Whos down in Whoville, the short and the tall.

Director Ron Howard (EdTV), in filming a live action rendition of How The Grinch Stole Christmas, has greatly embellished the popular Dr. Seuss tale, focusing largely upon the Grinch's motivation for wanting to steal it from the Whos in the first place. Some of the filler material works but a lot of it doesn't. The part that doesn't may be what keeps this modernized version from becoming tomorrow's classic.

The good news is that much of the children's fantasy tale remains intact and is readily familiar to fans of the storybook and the Chuck Jones' animated version which continues to this day to run on TV. Geisel's inventive wording and clever rhymes are present and even humorously recognized as the actors will often lapse into rhyming their dialogue, matching the rhythmic style of the narrator (Anthony Hopkins, Mission:Impossible II).

As most are aware, the Whos are a fun and frolicking people who just love to celebrate Christmas. The Grinch (Jim Carrey, Me Myself and Irene) is a curmudgeonly coot who lives on the outskirts of Whoville in a cave on Mt. Crumpit. For him, Christmas is nothing but noise and bother.

With the help of his faithful but somewhat reluctant dog Max, Grinch sets forth to steal Christmas from the Whos by stealing their presents and decorations.

The film veers from the familiar by trying to show what has made the Grinch so grinchy. Scenes of the young Grinch being teased and humiliated by his Who schoolmates may add a bit of information to the Grinch's history but it does little to increase our enjoyment. Another disappointing alteration is the depiction of the Whos themselves. In this version, Whoville has so commercialized Christmas that we'd be excused if we agreed with the Grinch that canceling the holiday might be a good idea.

Admittedly, this production rides heavily upon the prosthetic shoulders of Jim Carrey who is quite effective, though unrecognizable, as the green hairy one. He conveys as much of an animated quality as Chuck Jones' ink-drawn character. But along with Jim Carrey comes the question of taste. This Grinch production contains some humorous segments which do border on crudeness. They are relatively mild but perhaps inappropriate for the youngest of children. The pity is that there was no reason for such elements to be included. The producers lost more than they gained with this PG rating.

Still, there is a high degree of playfulness to the production which is undeniably enjoyable. And judging by the peels of laughter from both adults and children in the screening audience, the humor for the most part works just fine.

Included in the cast are Taylor Momsen (The Prophet's Game), as little Cindy Lou Who, Jeffrey Tambor (Girl Interrupted) as Mayor Who, Christine Baranski (Bowfinger) as the Grinch's love interest, Martha Mae Who-vier, Bill Irwin (A Midsummer Night's Dream) as Cindy Lou's father, and a knockout performance by the dog who plays Max.

The moral of the story remains as Dr. Seuss intended it to be, that the holiday season should be about more than presents and decorations. That it is more important to be happy and thankful for what you have rather than for what you get. However, for some reason, this basic philosophy appears to be a bit disingenuous coming as it does from a multi-million dollar production which has spared little expense in the telling of what used to be a sweet and simple story.

Still, seeing Cindy Lou's innocent, childlike honesty melt the stony heart of the Grinch can remind us that no one is beyond salvation and oftentimes a gentle word or a kind gesture can help to turn the hardest of hearts. Even the heart of a Grinch.

A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger. Proverbs 15:1 (KJV)

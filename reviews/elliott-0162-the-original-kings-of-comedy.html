The Original Kings of Comedy

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 18, 2000
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Acts 17:26aMalachi 2:10Genesis 9:9

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Race; human commonality; unity; equality; shared humanity

Author-verified transcription

In 1997, a cultural phenomenon known as the "Kings of Comedy" tour was launched by the young entrepreneur, Walter Latham. His concept was to package three or four comedians and book them into large arenas instead of small comedy clubs. Starting with the black comedians Steve Harvey, Cedric the Entertainer, and Bernie Mac (later adding D.L. Hughley to the tour in 1998), the Kings of Comedy tour took the African American community by storm, becoming one of the highest grossing comedy tours in history.

This concert film, shot in Charlotte N.C. over the course of two days, features Steve Harvey as the emcee. He riffs with the audience a bit, providing his own humorous commentary on current events, then introduces each of his fellow comedians to the vastly appreciative crowd.

Much of the humor shared by all four comics is based upon the differences between the races. "What black band do you know that would have kept playing while the Titanic sank?" asks D.L. Hughley. He also explains why blacks don't look to extreme sports for excitement. "To a black man, just living through the day is excitement enough... Excitement is reaching for my wallet and not getting shot 41 times."

The humor of these comedians is also largely laden with profanities and includes some sexual references. These are raw comedy observations phrased in the language of the street.

Bernie Mac is perhaps the most profane of the four, as he includes in his set an "explanation" for the black community's frequent use of a particular epithet, (The one that rhymes with "brother trucker.") His explanation consists of repeating the phrase in rapid-fire succession much to the delight of his audience. His is also the most sexually explicit of the comedians in the film.

Cedric the Entertainer has the most likable set of the four although he is not above peppering his speech with expletives. His explanation of how blacks and whites react differently to confrontation is amusing, having the ring of truth to it.

Steve Harvey is perhaps the sweetest and most plainly himself of the four. Near the end he implores the audience to listen to the lyrics of his beloved old school music which was all about love, contrasting it against today's hip hop music which, when you can understand the words, seems to involve someone getting shot or killed.

Director Spike Lee is at his best when he becomes less obtrusive and simply allows the cameras to focus on the four talents appearing on stage. The backstage cutaways and the pans to the audience members which he adds do little to contribute to the concert feel of the film. The casual segments with the four comedians while offstage are uninspired and unfunny.

It occurred to me that the comedians spent so much time focusing on the differences between the races during their time on stage, that they failed to touch upon the commonality we have. The best comedians, the real kings of comedy, find a way to transcend race to reach the hearts, minds, and funny bones of a multi-colored, multi-gendered audience.

Even though popular with the predominantly African American audience who shared the theatre with me, the humor contained in the film did not bridge the cultural divide to attain a universal appeal.

It shouldn't be that hard. After all, we all swim in the same genetic pool.

And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, Acts 17:26a (KJV)

Instead of focusing on that which makes us different, why not find the humor in that which brings us together? It would appear that these self-pronounced "kings of comedy" have a limited kingdom. Only time will tell if their "reign" will have lasting power and will impact future generations.

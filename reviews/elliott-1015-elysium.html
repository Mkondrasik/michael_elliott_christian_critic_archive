Author-verified transcription

There is an obvious socio-political message at the heart of Elysium, the newest sci-fi action picture from Neill Blomkamp. In it the “haves” have taken their wealth and resources and fled the earth, leaving the now desolate planet to the “have-nots,” for all the good it will do them.

Max (Matt Damon), since he was a young boy, has dreamt of one day gaining access to the utopian space station world known as Elysium. After suffering a lethal dose of radiation at the plant where he works, he now has little choice but to make that dream happen. It seems the elite have a medical device that can in minutes heal every disease or illness plaguing mankind. The “haves” are selfishly keeping this technology to themselves. The “have-nots” are simply expected to die.

The citizens of Elysium are protected from the illegal immigration of undesirables by a cold and compassionless bureaucrat named Delacourt (Jodie Foster), who is not above using a brutal mercenary named Kruger (Sharlto Copley) to infiltrate and disrupt any plots the “have-nots” have of changing their lower class status.

Writer/director Neill Blomkamp, having built this dystopian world, fails to populate it with characters whose actions and decisions are as complex as the world in which they live. The plot holes are plentiful and the two-dimensional characterizations have a tendency to fall into them.

It is difficult not to draw a comparison between the storyline of Elysium and the ongoing political crusade to bring affordable healthcare to all U.S. citizens. What the film doesn’t address and what the political arguments often sidestep is the problem of solving the financial costs of providing this access to all.

Scriptures contain any number of examples of how we are to extend compassion and love to all men and to freely give to those who are in need. It is a fundamental tenet of Christianity. However, a parallel truth that is sometimes ignored is that all men have a God-given responsibility to contribute to this life. Working in order to attain is as much a part of our nature as is anything else.

“For while we were yet with you, we gave you this rule and charge: If anyone will not work, neither let him eat.” 2 Thessalonians 3:10 (Amplified)

The difficulty with an entitlement culture or welfare state is that it does not provide strength or hope to those for whom it is designed. While God wants us to look out for each other, He does not desire for any one of us to grow to be entirely dependent upon another man (or government program initiated by man) for our needs. His vision for our lives is so much bigger than that.

“To make it your ambition and definitely endeavor to live quietly and peacefully, to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands, as we charged you, So that you may bear yourselves becomingly and be correct and honorable and command the respect of the outside world, being dependent on nobody [self-supporting] and having need of nothing.” 1 Thessalonians 4:11-12 (Amplified)

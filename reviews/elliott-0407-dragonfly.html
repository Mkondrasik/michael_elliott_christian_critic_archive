Dragonfly

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 22, 2002
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 30 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Ecclesiastes 9:101 Corinthians 15:54-55Revelation 21:4

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Death; grief; resurrection; the dead

Author-verified transcription

Of all the grief that we will experience in a lifetime, there is none so great as the grief that comes with the death of a loved one. It is a pain that, if we live long enough and should our Lord tarry, we will all share. It is this kind of grief that is at the heart of the story of Dragonfly.

Dr. Joe Darrow (Kevin Costner, For Love of the Game) lost his wife Emily in a bus accident that took place in Venezuela where she was providing humanitarian aid. Even as he grieves her death, he begins picking up signals that she might be trying to contact him from beyond the grave.

He begins by seeing dragonflies, his late wife's personal "totem." That soon leads to unexplained experiences in the emergency room as "flatlined" patients are resuscitated, afterwards claiming to have brought back a message for Joe from his wife.

A longtime unbeliever in the concept of heaven, Joe must reexamine his own beliefs and puts his career at risk as he pursues answers from those who have had near death experiences.

Director Tom Shadyac (Patch Adams) doesn't quite seem to know what kind of film he's helming. Is it a romantic drama about dealing with loss or is it a spooky ghost story about receiving messages from the dead? He straddles the fence here and the result is a wasted opportunity. Dragonfly has elements which will be sure to involve the audience but it never fully delivers an impact that will make it a memorable film.

Kevin Costner gives an adequate performance, something along the lines of his work in Message in a Bottle, but he is ultimately done in by the lack of adroitness inherent in the script. His costars do not fare as well as he, merely giving slight characterizations to their stereotypical roles. Academy Award winner Kathy Bates (Misery) blandly plays Joe's next door neighbor and confidante. Academy Award winner Linda Hunt (The Year of Living Dangerously) is almost laughable as a nun who studies near death experiences. Along with Costner, who won his Oscar for Dances With Wolves, there's an abundance of noted talent on the screen which only proves that if you hire a thoroughbred to haul a milk wagon, you still end up with a milk wagon.

There is enough mystery in the story to give the film a "hook" wherein we, the audience, will want to find out what happens. But the journey to the final payoff is a tedious one and afterwards we recognize how manipulative the whole experience was.

For me, it didn't help matters for the film to base its premise so strongly on the hereafter. Throughout the movie Joe is "haunted" by his dead wife who is trying to get him to do something but lacks the communicative skills to express herself clearly. Her difficulty is certainly understandable. After all, she's dead. The scriptures tell us plainly that the dead cannot speak.

Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest. Ecclesiastes 9:10 (KJV)

God tells us throughout His Word that those who have died will be in a state of sleep from which they will not awaken until Christ returns to gather his church. If the Word is true, (and if it is not then all Christianity is moot), then any post death messages or appearances must be false.

Grief can be so strong as to make us want to believe that the dead are alive and existing on another, higher plane. But the simple truth is that they aren't. True comfort comes from the knowledge that those who are dead in Christ shall be the first to awaken when our Lord returns leading to a time when all tears will be dried and death will lose its sting.

Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? 1 Corinthians 15:54b-55 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

Can a May/December romance work? That is the question that lies at the heart of Prime, a comedy by writer/director Ben Younger (Boiler Room).

The film features Uma Thurman as a 37-year-old divorcee who surprises herself by accepting a date from a 23-year-old Jewish boy (Bryan Greenberg). She surprises herself further by actually falling in love with the personable lad. But the biggest surprise is yet to come. After telling her therapist (Meryl Streep) the intimate details about her very young lover, she learns that her beau is also her therapist's boy (i.e. son).

The script spends a lot of time developing the attraction between Rafi (Thurman) and David (Greenberg) and the difficulties they encounter as they struggle with their age and cultural (he's Jewish, she's not) differences. Is love enough to bridge the yawning chasm that divides them?

While that relationship is at the heart of the romantic side of the film, the comedy is chiefly provided through the client/patient relationship between Rafi and Lisa (Streep). We know long before they do who David is and to watch Streep's reactions as the truth (and all its implications) begins to dawn on her is alone worth the price of admission.

Younger is blessed to have these three actors to anchor his film because the script is not without flaws. Most of the subplots and supporting characters don't really work. David has a "sociopathic" friend with dating issues who adds little; Rafi has a cabal of gay friends who advise her even while ogling her under-aged boyfriend in a scene that feels tacked on; Lisa has her own sessions with a therapist that are undeveloped.

There's an unpolished quality to the movie that keeps the film from totally drawing us in but the appeal of Thurman and Greenberg added to the inimitable skill of Streep keeps us engaged.

Lisa who, at one point, tries to reason with her son's decision to keep seeing Rafi makes the comment that love isn't enough to keep a relationship together - it takes work. I agree with her in principle but I view love as being the necessary motivation that makes us want to do the work.

I'm not talking about the physical love or the emotional love that people so often confuse for the love that God refers to in the Bible. The love that should motivate us and keep us involved in a relationship is deliberate, selfless, and, above all, giving.

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. John 3:16 (KJV)

To love means to give. And, as God shows us by His example, sometimes it means to give up or sacrifice in order to give that which is needed to those we love.

Can love bridge any divide? Yes, it can. Unfortunately, that isn't the only question that Rafi and David need to be asking. Love also requires them to ask whether or not it should.

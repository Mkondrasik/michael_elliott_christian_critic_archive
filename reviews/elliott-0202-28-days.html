28 Days

Author-verified transcription

Can a woman change her life in a mere 28 days? It is a question that Gwen Cummings (Sandra Bullock, Forces of Nature) is going to have answered in the newest Columbia Pictures film with the appropriate title 28 Days.

Gwen, a big city newspaper columnist with a big time addiction to alcohol and drugs, arrives late to her sister's wedding still inebriated from the night before. She falls into the wedding cake, borrows the bridal limo, and abruptly parks it in a living room, running over a lawn jockey in the process. Such actions bring her to the attention of the judicial system where the judge gives her a choice of 28 days of drug/alcohol rehab or prison time.

After seeing the Serenity Glen rehab clinic in action, she immediately regrets her decision. The people chant and sing "Lean on Me" at the slightest provocation.

But her stay proves to be a sobering experience for Gwen as she begins to recognize that there is a life that can be lived outside of a pill box or bottle. All she needs is to figure out how to attain it. It is a difficult thing to do for an independent city girl who has never learned how to ask for help.

And help is there in abundance. From Cornell (Steve Buscemi, Armageddon), an ex-addict turned counselor, to Gerhardt (Alan Tudyk, Patch Adams), an addict and homosexual German stripper whose over-the-top persona, mannerisms, and expressions provide much of the humor in this comedy/drama.

Working from an overly simplistic script by Susannah Grant, who also penned the far superior Erin Brockovich, director Betty Thomas (Doctor Dolittle) manages to elicit winning performances from her accomplished cast. Credibility is stretched a bit thin over the course of the movie, but the actors involved still present likable characters with whom we may associate and empathize.

Sandra Bullock, as the quintessential party girl, for whom the party has finally come to an end, shines. Yes, she uses her girl-next-door appearance to draw us in and yes, we find ourselves liking her in spite of her behavior. Perhaps that is why we feel so good when Gwen starts finding the way to like herself as well, by changing her actions to match the image she would like to have of herself.

Viggo Mortensen (A Walk on the Moon) and Dominic West (A Midsummer Night's Dream) play her romantic rivals and present two sides of the coin. Mr. West plays her current boyfriend whose wild and unconventional lifestyle serves as a continual temptation to Gwen and her old habits. Mr. Mortensen is a big-league pitcher, a fellow patient and the potential of a future boyfriend who is working through his own set of problems.

Each of the patients of Serenity Glen do have problems. Gwen's stem from the mantra taught to her from her alcoholic mother: "If you're not having fun, what's the friggin' point?" While there is nothing wrong with having fun, thinking that one may find it at the bottom of a bottle is misguided.

It is Mr. Mortensen's character who teaches her the key lesson which helps her recover. When teaching Gwen how to pitch a baseball, he told her not to think about the target because it will eventually begin to appear unattainable. Instead, if she concentrates on the little things, the things she can control (holding the ball, turning the shoulder, following through), she will receive the end result she desires.

It is the little things that count. The hundreds, even thousands, of daily decisions that we make is what adds up to a life that will serve God or not.

We see that one does not change one's life in 28 days (or 28 months for that matter). One changes one's life with every day that is lived... By every decision one makes. God has given us the knowledge to make sound decisions that will bless and benefit us and those around us. It is our continued faithfulness to make those godly decisions that builds the strength and adds the stability to our lives.

Author-verified transcription

Isn't She Great turns out to be a fitting tribute for the self-styled trash queen who gave the world Valley of the Dolls. It is a trashy, inconsequential movie which is all talk and precious little substance. To answer the implied question of the title in one word... No.

In the film, Jacqueline Susann is portrayed as a woman whose sole passion and motivation for life is to be famous. Not just well-known; not simply recognizable; but world famous. There is only one thing standing in her way... a complete lack of talent. After trying her hand at acting, radio broadcasts, even personal appearances (as a supermarket product demonstrator), she stumbles across what would be her claim to fame. That is, of course, to write a profanity filled novel about the seedy side of Hollywood including as much sex, drugs, and titillating behavior that she can muster on the page.

Supported by the puppy-dog fawning of her infatuated press agent/husband, Irving Mansfield, Ms. Susann is catapulted to super stardom through sheer chutzpah and by pandering to the lascivious tastes of a morally decadent society.

What the world didn't know was that all the time she was pursuing her dream of notoriety, she was also coping with various personal tragedies taking place in her life. Her only son is born autistic. She develops cancer. Her husband handles her growing fame by feeling unimportant and useless. But by this time, her foolish quest for fame is so unimportant to us, that we have little compassion for her or interest to see if she will hurdle the obstacles placed in her way.

Bette Midler (That Old Feeling) overplays the crudely flamboyant Jacqueline Susann by relying mainly upon histrionics and costume changes for her character nuances. Nathan Lane (At First Sight) plays the doting husband and business partner with his customary dry wit but because there is little of note to the character, there is little to remember about his performance. The same can be said for a cameo appearance by John Cleese (The Out of Towners) whose portrayal is marked only by the garish 1960's outfits he was made to wear.

Of the cast, only Stockard Channing (Practical Magic) as Jacqueline's best friend and David Hyde Pierce (Frasier) as her reluctant editor manage to rise above the weakness of the material.

And sad to say, the material is pretty darn weak. Written by Paul Rudnick (In & Out), the script contains ill-conceived scenes, implausible dialogue, and incredibly, a rubber chicken (surely a sign of a screenwriter who has run out of ideas. The schmaltzy musical score by Burt Bacharach reminds us of the worst the era had to offer. Director Andrew Bergman (Striptease) valiantly attempts to blend humor and pathos but outside of a few well placed one-liners which will evoke some laughter, fails to deliver either.

The filmmakers do include Ms. Susanne's firmly held belief in God by depicting her throughout the film holding personal conversations with Him. The depiction, however, is constructed to make her look a bit foolish (the only place she apparently feels able to speak with God is in a public park in front of a certain tree).

Ms. Susanne is also depicted as blaming God for her son's autism, for her cancer, for her lack of fame. This is a major error but one which many people make. We must remember that God is all good, the giver of every perfect gift.

Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning. James 1:17 [KJV]

God cannot contradict His own nature. He does not send illness, cause people to die, or bring unhappiness to those who profess to love Him.

Job, throughout all his trials and tribulations, never once blamed God for his tragedies or maladies. The source of his troubles is clearly given in the scriptures. It was Job's faithfulness not to remove his integrity and belief in God that drove off his spiritual attacker and allowed God to restore all that he had lost.

Author-verified transcription

Kicking and Screaming isn’t much more than another Bad News Bears’ derivative with one major distinction. It gives Will Ferrell plenty of room to make us laugh in his newest “everyman” role.

Ferrell plays Phil Weston, an average suburban father with below average sports skills. That must have been a disappointment to his own father, Buck (Robert Duvall), who is a highly competitive sports store mogul. How competitive is he? In order to keep his Pee Wee soccer team in first place, he decides to trade Phil’s son, Sam (Dylan McLaughlin), to the basement dwelling Tigers.

In order to support his son, the hapless Phil agrees to coach the hapless Tigers and quickly discovers that he’s completely wrong for the job. He turns to Buck’s neighbor and arch enemy: Chicago Bears’ former coach Mike Ditka (who plays himself). Ditka’s no-nonsense style, helped by his recruiting skills, result in the Tigers’ rise through the ranks where they begin to challenge Buck’s first place Gladiators.

The success of the team changes the perennial loser Phil. Proving that the apple never falls far from the tree, he turns into a coffee-guzzling, win at all costs, maniac who enjoys inflicting humiliation upon his opponents – even though they’re ten years old.

Except for the inclusion of a pair of gratuitous lesbian soccer moms and a handful of mild innuendoes, Kicking and Screaming keeps itself within the realms of a family friendly comedy, relying Ferrell’s appeal and clowning abilities to provide most of the humor. His behavior is, of course, unbelievably moronic but he manages to sell it to us through an unrelenting though goofy earnestness.

Unfortunately, the filmmakers haven’t really given him any three dimensional characters against which to play. Most of the kids on his team don’t have enough of a separate personality to really be called a character. Instead, each has been given a single identifiable characteristic by which we are to remember them: kid who eats worms; diminutive Asian kid; smart-aleck kid; etc…

Only Robert Duvall as Phil’s hard driving father has anything that might be called character depth although he too is restricted to wading in the shallow end of the cliché pool.

Director Jesse Dylan (American Wedding) does the best thing possible – he turns the cameras on Ferrell and tries to stay out of the way. The result is a barely passable and sometimes amusing family comedy.

Phil’s coaching tips range from the ill-informed to the outrageously irresponsible. And yet, as his team discovers, by the end of the film he has passed on valuable knowledge – the knowledge of what not to do.

Life is filled with examples from which we can learn. Some examples are worthy of emulation. Others are not. Sometimes a bad example can be just as important as one which we might want to follow. Scriptures are filled with records of people who made poor decisions. God included them in His Word to teach us what to avoid.

But with many of them God was not well pleased: for they were overthrown in the wilderness. Now these things were our examples, to the intent we should not lust after evil things, as they also lusted. 1 Corinthians 10:5-6 (KJV)

Phil’s idea of sportsmanship is so appalling that it serves as a warning to those who become so competitive that they lose perspective and forget that how you play the game is more important than winning it.

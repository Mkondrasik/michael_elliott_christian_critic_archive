Almost Heroes

1 hr: 30 min

Author-verified transcription

Almost Heroes is almost good. But as we've known since childhood, unless we're playing horseshoes, "almost" doesn't count.

This silly little movie starts out with a promising premise: Lewis and Clark's expedition to the Pacific Coast of North America had a rival team. Foppish Leslie Edwards (Matthew Perry, Friends) hired bumbling tracker Bartholomew Hunt (Chris Farley, Tommy Boy) and a sorry group of would-be explorers. Their mission: To beat their more famous counterparts to the Pacific Ocean.

The film quickly moves from promising to disappointing to ultimately ridiculous. The late Mr. Farley does most of his trademark shtick to no avail. His broad antics seem tired and embarrassing.

Mr. Perry tries to bring to his character the same sarcastic delivery that works so well for him on his TV show. Here, it is merely flat and one dimensional.

Overall, that just about sums up the movie: Flat and one dimensional. The characters are supposed to be in a race to the Pacific, yet there is no sense of urgency. Mr. Perry's character is supposed to be enamored with a young Indian maiden (played by Lisa Barbuscia, Serpent's Lair), yet there is no sense of passion or romance. The movie is supposed to be set in an uncharted land, yet there is no sense of discovery, fear, or wonder. Humor, even of a broad, farcical nature to which Almost Heroes aspires, must be rooted in something real for it to be effective.

Part of the problem is that director Christopher Guest (Waiting For Guffman) can't seem to breathe enough life into the singularly unfunny script attributed to Mark Nutter, Tom Wolfe, and Boyd Hale. To their credit, the actors are clearly working hard but they simply weren't given much with which to work.

To a certain extent, we are all on an expedition of sorts - a journey through the wilderness of this world. As we struggle to stay on the proper path that leads to our desired destination, we face obstacles, temptations and attacks that attempt to get us off course. They may not be as idiotic or absurd as the straw women or the Spanish conquistadors that await our insipid movie "heroes," but they are devilishly designed to stop us from reaching the goals we have set for ourselves.

Of course, this journey of ours is a spiritual one. The wilderness through which we blaze our trail is the senses realm in which we live. Our opponent is the devil who, instead of racing against us, is the one setting up the roadblocks, the obstacles and the temptations which entice us to quit.

To quit would categorize us as being an "almost hero." To quit the race amounts to our forfeiture to the devil. Why let him win when God guarantees our victory if we but keep running? A real hero is one who valiantly finishes the course, leaping over the obstacles and running through the blockades set by our spiritual opponent. And at the end of this race, a real hero is rewarded with a sight that is considerably more wondrous than the Pacific Ocean.

I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith: Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give me at that day: and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing. 2 Timothy 4:7-8 (KJV)

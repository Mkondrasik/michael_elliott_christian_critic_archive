The Basket

May 5, 2000

1 hr: 45 min

Author-verified transcription

In a summer filled with big budget films containing explosions, ribald humor, and nonstop action, there is a small sleepy little movie making its way to theaters across the US. The Basket is described by independent filmmaker Rich Cowan as a labor of love, the result of a desire to "make a life-affirming film that an entire family could enjoy together."

Set in a small farming community in the Pacific Northwest during 1918, a time when our country was enmeshed in the horrors of World War I, this small budget film manages to tackle some mighty large issues.

Peter Coyote (Random Hearts) stars as Martin Conlon, the new Bostonian schoolteacher with a shady past who introduces his students to two newfound passions in his life: Opera and basketball. The former, he uses to connect with his students by playing the opera a little at a time, narrating the story as it goes. Soon the entire town is enthralled by the story, discussing its many aspects and plot twists as if it were a modern day soap opera. The latter, which he learned at the feet of the game's inventor, James Naismith, is used to bring the community together at a time when one's nationality could breed hatred and contempt.

And there's plenty of that to go around. The war in Europe has touched all of America, including the tiny little town of Waterville, Washington. Many families have sons who will never return. Some, such as the Emery family, have to deal with the fact that their son, who has returned, has been irreparably damaged at the hands of the German soldiers.

But to every war there is more than one side. Also arriving in Waterville are two German orphans, Helmut and Brigitta Brink (played by newcomers Robert Karl Burke and Amber Willenborg), whose parents were killed by Americans while trying to flee the war zone they once knew as their home. Their assimilation into this unfamiliar and unfriendly (to them) American community will not be easy. Young Helmut, who develops his own passionate appreciation for the sport his teacher introduces, has two strikes against him. He's short and he's German. Mr. Conlon tells him that his day will come and that he must be ready when it does. So Helmut practices, using a laundry basket and rolled up clothes... patiently waiting for his day to come.

These strangers (the schoolteacher and the orphans) manage to teach the closely knit community the value of forgiveness and acceptance and togetherness.

Karen Allen (Starman) also stars as Bessie Emery who has long silently accepted the decisions of her opinionated husband, Nicholas (Jock MacDonald, Indemnity), even when she knew those decisions to be wrong. And yet, no villains have been drawn in the script - the character depth is appreciable and telling. Every character, including Nicholas, has valid reasons for acting and thinking the way he does.

The film does move slowly, especially when compared to the summer blockbusters currently in the theaters. But the film depicts a different time in our American history. A time when neighbors cared about each other. When communities banded together for a common goal. A time when people took life slower in order to more fully appreciate the time they had.

Spiritually speaking, scriptural examples abound in the film. The one that especially caught my eye was the way Mr. Conlon taught his basketball team to play defense. Reminding them of an example from the opera, "to defend the mighty wall... each person must fight for something small," he uses that phrase to teach them the principles of zone defense.

To protect the integrity of the body of Christ, of which we are all a part, requires each member in particular to protect the Word of God which resides in his or her heart.

For as we have many members in one body, and all members have not the same office. Romans 12:4 (KJV)

If each of us stand strong upon the Word we know, we, as a body, will grow in strength exponentially.

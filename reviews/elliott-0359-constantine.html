Constantine

Author-verified transcription

Whoa. The eternal struggle between good and evil gets a comic book treatment in Constantine, based upon the characters in DC/Vertigo's Hellblazer. A cross between The Matrix and Hellboy, the film is steeped in religious iconography but is so devoid of spiritual truth that for people of faith it may border on being offensively sacrilegious.

Keanu Reeves stars as John Constantine, a bitterly sarcastic, chain-smoking suicide victim, who, after visiting hell for a few moments ("a lifetime," he says), decides to devote the rest of his resurrected life to combating the demons in this world who are upsetting the "balance" originally set by God and Lucifer.

We are told that demons and angels are allowed to influence man but the line is drawn at direct contact. Demons that choose to cross the line will have to answer to John Constantine who somehow has the power to deport them back to hell. He's hoping that if he sends enough of them back, he'll earn a place in heaven and avoid the eternal fiery torment that would appear to be his destiny.

He's joined by his frustrated sidekick Chas (Shia LaBoeuf), who for the most part is relegated to driving a cab so he can shuttle Constantine from place to place, and Angela Dodson (Rachel Weisz) who is looking for some answers after her twin sister unexpectedly commits suicide.

Keanu performs his traditionally one-note characterization effectively even if it appears to be somewhat uninspired. Additional characters include the "half-breed" angel Gabriel (Tilda Swinton), a laughably mannered Satan (Peter Stormare), an alcoholic priest (Pruitt Taylor Vince), and Midnite (Djimon Hounsou), neutral owner of a nightclub that caters to demons and demon slayers alike.

The special effects are impressive even if many of them are ill-conceived or ridiculous. Still, there's no excusing the overly complex and hard to follow narrative line.

Constantine is informed by its comic book origins, which apparently wasn't too concerned with biblical accuracy or spiritual integrity. The doctrinal errors run free and deep. So much so that it is hard to know where to start in addressing them.

For instance, an accurate reading of scripture doesn't support the film's claim that God and Satan have a bet with the souls of man as the prized ante. Gabriel is not a half-breed or fallen angel. The demons do not have a Bible of their own that is published in hell. The very idea of hell as a place of eternal torment comes more from religious art and secular literature than from any information that God provides in His Word.

The most glaring discrepancy is the film's assertion that one must buy his or her way into heaven through good deeds or even self- sacrifice. Not only would God not demand it - He wouldn't accept it. The entrance fee to heaven has already been paid for all of man. We need not "earn" passage.

For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it's the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast. Ephesians 2:8-9 (KJV)

Not only is Constantine a flawed film artistically, it has little to add to man's ongoing consideration of spiritual issues.

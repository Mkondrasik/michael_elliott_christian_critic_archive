Author-verified transcription

There is something pathetically sad about a wasted life. Auto Focus tells the story of Bob Crane (Greg Kinnear, We Were Soldiers), family man, ex-radio DJ, and star of the TV series Hogan's Heroes.

In the 1960s, Hogan's Heroes became an unexpected CBS hit. Who figured that a comedy based in a Nazi POW camp would work? But work it did and Bob Crane's celebrity star was on the rise. Even as his popularity soared, it was his obsession with sex which led him on a long downward spiral toward professional ruin and self-destruction.

The film is narrated by Kinnear as Crane and as he tells us in his own words, he's "a likable guy." His casual affability set him atop of an industry where personality is king and, for a time, it would appear that life was rosy at the Crane roost.

But the signs were there. His wife (Rita Wilson, Sleepless in Seattle) discovers a stash of girlie magazines which he tries to explain away as nothing more than a healthy interest in photography. Later, he begins spending time in strip clubs. His excuse for that is even more of a stretch. A passionate drum player, Crane "unwinds" from a day's filming by sitting in with the club's house band, beating out the rhythms for the stripping girls.

It is his relationship with John Carpenter (Willem Dafoe, Spider-man) that finally fuels his obsession to the point that it takes over his life. Carpenter, a representative of Sony Electronics with access to all the cutting edge audio and video equipment, is a Hollywood leech and genuinely creepy guy. After hanging around the periphery of the entertainment world, he finally attaches himself to Crane to ride his celebrity status into a seemingly endless world of sexual pleasures and perversities.

Based upon Robert Graysmith's book, "The Murder of Bob Crane," Auto Focus unflinchingly shows and tells the depravity of the sexual obsession that brought Bob Crane such disappointment and misery. Kinnear plays Crane with a genial shallowness that leaves us wanting. Perhaps there isn't much more to the man to know but quite frankly, what is there isn't all that interesting.

Dafoe's portrayal of John Carpenter is meatier and more layered than Kinnear's Crane but neither character is sympathetic nor appealing. Unfortunately, director Paul Schrader (Affliction) and first time screenwriter Michael Gerbosi don't explore any of the other characters in depth. What we therefore have is a film involving characters about whom we don't care performing acts which we don't care to see. Not much there to recommend, is it?

The first definition of "obsession" listed in Webster's New World College Dictionary is "the act of an evil spirit in possessing or ruling a person." It goes on to clarify: "a persistent idea, desire, emotion, etc., especially one that cannot be gotten rid of by reasoning."

Regardless of one's belief or understanding about evil spirits or the devil spirit kingdom, obsessive behavior should be readily recognizable as unhealthy and eventually destructive. Sex in and of itself is neither unhealthy nor destructive. Handling the subject of sex in the context of the marriage relationship, here's what Paul said by revelation to the believers at Corinth:

Do not refuse and deprive and defraud each other [of your due marital rights], except perhaps by mutual consent for a time, so that you may devote yourselves unhindered to prayer. But afterwards resume marital relations, lest Satan tempt you [to sin] through your lack of restraint of sexual desire. 1 Corinthians 7:5 (Amplified)

The problem is therefore one of elevating sex to an undue level of importance. The mantra of Crane and Carpenter was "A day without sex is a wasted day." The lesson that is, sad to say, never learned by Crane but certainly illustrated by his story can be summed up by this alteration of his mantra: "A life based upon sex is a life that is wasted." It is a life lesson taught at the cost of Crane's own.

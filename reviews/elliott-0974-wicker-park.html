Wicker Park

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
September 3, 2004
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 55 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

1 Corinthians 13:4-6Proverbs 10:12James 4:1

Sermon / spiritual topic: Love; Obsession

Author-verified transcription

In Wicker Park, after having his heart broken by a woman who suddenly left without explanation, Matthew (Josh Hartnett) moved from Chicago to New York where he found a new career and a new woman. Now, two years later, he returns to the Windy City with his girlfriend on his arm. He suddenly has his emotional life again uprooted when he thinks he catches a glimpse of his old flame.

His curiosity getting the better of him, he lies to his girl, skips a career-advancing business meeting and becomes Gollum-like obsessive in tracking down his Lisa (Diane Kruger). Soon Matthew is breaking into hotel rooms, stealing personal items, and staking out places where he thinks she might be. He tells himself all this is just to confront her and find out why she left him but there’s a desperation to his actions that lets us know there’s a lot more going on than meets the eye.

Things get even more complicated when he finally tracks Lisa to her home where he discovers that the Lisa he’s been following is an entirely different girl (Rose Byrne) who just happens to wear the same shoes and coat and uses the same antique compact. Still he can’t shake the feeling that the girl he was in love with two years ago is nearby.

Sometimes, a film’s worst enemies can be those assigned to promote it. The trailers for Wicker Park make it out to be a suspenseful crime/thriller story of psychotic identity theft a la Single White Female. That’s hardly the case. This is a sometimes sweet, sometimes bitter tale of love and obsession.

Director Paul McGuigan (The Reckoning) weaves a number of two simple yet intriguingly interconnected stories together by showing us, at various times, the same scenes played from different characters’ perspectives.

The film is a tad longer than the material supports and thus tends to sag a bit in the middle acts but with some snazzy editing near the end, McGuigan reconnects with us as he brings the movie to its traditional Hollywood-esque ending.

Josh Hartnett apparently still has some box office appeal if the line of ladies outside the theater is any indication. He’s a bit bland in the role although he does play an awkward goofiness that is disarmingly effective. Matthew Lillard, as Matthew’s best friend Luke, is the surprise here. Known for usually obnoxiously overplaying his characters for comedy’s sake, Lillard downshifts and makes his role of Luke human, real, and touchingly funny.

The girls of Wicker Park are attractive but because of the film’s secrets, are not able to show a great deal of character depth lest they give away the ending. Diane Kruger is alluring and believable as the object of Hartnett’s obsession. Rose Byrne adds a bit of tension with her unpredictable and obviously insecure character. Jessica Pare has little to do as Hartnett’s quickly forgotten girlfriend.

The film is more about obsession than it is love. Furthermore, it is about how the two can get easily confused. As one of the characters explains, “Love makes you do crazy things - things you would never imagine yourself doing - but you can’t help it.”

What the character describes isn’t love. Whatever it is, it is debilitating, personally destructive and morally reprehensible. Love shouldn’t make anyone do “crazy” things. Love is the sanest and most selfless motivation that exists in this world.

“Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It is not rude, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no record of wrongs. Love does not delight in evil but rejoices with the truth.” 1 Corinthians 13:4-6 (NIV)

If more of the film’s characters acted out of love, this would have been quite a different movie.

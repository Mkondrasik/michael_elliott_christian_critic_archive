Author-verified transcription

The lead character of Phone Booth, Stu Shepard (Colin Farrell, Daredevil), doesn't have many good qualities. He's a fast talking manipulator who is playing so many angles that it's hard to know which direction he's headed. It isn't hard, however, to recognize that he's headed for a fall.

We first meet Stu, strutting along the streets of New York, doing business on the run via cell phone. As a publicist for wannabe stars, he knows that image is everything. Clad in designer clothes and sporting a faux designer watch, he spends his day telling people what they want to hear so they'll do what he wants them to do. He doesn't have much use for the truth which he bends, twists, and breaks at every opportunity.

That changes when he picks up a ringing pay phone in what may be the sole remaining closet-style phone booth in the continental United States. On the other end of the line is a man (voice of Kiefer Sutherland, 24) who seems to know about all the dirty little secrets and lies that Stu's been hiding from everyone. He also has a rifle trained on Stu to help motivate him to confess his sins in front of all those he's hurt or lied to.

It's a long list. It includes his wife (Radha Mitchell, Dead Heat), and a struggling actress (Katie Holmes, Abandon) with whom he's been flirting, but most importantly Stu is being forced to come clean with someone he's been lying to most of his adult life... himself.

The majority of the film's 80 minute running time is spent with Stu in the phone booth and this spatial limitation has actually helped the critically beleaguered director/producer Joel Schumacher (Batman & Robin), to maintain a dramatic tautness throughout the production.

Colin Farrell temporarily loses his Irish brogue to give a credible performance as a seriously flawed man who must go from arrogant to incredulous to humbled while keeping the audience's sympathy throughout.

Forest Whitaker (Panic Room) lends a commanding presence as the senior officer on the scene and Kiefer Sutherland's disembodied voice is appropriately menacing. Neither female lead has much of an opportunity to develop their character. The most memorable female performance on the screen might very well belong to Paula Jai Parker (High Crimes) who plays a sassy hooker resentful of the fact that her phone booth has been commandeered.

Phone Booth is an R-rated morality tale and as such has a message which can be easily traced back to the scriptures. Stu is being forced to recognize a spiritual reality that will eventually face us all.

For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things done in his body, according to that he hath done, whether it be good or bad. 2 Corinthians 5:10 (KJV)

Obviously, repentance is something that cannot be coerced. A change of heart always must come from within. God is not be pointing a gun to our head to get us to change our ways. Still, we should know that a day is coming when we will be held accountable for all that we say and do. Those things that we do in secret will one day be made known because our lives are an open book to God. So why not make it a book that is enjoyable to read?

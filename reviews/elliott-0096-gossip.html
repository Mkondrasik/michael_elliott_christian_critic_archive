Gossip

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 21, 2000
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Proverbs 26:22Matthew 12:36James 3:5-6

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Gossip; words; accountability; edifying speech

Author-verified transcription

GOSSIP is like a murderous version of the old campfire “telephone” game where you whisper a phrase into the ear of somebody and watch as the message is passed from person to person until it comes back to you in some kind of humorously mutated form.

University students Derrick (James Marsden, Disturbing Behavior), Jones (Lena Headey, Mrs. Dalloway), and Travis (Norman Reedus, 8MM) devise a new class project for their journalism professor. To illustrate the properties of gossip, they decide to plant a false rumor and track how far it spreads as well as catalogue the various contortions it makes along the way.

As a subject, they choose the wealthy and aloof Naomi (Kate Hudson, 200 Cigarettes), a girl well known for her chastity. Seeing her drunk at a party, they decide to plant the rumor that she and her boyfriend Beau (Joshua Jackson, The Skulls) had sex in an upstairs room. Naturally, the rumor spreads around the campus like wildfire, spinning off into ridiculous and humorous variations. But as the gossip turns ugly, things take a more serious note.

Beau is arrested for rape, Naomi is driven to pills and alcohol, and the three responsible for starting the rumor are at odds as to how to handle the consequences their actions have brought.

However, not all is as innocent as it first appears. There are characters with hidden motivations and questionable ethics. As the film progresses, it moves from being an interesting treatise on the dangers of gossip to an unbelievable tale of revenge and deception.

The acting, as a whole, was quite acceptable. The trio of journalism students played off of one another with a great deal of skill and apparent enjoyment. They each had specific and unique traits but their characters blended so well together that it was completely believable that these three would end up friends and roommates.

The director’s hand was quite evident in the manner in which the mystery/thriller was filmed. Using a number of cinematic gimmicks reminiscent of MTV or certain TV dramas, director Davis Guggenheim, who is best known for his work on television (NYPD Blue, ER), was not as effective in bringing his bag of tricks to the large screen.

Where the film was at its most engaging was when it was tackling the thorny issue of news vs gossip and the fine line that is all too often crossed by today’s media. Where it loses its sharpness is when it tries to move into the realm of a murder mystery. The logic of the mystery simply does not hold up under an introspective analysis. It may provide a kind of stylized fun, but it doesn’t make much sense.

Gossip, as a word, gives great insight as to the mercurial nature of rumors. Originally the word gossip was formed by compounding two words (god and sibb) and was used to describe the relationship of a godparent. The meaning of the word evolved to eventually connote a close friendship or bond. Within two centuries, the derogatory meaning of a spreader of idle talk had become the prominent usage.

The biggest difference between news and gossip is one of motivation. What is the reason or motivation for holding forth the information?

The words of a talebearer are as wounds, and they go down into the innermost parts of the belly. Proverbs 26:22 (KJV)

Scriptures attest to the truth that our words always have an impact upon those to whom they are delivered. It is why we are exhorted to speak things that are edifying and will serve to bless the hearer. We are also told that we will be held accountable for every idle word that we utter. Reason enough to pause and think before we speak.

Gossip is a titillating endeavor that serves no good purpose. It does not strengthen us or make us a better people. It is at best a distraction and at worse a destructive evil.

Author-verified transcription

Throughout I Dreamed of Africa we are reminded that Kenya moves at a different rhythm. If the movie is to be any indication, that rhythm could be likened to the pace of a funeral dirge.

Based upon the true story of Kuki Gallmann, I Dreamed of Africa is as much a tale of tragedy as it is one of adventure. Kuki, desiring an opportunity to start anew, embraces the idea proposed by her new boyfriend to leave the comfortable yet unchallenging life of affluent Italian society for the unclean, unsafe, uncertain life of the African wild.

With her young son, Emanuele, and now husband Paolo, Kuki arrives in Africa and quickly learns that this continent is both unforgiving and unmerciful. Life or death scenarios occur daily with little possibility of a do-over in case of a mistake or lapse of judgment. And we see plenty of examples of this. Poachers, poisonous snakes, lions, crocodiles and other creatures that can bring death - are familiar sights.

Yet even with the probability of death lurking behind every tree limb or under every rock, director Hugh Hudson (My Life So Far) manages to suck any and all tension out of most every frame of film. The only thing left in its place is some breathtaking scenery with a few actors possibly standing in the foreground.

Kim Basinger, in her first return to the screen since her Academy Award winning performance in L.A. Confidential, is handed the lead role of Kuki, and promptly turns the feisty Italian into a bland American with nary a flicker from her limpid eyes or flip of her tousled blonde hair. Ms. Basinger is given plenty of opportunity to emote as those around her succumb to the dangers of Africa, but at quieter moments, her characterization flattens and the nuances, which might have provided a spark of interest, disappear.

Vincent Perez (Indochine) fares a bit better as Kuki's second husband Paolo. Drawn to Africa like a moth to a flame (and suffering the same end), Mr. Perez's Paolo is a man who wants to experience a personal relationship with this untamed environment while at the same time have a loving and peaceful home with his wife and child. The desires are not always reconciled.

Eva Marie Saint (On the Waterfront) makes a welcome screen appearance as Kuki's mother who is not altogether pleased with her daughter's choices. Liam Aiken (Stepmom) plays Kuki's young son Emanuele with newcomer Garrett Strommen playing the teen aged version of the same role.

The major flaw of the film is a directorial one. Besides having a slow and plodding pace, many scenes are needlessly included. Needless because they offered no real insight to the characters and did not help to move the story forward to its anxiously awaited end. The desire was obviously to paint Mrs. Gallmann's story as an inspirational adventure but there is so little connection with the characters that it ends up being little more than a travelogue gone bad.

"Africa let us live an extraordinary life and then it claimed an extraordinary price," Kuki reasons. Some would insist that it was too high a price to justify the risks taken.

The film does point out the need to walk circumspectly or precisely when in the midst of dangerous territory. In a world where any misstep may bring death, one chooses one's path with great care and vigilance. It is an analogy which all Christians should take to heart.

Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour: 1 Peter 5:8 (KJV)

God's Word tells us that our adversary has made this world a wilderness. One need not travel to distant lands fraught with physical peril to test one's survival skills. Simply by living in this world one is automatically subject to the spiritual dangers of unseen enemies. But we are not left defenseless or without a guide. By adhering to the knowledge and wisdom of God, we can learn to overcome and prevail against them. Here then is the true inspiration.

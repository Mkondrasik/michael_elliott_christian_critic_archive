Hamlet

Author-verified transcription

The melancholy Dane becomes an angst-ridden New Yorker in this modern adaptation of the Bard’s classic tragedy, HAMLET. Set in the year 2000, yet retaining much of the Elizabethan language of the original script (severely condensed to a running time of just under 2 hours), this interpretation manages to squander an interesting premise and a talented cast.

Instead of King of Denmark, Hamlet’s father was the CEO of the Denmark Corporation. That is, up until his death just two months ago. Hamlet’s mother, Gertrude has remarried her late husband’s brother, Claudius, who now helms the business. The speed of the marriage disturbs Hamlet but not nearly so much as the idea that his uncle/stepfather may have had something to do with his father’s death.

As with the original script, the crux of the story is whether or not Hamlet will or should take action, a question served up by the “To be or not to be” soliloquy which, in the modern version, is delivered in part in a Blockbuster video store (the action aisle no less.)

Shakespeare’s other characters are also on hand: Polonius, the CEO’s business advisor; his two children, Laertes and Ophelia the latter of whom is in an ill-advised love relationship with the title character; Rosencrantz and Guildenstern (who are not dead), a pair of Hamlet’s fellow students; and of course, the ghost of Hamlet’s father.

According to producer Amy Hobby who was on hand at the screening I attended, one of the attractions to mounting this production was in casting a Hamlet closer in age to the character than is generally seen. However, young Ethan Hawke (Snow Falling on Cedars) doesn’t have the acting chops to pull off one of Shakespeare’s most difficult and complex roles. Many of his lines are delivered with a flat and leaden quality to them which quickly erodes into a monotone.

Other actors fare a little better albeit in an unorthodox manner. Julia Stiles (10 Things I Hate About You) shows a glimmer of promise as a modern-day Ophelia. Liev Schreiber (Jakob the Liar), who is set to play Hamlet on stage this year, shows his mastery of the Elizabethan language as Laertes. Bill Murray (Cradle Will Rock) demonstrates that a wry, sardonic personality can bridge centuries in his role as Polonius.

Personally, I was more enamored with the conceptualization of this updated version than I was with its execution. Moving the setting from the royal courts of Denmark to the modern-day cutthroat business world of corporate America was an idea that should have been able to work effectively.

The tragic flaw of the filmmakers, in my estimation, was in trying to work with the original language and dialogue. Had they rewritten the script using modern language, their young star may have been more comfortable and the corporate analogies could have been both clarified and emphasized. As it is, once the premise was set, it was quickly forgotten in the old English verbiage that followed.

However, there were some fine touches as a result of the modern motif. In addition to the scene in the video store, the cameras also went inside the Guggenheim Museum. Some of the dialogue was delivered by electronic means (fax and answering machines) and Hamlet’s play within a play by which he means to expose Claudius’ treachery has become a video within a film.

William Shakespeare drew from many sources for the inspiration to his well-crafted words so it is not surprising to find similarities to biblical passages in many of his works. From Hamlet is this line:

“My words fly up, my thoughts remain below: words without thoughts never to heaven go.”

God is a searcher of hearts, not of eloquence or vocabulary. Words, without the backing of the heart’s intent, are just words.

But when ye pray, use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking. Matthew 6:7(KJV)

We are exhorted to pray. But our prayers must be the honest and fervent desires of our souls and not a memorized passage that through repeated use has lost much if not all of its meaning.

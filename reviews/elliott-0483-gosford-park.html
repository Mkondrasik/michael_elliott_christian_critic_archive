Looking at the cast list for Gosford Park can be likened unto gazing upon the elitist guest list for a weekend stay at an English country estate circa 1937. In fact, that is exactly the setting of this fine, often amusing, "Upstairs, Downstairs" examination of the two distinct classes of a dying form of society with a touch of an Agatha Christie murder mystery thrown in for good measure

Sir William McCordle (Michael Gambon, Charlotte Gray) and his wife, the Lady Sylvia McCordle (Kristin Scot Thomas, Life as a House) have invited friends and family to a weekend "shoot" in the country. As customary for that time, no upper class citizen would be caught dead without their entourage and so their manservants, valets, and maids are also invited to join them as a part of the estate's staff so as to make themselves available to meet the every need and whim of their employers.

As we follow the story, we soon discover that there are far more similarities than differences between the classes. They are both, after all, composed of people... and people seem to be the same the world over, regardless of social status. Deceptions, secrets, inflated egos and hidden desires are displayed by masters and servants alike. It is just that in the masters, such traits reflect a weakness of character while in the servants, such characteristics are tools of survival. murdered, so many different threads have already been woven into this tapestry that it will take the infallible logic of a Charlie Chan to unravel the mystery. Unfortunately, while an American producer (Bob Balaban, Ghost World) of Charlie Chan's films happens to be one the guests, the inscrutable detective is not. Instead, we get a bumbling English version of Inspector Clouseau (Stephen Fry, Wilde) to add a bit of comic relief.

Director Robert Altman (Dr. T and the Women has truly assembled an A-list of accomplished English and American actors, blending their talents into a glorious ensemble treat. It is a wonder that PBS did not go off the air with so many stars of English drama committed to this project. Such a large cast can tend to be overwhelming for an audience and there may be times when the nuances of character might have escaped us due to the number of relationships we're asked to keep in our heads, but the sheer opulence, magnificent set design, and polished performances, manage to keep us entertained even when we are not completely enthralled.

The cast is uniformly excellent with many worthy performances from which to choose. I found the "downstairs" staff to be generally more interesting to watch than their "upstairs" counterparts, although

Maggie Smith (Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone) is delightfully catty and snide as the gossip-loving Countess of Trentham.

Man has long made distinctions between classes of people where God makes none. What we do for a living matters nary a whit to Him. It is what we do with life that gets His interest.

And, ye masters, do the same things unto them, forbearing threatening: knowing that your Master also is in heaven; neither is there respect of persons with him.

Author-verified transcription

Meet Joe Black is an idea lifted from the 1930's play and movie, Death Takes A Holiday. Its concept is simple: Death, having toiled for many millennia, decides to take human form to learn firsthand about life and love. He chooses the 65-year old wealthy financier, William Parrish as his guide through the wonders and marvels of life. Once he learns what he wants to learn, he and Parrish will leave together.

Along the way he experiences the simple joys of being human. Pleasures such as eating peanut butter or being exposed to a loving family are brand new to him. Additionally, a subplot deals with an inside threat to Parrish's financial empire as well as some unresolved family issues.

Martin Brest (Scent of a Woman) has directed this film with the deliberate pacing of a ketchup bottle. Long pauses and thoughtful silence inhabit most scenes, giving a ponderous feel to the production. The subject matter might have been better served had Mr. Brest made substantial cuts during editing. The three hour running time of Meet Joe Black is simply one hour too long.

Anthony Hopkins (The Mask of Zorro) is the fated-to-die William Parrish. His is a thoughtful, intelligent characterization of a man who, having everything, has everything to lose. His performance is wasted, however, under the plodding direction of Mr. Brest.

The supporting characters also comport themselves admirably, most notably Marcia Gay Harden (Flubber) as Allison, Parrish's eldest (but not favorite) daughter who yearns for his love and acceptance, Jeffrey Tambor (The Larry Sanders Show), as Quince, Allison's husband, and Jake Weber (Dangerous Beauty) as Drew, the man who threatens to take down Parrish's empire.

Sadly, two of the main characters do not fare as well. Claire Forlani (Into My Heart) is nondescript as Susan Parrish, the apple of Daddy's eye who also catches the eye of "Death". Hers is a textbook performance which neither bores nor excites.

The major and insurmountable problem of Meet Joe Black lies within the performance of Brad Pitt (Seven Years in Tibet). His title character brings to mind the work of Peter Sellers as Chauncey Gardner in the brilliantly crafted Being There. In that movie, Mr. Sellers played a man who was shielded from the world then suddenly thrust into the middle of it, unfamiliar to him as it was. Mr. Pitt's portrayal of Death is remarkably similar. But it doesn't work.

Mr. Pitt is a fine actor and his scenes as the young lawyer who meets Susan in the coffee shop (this is before Death uses his body to visit the earth) are delightful. But as Death, Mr. Pitt's portrayal gets off on the wrong foot and never recovers. As a result, Death is portrayed as a handsome, intelligent but unsophisticated goober.

Spiritually, the movie has many flaws... not the least of which is its portrayal of death as a compassionate, almost tender entity. That is a devilish lie. God tells us plainly that death is an enemy. (I Corinthians 15:26). To characterize it any other way does an injustice to God and only serves to bolster the being who holds the power of death.

Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil; Hebrews 2:14 [KJV]

We have no need to fear death, as we can claim the victory of Jesus Christ, who overcame it. But neither should we welcome it to our homes and families. Death is the devil's work and one of his ultimate goals. Should the Lord tarry, death is something that will visit us all. But we know, because of the Christ in us, it will only be a temporary visit.

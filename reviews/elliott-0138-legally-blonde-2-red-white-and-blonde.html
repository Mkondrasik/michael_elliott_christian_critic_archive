Legally Blonde 2: Red, White & Blonde

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 2, 2003
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Joshua 23:10Romans 5:19James 5:16

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Influence; courage; conviction; making a difference; God’s power

Author-verified transcription

"It is great to be a blonde. With low expectations it's very easy to surprise people." So says Pamela Anderson with what could have been a mantra for the Legally Blonde movie. But in the case of both Ms. Anderson and the Legally Blonde sequel, we find ourselves still waiting for the surprise.

Legally Blonde 2: Red, White & Blonde is pretty much the same movie as the original... just in a different setting.

The eternally perky and undeniably blonde Elle Woods (Reese Witherspoon, Sweet Home Alabama) takes her perfectly pink self to our nation's capital to fight for the rights of her beloved Chihuahua, Bruiser. She has discovered that Bruiser's birth mother is being held against her doggie will and forced to serve as a test subject for a cosmetics company.

Outraged, Elle is ready to tackle the political powers-that-be in order to change the legislation on animal rights. Armed with the same brand of naiveté that Jimmy Stewart carried in Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, Elle goes about imprinting her unique but Cosmo-approved style upon the staid button-down community of Washington DC.

Just as in the first film, where the blonde sorority girl finds herself out of her element in the legal jungles at Harvard Law School, the former litigant in this issue is now facing the political jungles of Congress.

What makes the film work despite its derivative nature is the same thing that made the first film work. Reese Witherspoon is spot-on perfect in the role. Her wide-eyed, optimistic, and oh-so-blonde enthusiasm is once again contagious. She is as delightful as she was in the original film.

The rest of the supporting cast are functional but are given little of note to do. Mainly they serve as conveyers of plot points and twists, most of which we see coming long before they arrive. Sally Field (Forest Gump) is a career politician whose integrity becomes compromised when Elle's activities start hurting her campaign chest. Bob Newhart (Catch-22) is a political savvy doorman who helps Elle frame her first bill. Regina King (Daddy Day Care) takes over for Selma Blaire as Elle's new co-worker/jealous rival.

The one supporting role which did stand out belonged to Bruce McGill (The Legend of Bagger Vance) who, as a conservative Southern senator (and member of the NRA), wrestles with the realization that his beloved dog has developed a crush on Elle's Bruiser. Both dogs are male.

There are a handful of other homosexual references which have been inserted for comedic purposes. Some are funny. Some are not. Regardless, it is the one element that is noticeably different from the original film. Whether it is due to director Charles Herman-Wermfeld's (Kissing Jessica Stein) influence, or to novice screenwriter Kate Kondell, it is nevertheless there.

The underlying theme to LB2 is the impact that one person can have upon a system. Elle Woods, acting alone and against the odds of opposition, desired to change the way people thought about a specific idea.

Certainly we, who follow the one man who changed the world and brought new light into the lives of all men, can understand and appreciate this concept.

One man (or woman) can indeed have an impact that reaches far beyond his life. Jesus Christ made such an impact. Moses made such an impact. Martin Luther made such an impact. The impact was made because these men acted according to their unwavering belief in God. One man, acting from his convictions and backed by the power of God, is able to stand against a multitude who oppose him.

One man of you shall chase a thousand: for the LORD your God, he [it is] that fighteth for you, as he hath promised you. Joshua 23:10 (KJV)

We can argue whether animal rights is an issue that needs to be debated but what is more important is the fact that the film illustrates that a person who, believing in what she was doing, could make a difference. So can we.

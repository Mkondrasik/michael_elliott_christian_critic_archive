Nicholas Nickleby

Author-verified transcription

I was living in New York City when the acclaimed stage version of Charles Dickens' voluminous novel Nicholas Nickleby came to Broadway. With a running length of a mind boggling 8½ hours, I just couldn't imagine ever being persuaded to sit through it. For those among you who might share my aversion to overpriced, overly long costume dramas, there is now an alternative.

A new film adaptation of Dickens' classic work by Douglas McGrath (Emma) condenses the material to a much more manageable 2 hours and 12 minutes. It is sure that much will be lost with this Dickens "Lite" version but at least we can follow the story without fear of becoming permanently attached to the theater seat.

Losing one's parent early in life is always hard, but in 19th century England it was nearly impossible to survive it. When Nicholas Nickleby's father dies, leaving his family penniless and without visible means of support, 19 year old Nicholas (Charlie Hunnam, Abandon) turns to his uncle (Christopher Plummer, The Insider) for help, only to be betrayed by his treachery.

Separated from his beloved sister and mother, he is shipped off to work in the country at a boys' academy run by the vile and hateful Mr. Squeers (Jim Broadbent, Iris). When he can no longer stand by while Squeers and his shrewish wife severely abuse those in his charge, he leaves the academy, taking one of the abused young men, Smike (Jamie Bell, Billy Elliot), with him. We follow their adventures as they work their way back to London to find gainful employment and be reunited with Nickleby's family.

What a wonderful supporting cast McGrath has assembled for this character rich film. Broadbent, Bell and Plummer are superb in their respective roles. As are Anne Hathaway (The Princess Diaries), Juliet Stevenson (The One and Only), Nathan Lane (The Producers), and Timothy Spall (All or Nothing).

Charlie Hunnam, as the lead character, is serviceable in the role but not particularly captivating. A Heath Ledger look alike, much of the Dickensian dialogue sounds stilted or rehearsed as he delivers his lines.

In order to bring the film to its tolerable length, McGrath has cut away any sections which do not directly add to the narrative. As a result, the film's core message, a struggle between good vs. evil, is strongly emphasized. Nicholas is the very picture of virtue and righteousness who is treated unjustly by the evil machinations of his uncle Ralph. He places a high value upon his word, his name, and the honor of his family.

On the opposite end of the spectrum, there is Squeers and his wife who have absolutely no redeeming qualities whatsoever. Such a black and white depiction may ring a bit false in our world of nuances and various shades of gray, but at the same time, there's something refreshing about watching characters whose intentions and motives are so purely defined.

It should come as no surprise to see who triumphs over whom. God wrote this ending long before Charles Dickens did.

"Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." Romans 12:21 (KJV)

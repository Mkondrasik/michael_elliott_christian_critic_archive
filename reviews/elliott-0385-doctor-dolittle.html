Author-verified transcription

If we could talk to the animals, learn their languages... we'd find out that they have an obsession with butts. Or so it would seem from Eddie Murphy's latest offering, Doctor Dolittle.

Based on the writings of Hugh Lofting, Doctor Dolittle bears little resemblance to the film starring Rex Harrison that was released in the 1960's. Here, the humor is more bathroom oriented. Instead of exotic animals like the pushmi-pullyu that Mr. Harrison conversed with, Mr. Murphy spends most of his time talking with a dog, a couple of rats, and a gerbil.

The story begins with a young James Dolittle having conversations with the family dog. His father, not knowing what else to do, sends the dog away thus ending young James' animal discussions. That is until an event inexplicably triggers the phenomenon and the now grown up Dr. James Dolittle begins to hear and understand all of the animal kingdom. What is worse, they can understand him.

Soon, his house is overrun by creatures of every shape and size: geese, sheep, an owl, even a drunken monkey. They are all looking for free medical and psychological advice.

Eddie Murphy (The Nutty Professor) has toned down his comedic style considerably since his early success playing brash and brazen characters in films like Beverly Hills Cop and 48 Hours. Here, he is a family man, a bit of a milquetoast. And although he plays the title character, it is as if he has taken a supporting role. The real stars are the animals.

An all-star cast provides the voices for the various creatures. Norm MacDonald is wryly amusing as Lucky, the dog. Chris Rock's rapid delivery is a perfect fit for Rodney, the gerbil. Albert Brooks provides the suffering voice of a sick tiger, and Julie Kavner and Garry Shandling lend their voices to a pair of dueling pigeons.

The human actors have their moments as well. Jeffery Tambor (The Larry Sanders Show) is hysterical as a vet whose diagnosis always seems to center around the animal's nether regions. Oliver Platt (Dangerous Beauty) plays the self-absorbed partner of Dr. Dolittle whose only concern is how to spend the money that will be derived from selling their practice to Peter Boyle (Everybody Loves Raymond).

Director Betty Thomas (Private Parts) and the script by Nat Mauldin (The Preacher's Wife) too often goes for the cheap laugh. When it works, such as the scene that pairs Lucky the dog with the "anal-attentive" vet, it can be very funny. When it doesn't work, such as when Rodney the gerbil gets flushed down a toilet, we are reminded how foolish all this is.

And it is foolish. Dogs, cats, tigers, gerbils, fish, etc. don't talk. Is it egotism that makes us as humans want to give human characteristics to all things? God made the animal kingdom to be subject to man. Whether it be for food, labor or clothing, animals are placed for us to use. It is amazing how many well-intentioned and sympathetic humans buy into the lie that animals have the same rights as we. That is not only illogical, it is contrary to God's Word.

The biggest difference between a man and an animal is a spiritual one. Animals cannot become born again. They cannot receive the gift of holy spirit. You'd think that as Christians we wouldn't get sucked into this "humanization of animals" deception and yet one of the more absurd myths associated with December 25th is that the animals in the manger where baby Jesus was born had the ability to talk for that one night. What hooey!

On a more positive note, after the initial shock, Dr. Dolittle begins to see the benefits of his newfound ability. He starts to look upon it as a "gift" rather than a curse. All of us have longsuits - things that we do better than others. We shouldn't try to stifle these "gifts". Instead, we should develop and nurture them so that they may serve to glorify God and bless His people. What better use of our talents? It sure beats sitting around trading "poopy" jokes with a rat.

--Michael Elliott--

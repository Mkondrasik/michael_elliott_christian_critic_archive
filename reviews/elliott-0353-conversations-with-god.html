Conversations with God

Author-verified transcription

It all started, as deliverance often does, with a still, small voice. It led to a worldwide publishing phenomenon as Neale Donald Walsch's Conversations with God reached out to touch the hearts of millions of people seeking answers to the never-ending stream of questions facing them.

In the movie based upon his popular series of books, we see and hear the story that Walsch has related countless times during his speaking engagements. We see Walsch (Henry Czerny) in the car accident that puts him into a neck brace and begins a downward spiral that eventually lands him on the streets - homeless and jobless.

We see him struggle and fail as one setback after another leaves him with little hope and less confidence. These are the scenes where Canadian actor Henry Czerny really shines - showing us the self-loathing of his character as he must degrade himself in order to survive. The image that sticks with me is the sight of watching a shame-filled Walsch sitting by a dumpster eating a sandwich that he just pulled out of it as a mother and child stand judgmentally by.

Surprisingly enough, where the film comes up a little short is in the depiction of how Walsch traveled from the streets to the penthouse. His "Conversations with God," which he claims not to have written but to have merely dictated for publication, are given surprisingly brief screen time. Walsch goes from a street person to a motivational speaker in what appears to be a blink of an eye.

Walsch gives all the credit to God during the post-publication scenes where he is addressing an audience, but director Steven Simon breezes through the actual events where God is actively working to deliver Walsch from his troubles. A little shift of focus during some critical scenes would have gone a long way to make the film even more spiritually meaningful. Simon also chooses to end the film with a cheesy bit of theatrics that doesn't quite work for me.

Still, the message is clear and poignant without being overly "preachy." Simon and screenwriter Eric DelaBarre avoid many of the pitfalls that evangelical filmmakers often fall into. Conversations with God is a simple story that unfolds easily, largely in part to Czerny's naturalness as a performer.

Walsch's story is not unique. Nor should it be. After all, God is in the delivery business. When you need Him to be there, He is absolutely, positively, on time. And He is there for anybody who calls on Him. It wasn't that God chose Walsch of all men on this earth to deliver a message to the rest of the world. He has been asking all men to deliver the same message throughout time. Perhaps Walsch was just in a place in his life that he began to listen.

God uses a phrase throughout the Scriptures that speaks to this truth:

If any man have ears to hear, let him hear. Mark 7:16 (KJV)

God has been "speaking" to us all along. And he will continue to speak to us. It simply remains to us to start listening. God's still small voice can be lost to us if we allow the din of this world and the selfish lusts of our own hearts to drown out what He is saying. Adopting a meek and humble spirit is the first step to developing the spiritual ears to hear God.

Author-verified transcription

Forget "beauty." What needed to be in this Eye of the Beholder was a little logic. Based on the 1980 novel by Marc Behm, the film is a heavily stylized noir-like thriller cum psychological study whose story is secondary (if not completely irrelevant) to the manner in which it is told.

Ewan McGregor (Star Wars 1: The Phantom Menace) stars as an unnamed British surveillance agent known only by his code name "Eye." He has been assigned to watch the son of a highly placed official who suspects said offspring of wrongdoing. Using high tech equipment which enables him to look and listen from afar, the Eye watches his assignment die at the hands of a beautiful, mysterious woman who afterwards collapses in tearful regret.

Her name is Joanna Eris (Ashley Judd, Double Jeopardy) and he feels compelled to follow her, driven forward by the psychological urging of his young daughter who vanished seven years ago. "She's just a little girl, Daddy. Don't leave her."

Thus, the Eye begins a kindly stalking mission, becoming in essence a "guardian angel" for this remorseful killer. Quitting his job, he begins a 24 hour/7 day a week surveillance of Joanna, following her from city to city, wig to wig, watching her kill, helping her avoid capture, and collecting snow domes along the way.

For a character study, the film gives precious little insight to the characters themselves. The Eye lost his wife and daughter (and almost his mind, he says) for no reason given. He just came home one day and they weren't there. Joanna was similarly abandoned by her father one Christmas day, which I imagine is supposed to explain her murderous bent. After she kills, she sobs and says, "Merry Christmas Daddy."

The acting is adequate for the enigmatic characters which the cast is asked to play. Mr.

McGregor is enigmatically obsessed as the Eye. Ms. Judd is enigmatically murderous as Joanna. Genevieve Bujold (Last Night) is enigmatically instructive as a psychiatrist who helps young abandoned girls. Jason Priestly (Love and Death on Long Island) is enigmatically creepy as Gary, a drifter who must have drifted into this movie because there is little justification for his being there. k.d. lang is not that enigmatic but also not that interesting as Eye's tele-conduit to his superiors.

To be fair, writer/director Stephan Elliott (Welcome to Woop Woop) has not manufactured a complete bomb. Just an empty one. I suppose the term "dud" would be most accurate. Stylistically, there are some things to appreciate. Many of his scenes are well crafted and shot in an interesting way. His use of the snow domes as segues into scene changes was clever and effective. His integration of the highly technological "spy" devices employed by the Eye are attention getting.

But Mr. Elliott's artistic stylings and cinematic tricks are wasted on a story which has no point and characters which we never get to know. At the core of the film is a hollow space, a void which desperately needed to be filled.

Jesus Christ confronted people for treating their relationship with God in the same superficial manner. He exposed those who considered themselves righteous but got so caught up in the ritual of their religions that they lost the meaning of what they were doing even while rigidly adhering to the appearance of their supposed righteousness.

Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness. Matthew 23:27 [KJV]

Superficiality will only go so far. For something to have meaning and impact, it must have substance. Beauty may still be in the eye of the beholder, but only because there's no accounting for taste or intelligence. A wise beholder is one who will look beneath the surface and beyond the facade to behold the true beauty of an object, person, or place.

Author-verified transcription

There are certain films that should have been killed off in their infancy, before they ever were allowed to mature into a big screen production. The Big Hit is one such example.

It tells the story of Melvin Smiley, a young contract killer (Mark Wahlberg of Boogie Nights) whose only desire in life is to be universally liked. This little affectation of his is played for big laughs, all of them forced. His partners take advantage of him... his fiancée (Christina Applegate of Mars Attacks!) takes advantage of him... his girlfriend takes advantage of him. He takes Maalox. Anyone who paid full price to see this mess can readily identify with how he feels.

The "plot", if you can call it that, involves a kidnapping scheme cooked up by his moronic partners led by Cisco (Lou Diamond Phillips of Courage Under Fire). They grab the daughter (China Chow in her film debut) of a wealthy Japanese industrialist. What they somehow manage to overlook in their planning, is the fact that she is also the goddaughter of their crime boss, Paris. Let the hilarity commence.

The filmmakers never had a sense of where they wanted to take this movie. To make it look like an action adventure, they added lots of gunplay and explosions. To make it look like a dark comedy, they threw in a running gag involving a dismembered body. To make it look like a madcap romantic comedy they saddle Melvin with prospective Jewish in-laws played by Elliott Gould (M*A*S*H) and Lainie Kazan (Permanent Midnight) who object to their daughter marrying a "goyim". There's also an "wacky" bit involving an overdue video tape.

(Note to all filmmakers: Before attempting any comedy, please make sure that someone on your production crew has a sense of humor.)

Mr. Wahlberg and company flounder in uncharted seas as director Che-Kirk Wong, in his North American debut, fails to set a recognizable course for them. The script, by newcomer Ben Ramsey, is more of a hindrance than a help as its lame dialogue keeps interrupting the action scenes which are, after all, the only thing that is remotely interesting about this film (and even they, after a while, become mundane).

The stereo-typical Jewish portrayal that is turned in by Mr. Gould and Ms. Kazan is reminiscent of the poorer episodes of The Love Boat that we've seen. They are not alone in their broad, almost cartoonish characterizations. Mr. Phillips' frenetic, over-blown, presentation of a punk will not put him back on any casting director's A-list.

The only actor who escapes this film with his reputation untarnished is Avery Brooks (Star Trek: Deep Space Nine) as the leader of a crime cartel. His is a commanding presence on the screen. Ultimately, however, his performance is wasted, lost amidst the general confusion of mixed styles and vapid substance.

His character did have a line which was worth noting. When finding out that his goddaughter was kidnapped, he calls an emergency meeting of his henchmen to find the culprits. He opens the meeting by saying, "Even those who live outside the law agree to abide by certain rules." I suppose it is a twist of the adage, "Honor among thieves."

It happens to be a true statement. One of the accomplishments of Jesus Christ was that he exposed the hierarchy of the devil spirit kingdom. The chaos of this world only serves to mask the highly structured organization behind it. The lower ranked devil spirits operate at the command of their ruling spirits. A mistake that people will all too often make is to forget that the spiritual competition is comprised of two opposing forces. Each side has its goals and objectives as well as plans that will enable them to achieve those goals. Underestimating our spiritual opponent by ignoring the systematic nature of his attacks is a costly error.

Speaking of costly errors, don't make one by spending your money on The Big Hit. It is sure to appear on network television one day where you could see it for free. Of course many of the scenes will have to be cut and edited. In my estimation, that could only be an improvement.

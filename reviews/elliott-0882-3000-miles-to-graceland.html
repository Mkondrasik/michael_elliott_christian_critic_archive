3000 Miles to Graceland

Author-verified transcription

We should know by now that we can't believe everything we see in the "coming attractions." The trailers for 3000 Miles to Graceland make it look like we're going to watch a comic film about Elvis impersonators robbing a casino. In actuality, Elvis leaves the building less than an hour after the movie begins to roll. In his place, we are left with a highly violent picture about a sociopathic criminal and his attempt to recapture the 3.2 million dollars stolen from said casino.

Kevin Costner (Thirteen Days) stars as Thomas Murphy, an ex-con and very bad man who not only believes that he is the illegitimate child of Elvis Presley, he has decided to use the "King's" persona as a disguise to rob a Las Vegas casino during an Elvis convention.

Joining him in his masquerade is his ex-cell mate, Michael (Kurt Russell, Breakdown), along with Hanson (Christian Slater, The Contender), Gus (David Arquette, Scream), and Franklin (Bokeem Woodbine, Dead Presidents). Although the robbery turns bloody, they successfully walk away with the money. That's when the real trouble starts. Little did his partners in crime know that Murphy doesn't like to share.

When the gun smoke clears, Michael and Cybil (Courtney Cox Arquette, Friends), a truck stop floozy looking to cash in on an opportunity for a better life, find themselves pitted against Murphy for possession of the loot. The double-crosses multiply rapidly, along with the increasingly violent gunfights, as the money grows and changes hands, leaving in its wake a trail of bodies.

Elvis, Las Vegas and this film all share a common element... excess. Director Demian Lichtenstein (Lowball) doesn't know when to pull back from exercising the editing effects that must be employed judiciously if they are to be effective. Here, the film doesn't require the stylized effects which Lichtenstein provides and the over-the-top gunplay quickly becomes tiresome.

Murphy is a sociopathic maniac. His inherent sense of right and wrong is nonexistent. Costner captures the strong-willed, strong-minded, immoral personality of Murphy with a chilling clarity.

Outside of Kevin Pollack (A Few Good Men), cast in a minor role as the FBI agent chasing Murphy, Kurt Russell, as Michael, is the most appealing character in the film. He's also the one that has the Elvis persona down pat, having played Elvis Presley in a 1979 TV movie. Though still a criminal, Michael appears to have a moral code by which he lives.

Everyone else in the film falls somewhere in the spectrum between Murphy and Michael, including Courtney Cox Arquette as Michael's lust interest and young David Kaye (Legends of the Fall) as her kleptomaniac son.

When all is said and done, this is a film about bad guys. Bad guys who kill without hesitation. The filmmakers ask us to sympathize and root for them to succeed and use their considerable manipulation skills to marshall our emotions to their side. It says a lot about our society when the only verbalized reaction of regret or dismay from the audience comes at the demise of a cherry red 1959 Cadillac convertible. The dozens of characters who were killed along the way don't even merit a whimper.

The main problem with a story which features bad men as the protagonists is that there is no good way to end the film. If the bad men win, it sends a message that crime pays. If the protagonists lose, it's a depressing, unedifying experience. Either way, the audience suffers. Going to the scriptures reminds us of the truth God would have us keep in our hearts:

Be not thou envious against evil men, neither desire to be with them. For their heart studieth destruction, and their lips talk of mischief. Proverbs 24:1-2 (KJV)

No sum, not 3.2 million nor 32 million, is worth forsaking the basic tenets of God.

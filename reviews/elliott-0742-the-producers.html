Author-verified transcription

"We did everything wrong. Where did we go right?" is the lamented cry of Max Bialystock, Broadway's favorite failed showman in Mel Brook's The Producers.

Originally a 1968 comedy starring Zero Mostel and a then-unknown Gene Wilder, it had a wildly successful revival in 2001 as a multiple award-winning Broadway musical. Now, in 2005, many of the original Broadway cast members reunite to recapture the magic for movie audiences.

Max Bialystock (Nathan Lane), after yet another failed show (Funny Boy, a musical version of Hamlet), is paid a visit by Leo Bloom (Matthew Broderick), a cowardly, neurotic accountant. Bialystock and Bloom join forces to concoct a scheme that will make them wealthy. All they need do is to produce a sure fire flop.

They choose "Springtime for Hitler," a musical love letter to the former Fuhrer of the Third Reich written by a crazed neo-Nazi (Will Ferrell). To make sure the show fails, they hire Roger DeBris (Gary Beach), a flamboyantly flouncing director who will be sure to put his own flagrantly homosexual stamp upon the production.

All is going according to plan which makes it all the more confusing when the show gets rave reviews. Bialystock and Bloom sold more shares of the show (mostly to oversexed octogenarians) than is mathematically possible to divide. That's why they needed a flop. Their scheme just won't work if the show's a hit.

Susan Stroman, who directed the phenomenally successful Broadway musical, makes her feature film debut here but doesn't really make a smooth transition from stage to screen. She mounts her movie as if she were still working under a proscenium arch. She directs her cast to perform the broad, slapstick moves and mugging that may have played well to the 12th row and beyond but seem obvious and artificial when magnified for a motion picture audience.

The songs are instantly forgettable but they were never the draw of the show. The energy and commitment of the two central performers (Lane & Broderick) give testimony as to why The Producers was, for a time, the hottest ticket on Broadway. They throw themselves into the roles with much practiced abandon. What they needed to find for the film were the small nuances that might have smoothed out the rough theatrical edges.

Generating far more laughs are the stereotypically exaggerated performances of Gary Beach (reprising his Tony Award winning role as the fey director) and Roger Bart (as the director's common law assistant, Carmen Ghia). They prance about the screen, borrowing just about every stereotypically "gay" mannerism or affectation ever depicted.

Forgetting the potentially offensive elements (the Nazi jokes, the gay jokes, the sex jokes, etc.), the film is ultimately about a man's desire to get rich by deceiving others. The odd thing about deception: those who practice it are most effective in deceiving themselves.

But evil men and seducers shall wax worse and worse, deceiving, and being deceived. 2 Timothy 3:13 (KJV)

Lying, cheating and stealing may seem to some to be a shortcut way to get one's uttermost desires but shortcuts often take you to places you really don't want to go. Despite the outcome of this musical comedy romp, the fruit born of Max and Leo's actions are hardly the kind of thing we want to produce in our own lives.

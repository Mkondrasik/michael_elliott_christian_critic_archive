Dance With Me

Author-verified transcription

In 1996, the highly acclaimed Japanese film, Shall We Dance? asked its audience the wonderfully romantic question of its title. Now, two years later, here comes the American film, Dance With Me, which takes that same question and transforms it into an unromantic demand.

Dance With Me tells the story of Rafael Infante (Chayanne, Linda Sara), who leaves Cuba to come to America in order to meet the father he never knew. In the process, he is introduced to the members of a dance studio whose choreographed styles are nothing like the rhythmic, emotion-filled dancing of his native land.

Vanessa Williams (Eraser) stars as Ruby Sinclair, the dance instructor with whom Rafael quickly becomes enamored. Ruby, however, has big plans for succeeding as a professional ballroom dancer. Her hopes may hinge upon her reconciliation with her ex-partner (and father of her child) Julian Marshall, played by three time U.S. Latin Dance champion Rick Valenzuela.

Mr. Chayanne uses a dance technique which resembles something along the lines of a two ton grizzly with its paw caught in a bear trap. From the context of the film, I gathered that he was supposed to be a good dancer, but you couldn't prove it by me.

Kris Kristofferson (Blade) takes on the part of John Burnett, a man who, taking the Love Boat designation far too literally, had a brief affair with Rafael's mother while working on a cruise ship. Years later, the result of that assignation shows up at his dance studio, looking for work.

Joan Plowright (Tea With Mussolini) does an amusing turn as Bea Johnson, one of the regulars (read amateurs) of the studio. With the exception of an ill-conceived and embarrassingly bad segment set at a dance competition, Ms. Plowright is delightful to watch.

Dance With Me started out with great promise: It begins in Cuba, with its Latin beat and flavor permeating every scene. It began to lose its focus when it shifted settings to the United States. It lost its way completely when it went to Las Vegas for an interminable dance competition.

In Rafael's world, there is no such thing as "professional dance." Everybody dances in Cuba. Dance is an emotional response driven by the music that is felt more than heard. Rafael watches in an amused bewilderment as the American "professionals" practice their dance routines without the benefit of the music to guide their way.

Dancing has long been a method used to express oneself before God:

"Praise him with the timbrel and dance: praise him with stringed instruments and organs." Psalms 150:4 (KJV)

And Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a timbrel in her hand; and all the women went out after her with timbrels and with dances. And Miriam answered them, Sing ye to the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously; Exodus 15:20-21 (KJV)

Dancing can and should be an expression of the abundance of joy and thankfulness that resides in and overflows from our hearts. Or it can be the soulless, mechanical, over-choreographed and over-rehearsed memorization of steps. The difference may not be always discernible to the eye, but there's no deceiving the spirit.

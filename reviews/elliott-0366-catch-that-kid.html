Catch That Kid

1 hr: 34 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Consequences; means and ends; judgment; responsibility

Author-verified transcription

Catch That Kid is the American remake of a critically acclaimed Danish film called Klatretesen which played in some US film festivals a couple of years back. I hope that the film makes more sense when spoken in a foreign language. It is hard to recommend that anyone "catch" this American version.

12-year-old Maddie (Kristen Stewart) is very attached to her Father (Sam Robards), a go-cart track owner, and tries to emulate him in every aspect...

Especially his fondness for rock climbing. When he suffers a delayed paralysis because of a nasty fall that occurs years earlier, Maddie learns that the only thing that can help him is an experimental procedure available only in Denmark.

The problem is that the procedure is not covered by insurance and costs a quarter of a million dollars. Unable to raise or borrow the money by conventional means, Maddie decides to steal it. Fortunately, her mother (Jennifer Beals) is a security expert who had just been hired to install a high tech system in the local bank so Maddie has a bit of inside information at her disposal.

To help her with her crime, she enlists her two friends, Austin (Corbin Bleau) who is a whiz with computers and Gus (Max Theiriot) who is handy with go cart mechanics. Since both of them have school kid crushes on Maddie, they are more than happy to break the law for her.

Director Bart Freundlich was most probably looking to break into the Spy Kids, Agent Cody Banks genre (kids with high tech equipment) but he really needed to do something more than just casting a bunch of kids and giving them high tech equipment to play with. The story is beyond juvenile - it is an insult to juveniles everywhere. The implied message is immoral. The adults in the film are either weak and disabled or belong to the moronically deranged. The latter category is led by James LeGros as the bank's buffoon of a commando security guard.

Kristen Stewart as Maddie is tomboyishly appealing in a kid sort of way. The rest of the cast range from average and bland to stereotypically cartoon-like.

Though it is meant to be all in good fun, the film's message is unmistakable. The ends justifies the means. If the stakes are high enough, any action - even illegal action - is excusable. Is this really the type of lesson we want to teach our preteens?

In the film we see a young girl lie to her friends, using their affection for her to get what she wants. We see her blatantly disobeying her parents. We see her risk her life on a foolhardy untethered "free climb." We see her breaking into a safe and stealing $250,000. We see her running from the police. We see her involved in a high speed go cart chase with her 2 year old brother sitting in her lap. But are we to accept that because she is doing all this for a good cause, there won't be consequences to these outrageous actions?

The film closes with a schmaltzy, feel good, "everything works out in the end" final act but by then the message has already been delivered. And it is a lie. The ends do not justify the means. We will be judged and rewarded by the means we employ regardless of what the end may be.

For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things done in his body, according to that he hath done, whether it be good or bad. 2 Corinthians 5:10 (KJV)

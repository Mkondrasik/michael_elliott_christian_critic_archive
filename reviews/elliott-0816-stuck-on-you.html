Stuck on You

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 12, 2003
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 40 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Ephesians 5:31-32Philippians 2:3Ecclesiastes 4:9-10

Sermon / spiritual topic: Marriage; Selflessness; One Flesh

Author-verified transcription

The Farrelly Brothers have a brand of humor that takes them into some pretty unconventional areas. After their debut with Dumb and Dumber (about a pair of incredibly stupid friends), they continued their streak of perverse, off the wall character comedy with Kingpin (about professional bowlers), and then struck cinematic gold with the wildly popular There's Something About Mary. Since then they've produced or directed films which attempted to find the humor in incest, bodily functions, multiple personalities, and extreme obesity.

They're back and this time turn their comedy spotlight on the imagined life shared by a pair of co-joined twins. Matt Damon and Greg Kinnear star as the inseparable brothers who have managed to get along quite well in life despite their obvious (to us) handicap.

Bob (Matt Damon) is a short order cook and restaurant owner in Martha's Vineyard. Walt (Greg Kinnear) is an aspiring actor who longs to go to Hollywood and see where his talent can take him. Vowing never to stand in the way of the other's goals, Bob agrees to "tag along" while Walt looks for his big break.

It just so happens that Bob's Internet girlfriend lives in LA and is anxious to meet him. The problem is that he never had the courage to tell her about Walt. So they must devise ways to keep their physical uniqueness from her.

Much of the humor, some of which works and some that doesn't, comes from the way Bob and Walt hardly acknowledge that they are co-joined. Whether they are playing hockey, cooking burgers, or acting in a community theater production, they've worked out the physical details that allows them not only to function but to excel at what they do.

Obviously there is a potential here for pushing the envelope of taste over the edge. While the Farrellys do nose right up to that precipice, they are careful to infuse the characters with enough warmth and sweetness that we don't feel they are being exploited.

Much of the credit belongs to Greg Kinnear whose heartfelt characterization of the more gregarious Walt is one of the better performances in the film. The other belongs to none other than Cher, who gamely plays herself... at least a tongue-in-cheek distorted version of herself as a diva with little compassion and even less self awareness. Matt Damon does well with the physical aspect of being Walt's other half but, as the more pensive, insecure brother, his work is less showy and far less humorous.

What lifts this film out of the danger zone of political incorrectness is the heart that the filmmakers have given to the brothers' relationship. It is clear that both Bob and Walt complete the other in ways that transcend physicality. They strengthen each other, encourage each other, respect and love each other with a selflessness that is pure.

Without intending to be flippant, I can see how we might view the brothers' relationship to be an example of the "one flesh" commitment God had designed a marriage to be. Bob and Walt were forced, through a physical abnormality, to go through life always conscious of the other person. In order to function, each twin was forced to place the needs of his brother on an equal plane with his own needs.

If more husbands and wives could view their relationship with the same needful consideration, they might develop a bond that is just as strong as the unimagined tie between Bob and Walt. Perhaps then we would better understand why God used marriage as an analogy for our relationship with Christ.

For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh. This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church. Ephesians 5:31-32 (KJV)

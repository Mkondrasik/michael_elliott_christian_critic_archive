Anywhere But Here

Michael Elliott
Original publication date

Rating
3 stars
Running time
1 hr: 54 min

Published Web Version

Sermon / spiritual topic: Parental Responsibility; Parenting; Correction; Teaching Children; Maturity

Author-verified transcription

In Stepmom, Susan Sarandon portrayed a terminally ill mother who tries to cope with the knowledge that she will be leaving her children for another to raise. In the new film Anywhere But Here, her motherly character isn't going anywhere... much to the dismay of her daughter.

She plays Adele August, a single mother who has decided to escape from her dreary, boring life in Bay City, Wisconsin, dragging her uncooperative and unappreciative daughter Ann with her. Heading for the glitzy glamour of Beverly Hills, CA, Adele has as many stars in her eyes as she has rocks in her head. At fourteen, Ann (Natalie Portman, Star Wars: Episode 1) has more common sense and a more realistic view of life than her mother, but since she's the child, she's forced to reluctantly follow the flighty albeit interesting path of Adele, leaving her friends, family, and admittedly, a small town perspective behind.

Of course, without proper planning or realistic expectations, their life in Beverly Hills is not the movie star fantasy of which Adele dreams. Their strained relationship is tested further as they struggle to succeed in their new environs. Ann misses her friends in Bay City, does not share the same vision for her life that Adele forces upon her, and is merely counting the days until she can leave her mother with the same glee that her mother left Wisconsin.

The rather mundane script by Alvin Sargent (Bogus), which is based upon the novel by Mona Simpson, is salvaged by a pair of heartfelt and winning performances on the part of the leads. Ms. Sarandon can play ditzy with the best of them, although it is difficult to imagine a bulb as dim as the one portrayed here. Looking at life through her rose-colored glasses, Adele's response to problems is to get ice cream and sing cheery songs until the moment passes. Meanwhile, the lights go out because the electric bill remains unpaid.

Natalie Portman is granted an opportunity to exercise the acting muscles which lay dormant in her Star Wars adventure. As Ann, she is able to convey the conflicting emotions that seem to lie at the heart of a burgeoning adolescent. Simultaneously close to her mother even as she is embarrassed by her, Ms. Portman gives us a number of heart-wrenching moments.

Shawn Hatosy (Outside Providence) is very likable as Ann's cousin and closest friend, Benny. Michael Miloban (She's All That) has two amusing scenes as a compassionate cop who, instead of tickets, gives both Ann and Adele advice at pivotal moments.

Director Wayne Wang (The Joy Luck Club) has shaped a wafer-thin but still pleasing story in which two actresses are given an opportunity to build complete and complex characterizations. The emotions run deep and strong but never in a manipulative or exploitative manner.

Though set in the mid to late 1990's, the film has the feel of being strangely out-of-time. The naiveté of certain characters, the awkward goofiness of the teens, and the squeaky clean California image being presented all help to add to the general anachronistic aura.

What is timeless is the godly responsibility of a parent or mother. Adele, as well-meaning as her heart may have been, was not a great example of a mother nurturing her child or preparing her child by the teaching a parent is called to do.

Our children cannot raise themselves. And we certainly cannot expect them to take on the "parental responsibility" while we, as adults, lose ourselves in selfish fantasies and unrealized dreams.

The rod of correction imparts wisdom, but a child left to himself disgraces his mother. Proverbs 29:15 [NIV]

The rod of correction is not a physical implement used to beat a child into submission but rather a figure of speech used to represent the standard of truth (i.e. The Word of God) that can be used to correct a child when he or she errs. The godly responsibility for correcting a child is given not to teachers, not to the child's peers, but only to the parents of the child. And we will each be judged on how well we handle that responsibility.

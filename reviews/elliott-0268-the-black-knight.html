The Black Knight

Rating

1 hr: 45 min

Published Web Version

Author-verified transcription

Gadzooks! What stench is it that befouleth the eventide air? Methinks it cometh from yonder multiplex. Ay me, tis true; for there doth The Black Knight run its unfragrant length. The name alone doth portend the unhappy truth for 'tis indeed a black night that such fare shouldst appear before our suffering eyes.

Sheesh, a guy could get a brain cramp from writing such tripe... (let us pause in tribute to the screenwriters of The Black Knight for whom tripe apparently comes so trippingly off the tongue.)

In a nutshell: Martin Lawrence (Big Momma's House) plays Jamal Sky-Walker (another pause for groan from Star Wars fans), disgruntled employee of Medieval World Fun Center. Just before he could transfer his allegiance to the more upscale Castle World, he falls into a scum-covered moat only to resurface in the year of our Lord 1328.

Being as dense as the average castle wall, Sky-Walker doesn't realize at first that he's shifted time zones by a matter of centuries. His 21st century urban street persona confuses the poor English townsfolk who explain his oddness by concluding that he must be from France.

Sky-Walker befriends King Leo (Kevin Conway, Thirteen Days); romances the only black woman in the entire fiefdom (Marsha Thomason, Long Time Dead); opposes the evil knight Percival (Vincent Regan, The Messenger); and helps a drunken knight (Tom Wilkinson, Shakespeare in Love) restore the rightful queen to the throne which King Leo has unlawfully usurped. Worthy deeds for a noble knight... or as Jamal would say, "Ain't nothin' but a l'il sumpin' sumpin'."

Martin Lawrence overplays his role in a shameless display of mugging; shucking and jiving as he does through the less than sanitary 14th century. The film is built not so much from a narrative plot line as it is from a string of comical situations in which to place Lawrence (Jamal teaches the king's court how to boogie down; Jamal is introduced to a 14th century outhouse; Jamal pretends to be a court jester). All in all The Black Knight is a silly exercise that will quickly become tiresome to all but the most enthusiastic Martin Lawrence fan.

Kevin Conway looks appropriately regal as Leo and Vincent Regan could have stepped off the set of Camelot. But there is little substance beneath the appearance. The Black Knight is a star vehicle (albeit one badly in need of a tune-up) for Lawrence while the supporting players serve as the fuzzy dice which dangle from the rear view mirror. We know that they are there, but there's not much they can do to make the car run better.

In the course of the story, Jamal moves from a "every man for himself" mentality to a more altruistic, community-minded outlook. The latter, of course, is more in line with scriptural exhortation.

Hereby perceive we the love of God, because he laid down his life for us: and we ought to lay down our lives for the brethren. But whoso hath this world's good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him? 1 John 3:16-17 (KJV)

Life is far too short and precious to go through it alone, only acting when it benefits us. As we cast off our own selfishness and reach out to assist, support and bless others, the "benefits" that will come our way will far outstrip anything we could horde for ourselves.

It is a lesson that Jamal learned by traveling back to a time when chivalry was not dead. A time when a man would rather lose his life than his honor. Such characteristics, which literature has long associated with the Middle Ages, are in actuality timeless. Such noble qualities will never go out of fashion nor will they ever go unrewarded.

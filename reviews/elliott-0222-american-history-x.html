American History X

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 20, 1998

3 stars

Sermon / spiritual topic: Hatred; racism; prejudice; renewing the mind; change

Author-verified transcription

If there is one thing this world has in abundance... it's hate. American History X is a "warts and all" telling of a young man consumed with hatred and racial prejudice and where that perverted mindset leads him.

Derek Vinyard (Edward Norton, The Rounders) is your typical poster child for neo-nazism. A skinhead, he wears his white supremacy badge as proudly as he does the enblazened swastika tattooed on his chest. Intelligent, fiercely proud of being an American yet disillusioned with where his beloved country is headed, Derek is one of the leaders and recruiters for the disenfranchised youth in his community.

His brother, Danny (Edward Furlong, Pecker), who suffers from a blind hero-worship of Derek, is headed in the same direction unless something happens to turn the kid around. After writing a book report on Mein Kampf, Danny's "education" is turned over to history teacher Bob Sweeny (Avery Brooks, The Big Hit) who assigns Danny a project... To write about his brother who was just released from prison. The impromptu "class" is simply called American History X.

The subsequent story of Derek Vinyard is told in a series of flashbacks (shot in stark and dramatically effective black and white photography). The current scenes dealing with Derek's actions upon being released from prison are shot in color. The clear delineation between past and present added greatly to our understanding of the story line and the overall impact of the film.

The depiction of the brutal violence and ugliness of racial hatred is unsettling to say the least. What lifts this movie out of the muck and mire of its subject matter is the amazing performance of Edward Norton. His studied development of a young and vulnerable man who buys into the neo-Nazi doctrine, becomes one of its most committed devotees, gradually learns that the doctrine is a lie, tries to break from it while stopping his brother from repeating his mistakes, is perfect. His characterization is totally believable and frighteningly real.

Edward Furlong's Danny shows enough shadings for us to believe that he is capable of becoming a mirror image of Derek. Stacey Keach (Escape From LA) is Cameron, the sickening head of the neo-Nazi movement of which Derek is a disciple. Cameron stays behind the scenes when violence or action is "required" in order to keep his nose (and record) clean.

Novice director Tony Kaye has publicly expressed his displeasure at the way his film has been edited, going so far as requesting his directorial credits be removed. While the movie does show us its flaws, it remains a remarkable achievement for a first time director.

Hatred is a losing proposition. As one of the characters in the film put it. It's baggage. With the various responsibilities and obligations that we carry through this life, who's got the room or the strength to waste on hatred?

Hatred is not a born or inherited trait. It is a learned behavior. It is an attitude, emotion, or mindset that has to be first placed in the mind. Which means that conversely, it can be removed. It can be unlearned. The best scriptural example we can point to is the great apostle, Paul.

For ye have heard of my conversation in time past in the Jews' religion, how that beyond measure I persecuted the church of God, and wasted it: And profited in the Jews' religion above many my equals in mine own nation, being more exceedingly zealous of the traditions of my fathers. Galatians 1:13-14 [KJV]

Paul persecuted the church to the point of dragging believers out of their homes and sending them to their deaths. And yet he became the greatest believer of his day. If Paul could change his mindset to agree with the Word of God, anybody could... Regardless of the devilish nature of their past.

Black Hawk Down

Author-verified transcription

"It doesn't matter what I think... Once that first bullet goes past your head, politics goes out the window." This philosophy, stated by a seasoned veteran portrayed in the film Black Hawk Down, is being fully embraced by the film's director Ridley Scott (Hannibal) and producer Jerry Bruckheimer (Pearl Harbor).

Based upon an actual event, Black Hawk Down follows a 1993 mission by US special forces (a combination of Rangers, Delta Force soldiers, and pilots of the 160th SOAR) to extract a warlord interfering with humanitarian efforts.

It is immanently clear that the filmmakers do not wish to explore the political aspects of whether or not American forces should have been there in the first place. They are far more interested in examining the nature and character of men in the midst of combat.

For those unfamiliar with the historical event, Somalia was being decimated from within due to a civil war that had been going on for years. Famine had reached so crucial a level as to warrant international intervention. When the flow of humanitarian aid was interrupted by the Somalian warlord, Mohamed Farrah Aidid, American forces were deployed to Mogadishu to remove him and his chief lieutenants from the scene.

Things did not go according to plan and the end result was that 2 helicopters were shot down and a number of ground forces were pinned behind "enemy lines." Rescue operations were being hampered by opposition forces so those in Mogadishu were left to their own devices against enormous (and well armed) odds until armored extraction became available.

During this extended firefight, the fiercest involving American troops since the Vietnam War, 17 American soldiers lost their lives and over 1,000 Somalians were reportedly killed. Black Hawk Down presents its story from the American soldiers' point of view.

Scott and Bruckheimer vividly depict the chaos and physical devastation that exists in a war zone. Anyone who felt that the first half hour of Saving Private Ryan was difficult to watch will have a similar problem with the bulk of Black Hawk Down. The body count is high and in war, men don't die clean deaths. There is a considerable amount of carnage up there on the screen and it continues throughout the length of the film.

The large ensemble cast of men include Josh Harnett (Pearl Harbor) as a young sergeant given his first combat command; Ewan McGregor (Moulin Rouge) as a clerk called to see action for the first time in his military career; Tom Sizemore (Saving Private Ryan) as the cool-under-fire Ranger squad leader; and Eric Bana (Chopper) as "Hoot," a legendary Delta soldier.

There have been complaints from some groups that Black Hawk Down paints the Somalians as savages. There are others who claim that the movie does not accurately portray some of the more unpleasant actions of the US troops. I can't speak to the accuracy of the film's narrative but its heart is something that does ring true. After all the propaganda and political positioning is swept away, the bottom line is that these highly trained and conditioned soldiers put themselves in harm's way to support, protect, and defend the lives of the men fighting with them. Let others debate the politics of the time. This film instead recognizes and portrays the incredible camaraderie that exists among combat troops. These men were willing to sacrifice their lives, not for some idealistic cause or humanitarian effort... but simply for each other.

Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends. John 15:13 (KJV)

May God continue to bless the men and women in our armed forces here and abroad for their patriotism, dedication, and sacrifices made.

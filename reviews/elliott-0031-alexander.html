Alexander

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 24, 2004
Rating
1 star
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Counsel; wisdom; spiritual truth; consequences

Author-verified transcription

How can a man whose name is forever linked with greatness be the subject of a movie so bad? Blame the man behind the camera. Oliver Stone gives Alexander a flawed-filled epic treatment that may do more to destroy the conqueror’s legend than all the armies he once opposed.

Colin Farrell plays the titular warrior, son of a power-hungry, snake-handling woman (Angelina Jolie) who insists on telling her son that he was conceived by Zeus himself. For her, that fiction is preferable to the truth – that he is the son of a one-eyed lout of a king (Val Kilmer) who plans to leave his Macedonian kingdom to the offspring of another bed partner. He never got the chance.

Alexander then hits the road and with his massive army, manages to conquer most of the known world. Stone shows us little of this, preferring to concentrate on the emotional and psychological dramas that are being played out between Alexander, Olympius (his mother), Philip (his father), Roxane (his non-Greek wife played by Rosario Dawson), and Hephaestion (his homosexual lover played by Jared Leto).

Three hours later, as Alexander lies on his death bed, we are left with a dull headache and the sense that we know less about Alexander’s life than when the movie began.

Stone fails in his attempt to tell the story of Alexander’s life early on. Utilizing an old screenwriting technique, he has Anthony Hopkins, as the aged Ptolemy, narrating the history of Alexander to his scribe so that it might be recorded for posterity’s sake. The droning narration may have worked for posterity but it did nothing for my posterior which grew numb waiting for something to happen.

Colin Farrell just doesn’t portray enough of the inner strength and confidence that a world leader must exude. His Alexander is filled with doubts, uncertainties, and paranoia - hardly the kind of man we’d expect an army to follow to the ends of the earth.

Jared Leto, as Alexander’s male “life-partner” wears eye liner and, for the most part, does little except stand in the presence of his king and lover to gaze adoringly upon him.

Angelina Jolie, who is one year older than Farrell, was simply miscast in the role of Alexander’s mother. Though she’s a credible actress, she’s not credible in this part.

There isn’t much that I can find to recommend about this nearly 3 hour debacle. Perhaps it was the very lack of redeeming qualities that caused me to reflect upon the significance of this time in history.

Alexander the Great ruled around from 336BC – 325BC. In the 400 years preceding the birth of Jesus Christ, there was no functioning prophet anywhere on earth. The last, Malachi, is recorded to have operated some 50 years earlier than the time of this film.

Even though Macedon was a Gentile nation and would have only been slightly influenced by the teaching and preaching of the Judean prophets, the complete lack of a strong spiritual voice meant that there was absolutely no single standard for truth anywhere in the land. No wonder it was so hard to find a redemptive element in the characters or the story of their lives.

For they [are] a nation void of counsel, neither [is there any] understanding in them. O that they were wise, [that] they understood this, [that] they would consider their latter end! Deuteronomy 32:28-29 (KJV)

It is heartening to know that Macedonia would one day be flooded with light and serve as a stepping stone in the outreach of God’s Word to all nations. It just didn’t happen during the timeframe in which this picture was set.

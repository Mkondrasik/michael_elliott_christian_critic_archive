Author-verified transcription

“The Legend of Drunken Master” was filmed and released in Hong Kong in 1994 and is the sequel of the 1978 film, “Drunken Master” which helped to put Jackie Chan (“Shanghai Noon”) on the celebrity map. Now dubbed in English and getting its US release via Dimension Films, “The Legend of Drunken Master” is sure to please Chan’s fans and kung-fu aficionados.

As with most of Chan’s Hong Kong films, the plot is secondary to the action. Here, Chan’s character, Wong Fei-hong, becomes accidentally involved in the illegal smuggling of Chinese artifacts by political officials. Honestly, that’s all that is needed to be known as the filmmakers pay little attention to such mundane matters.

The title comes from a style of fighting called “drunken boxing” which resembles an inebriated man’s stagger. In fact, as the movie makes clear, a few drinks even gives the fighter more power as it loosens him up and helps to raise his pain threshold. Fei-hong is a master of this style of fighting although his father (Ti Lung, “The Kid”) disapproves and forbids him to practice it. On the other hand, Fei-hong’s stepmother (Anita Mui, “My Father Is A Hero”) secretly encourages Fei-hong to use his drunken boxing technique at every opportunity.

The film’s action sequences are breathtaking, with Chan’s fight choreography as inventive, exciting, and oftentimes unbelievable as anything we’ve seen before from this genre. The pure speed of the action and the intricacies of the movements are more than worth a look-see.

The main fights, which are after all the benchmarks of a Jackie Chan film, take place underneath a train, in a village market, on the second floor of a restaurant, and in a steel factory. The first and the final sequence, in my mind, stand out as being the most effective.

To evaluate the acting in a kung-fu movie sometimes feels like a lesson in futility, but in “The Legend of Drunken Master” there are actually a number of performances to mention. Anita Mui, who plays Chan’s scheming and manipulative stepmother, gives us an idea of what Lucille Ball might have been like if she had known how to kick-box. Ti Lung, as Chan’s loving but stern father is a perfect picture of propriety. And Ken Lo Houi-Kang, who serves as Jackie Chan’s body guard off the screen, does quite well in the 20 minute one on one fight in the steel mill which ends the picture.

Chan, of course, is exceptional in the fight sequences. Few martial artists have the necessary speed, skill, and comic timing to do what Jackie Chan does time and time again. The main enjoyment of a film such as this is in the observation of an artist doing what others haven’t even thought about trying.

The obvious disappointment is in the thematic device which involves the “glorification” of alcohol. While moderation is preached in the film by Fei-hong’s father (“Drunken boxers become drunken fools”), it is seldom practiced. Even when a bout of overindulgence leads to family shame and disgrace, Fei-hong continues to rely on his drunken style to win back his family honor. The scriptures warn us about the temptations of alcohol:

Wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging: and whosoever is deceived thereby is not wise. Proverbs 20:1 (KJV)

To arrive at one’s peak performance, be it physically or mentally, we can be assured that the route to take will not be via a bottle. This is especially true of those of us who actively engage in the spiritual competition of life.

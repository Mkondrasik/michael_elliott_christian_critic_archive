Author-verified transcription

Near the end of the film, adventurer Rick O'Connell (Brendan Fraser, Monkeybone) is asked what he's up against this time. His response: "Oh, the usual... mummies, pygmies, big bugs." The usual indeed. The Mummy Returns just in time to launch the summer season of the studios' budget busting films and it proves that there are those who still believe in the old adage, "if it ain't broke, don't fix it." This is The Mummy slightly repackaged, definitely revisited.

It is true that the story is even more complex than the original 1999 megahit, having more characters, bigger special effects, and more dead bodies walking around in unregenerated states. Still, watching it is a bit like having deja vu because it sticks pretty close to the original formula. The world is going to be threatened by a major force from the “underworld” unless the same people who accidentally unleashed it can stop it from happening.

In a convoluted twist, Imhotep (Arnold Vosloo, reprising his role), isn't the only dead guy threatening our heroes. He is brought back from the dead by a very foolish museum curator in order to do battle with the Scorpion King (WWF wrestler, The Rock) who is set to be resurrected in ten days. Legend has it that whoever defeats the Scorpion King will gain command over his army of undead, dog-like warriors and thus be able to rule the world.

The reason the Scorpion King is being resurrected? O'Connell, his wife Evie (Rachael Weisz, Enemy at the Gates), and their 9 year old son Alex (Freddie Boath, in his film debut) stumble across an ancient artifact and, as is their custom, completely disregard the “Warning: Curse Inside!” label engraved on the front.

In addition to the aforementioned actors, also on board for this second roller coaster of a ride are: John Hannah (The Hurricane) as Evie's brother Jonathan; Oded Fehr (Deuce Bigalow, Male Gigalo) as the Medji, Ardeth Bay; and Patricia Velazquez (Turn it Up) as Imhotep's main squeeze, Anck-Su-Namun. In a film where the dead are resurrected more times than John Travolta's career, it is a small matter that one or more of the characters in this sequel met their demise the first time out.

Also returning is director Stephen Sommers working from his own script which, like the 1999 version, ranks very high on action and CGI effects but quite low on originality. Sommers is successful in copying the style and substance of the first film. If you're a fan of one, you'll probably like both. If you didn't like the first one, best check your TV listing for alternative viewing options... or better yet, pick up that book you've been meaning to read because there's been nothing added to the sequel that will cause you to change your opinion.

Brendan Fraser has made himself quite at home in the persona that has been called a poor man's Indiana Jones. There's nothing poor about his performance. He plays the typical Saturday matinee movie hero, all swagger and style. Rachael Weisz and John Hanna seem to have been given some of O'Connell's heroic qualities as they both get an opportunity to save a day or two.

Where the original film banked heavily on the concept of resurrection from the dead, this sequel adds the just as erroneous premise of reincarnation. Evie, troubled by dreams and visions, comes to realize that in her past life, she was Nephertiti, daughter of the Pharoah and rival of Anck-Su-Namun.

And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment... Hebrews 9:27 (KJV)

Death, should our Lord tarry, will come unto all of us. But, praise God, only once. We have but one life to live on this earth which is why God exhorts us to "redeem the time." The fantasy of reincarnation is a devilish lie which can be traced back to the very beginning of mankind. The serpent deceived Eve by saying, "Ye shall not surely die." He was lying then. He is lying now.

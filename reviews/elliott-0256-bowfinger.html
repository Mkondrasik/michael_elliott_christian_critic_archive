Author-verified transcription

Bobby Bowfinger wants to make movies in the worse way... and like Ed Wood before him, that is exactly what he is going to do.

Bowfinger is an extremely funny inside look (exaggerated, of course) at the art of making movies and the strange and wonderful people who aspire to make them.

Steve Martin (The Out-Of-Towners) wrote the script and stars as Bobby Bowfinger, a rapidly aging would-be filmmaker who desperately needs to make his breakthrough film before he turns fifty, an age equivalent to death in Hollywood. "They can smell fifty!" he complains to his ragtag team of fellow losers and dreamers.

After reading a sci-fi script entitled "Chubby Rain," penned from the hand of his very own accountant, he realizes that his longtime quest of actually shooting his "signature" picture is just around the corner.

While hawking the script, he receives assurances that if action picture megastar Kit Ramsey (Eddie Murphy, Life) will do the film, he will have a "go" picture. Bowfinger decides to proceed with the filming... even after Kit turns him down by tossing him out of his car.

His backup plan requires the cast to approach Kit on the street and deliver their scripted lines while Bowfinger surreptitiously films Kit's response, which is generally abject fear and terror. When questioned, Bowfinger claims it to be an experimental method of moviemaking called "Cinema Nouveau."

If a B-movie cast is known for its amateurish overacting, the talent that Bowfinger assembles for "Chubby Rain" originates a bit further down the alphabet scale... like somewhere around the "Q" range.

Heather Graham (Austin Powers II) plays Daisy, an Ohio farm girl fresh off the bus who is determined to become a star even if it means sleeping with everyone on the set. Christine Baranski (Cybill) is Carol, the classic "diva without a cause." Adam Alexi-Malle, Jamie Kennedy, Kohl Sudduth and a truck full of Mexican wetbacks make up the rest of Bowfinger's motley cast and crew. Robert Downey Jr. (Chaplin) has some wonderful moments as a high level studio executive that inadvertently gives Bowfinger the green light for his movie.

But as talented as the supporting cast might be in portraying bad actors, Bowfinger would be just another mildly amusing look at a crazy industry if it were not for the brilliant work of Eddie Murphy. He treats us to not one but two outstanding personifications.

As the eccentric Kit Ramsey, Mr. Murphy is electric. Paranoid to a fault, Kit counts the number of K's in a script to make sure it isn't divisible by 3 (which would obviously indicate racist support for the KKK.) If it weren't for Mind Head (an obvious dig at Scientology) and its leader Terry Stricter (Terence Stamp, Star Wars 1), Kit would be unable to "keep it together." He would more than likely go off and follow through on his desire to flash the Laker girls.

As humorously edgy as Kit is, Mr. Murphy's portrayal of Jiff is a comic invention of genius proportions. Jiff is a dead ringer for Kit (if Kit wore coke bottle glasses, braces, and flipped burgers for a living). Mr. Murphy's endearing performance of this nerd gives the movie its heart and soul and most of its belly laughs. His on camera dash across a busy Los Angeles highway is a scream, made funnier when one recalls his casual stroll across a similar highway in Holy Man.

Director Frank Oz (In & Out) keeps the mood light and the humor (thankfully) non-raunchy. No pies are violated and no bodily fluids were used in the making of this picture.

Of course, regardless of the rich comedy fodder it provides, gaining anything by deceit and trickery is to lose far more than is ever possibly gained. A true winner in the game of life is one who can truthfully say in his final days "I have kept my integrity." Odds are that Bowfinger's comments will run more along the lines of:

"Gotcha sucker!"

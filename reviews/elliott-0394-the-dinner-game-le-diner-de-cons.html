The Dinner Game (Le Diner de cons)

Author-verified transcription

The idea behind The Dinner Game can be more clearly seen in the working title of the planned American version: Dinner for Schmucks. Based on that somewhat offensive title, I don't foresee the Americans rivaling any in the European community when it comes to exhibiting class. But then, this classic French farce doesn't particularly ooze sophistication.

Thierry Lhermitte (An American Werewolf in Paris) plays a well-to-do French publisher named Pierre Brochant who is a participant in a weekly dinner game. The object of the game is to bring to the Wednesday dinner, the best, most boorish or foolish idiot one can find. After spending the evening ridiculing the invited idiots (without their awareness, of course, that would be cruel), a grand winner is declared. This particular week, Brochant is especially excited about the dinner because he has stumbled across a "world-class idiot."

Rotund and jocular Jacques Villeret (Golden Boy) plays Francois Pignon, the idiot of Brochant's dreams, who spends his free time building intricate structures made completely from matchsticks. Among his sculptures are The Eiffel Tower, the Golden Gate bridge, and a crane which, he explains between convulsions of laughter, he calls Beau Derrick.

With an idiot like this, Brochant figures he's a shoo-in for the grand prize. But since this is a farce, you can be sure that things do not go as planned. After wrenching his back playing golf, Brochant must beg off dinner. Unfortunately Pignon does not get the message and shows up at his door. The rest of the movie deals with the adventures of this oddest of couples and the humorous devastation one man can bring.

The relationship between Messrs. Lhermitte and Villeret is absolutely priceless. Their comic timing is brilliant and the facial expressions they wear as disaster strikes time after time are side-splittingly funny. As the evening wears on, it is difficult to tell who is the bigger idiot.

Thierry Lhermitte's Brochant is a droll, upper class individual with a taste for the finer things of life. As his invited guest begins to make a shambles of his life, he is torn between utter disdain for the sheer idiocy of Pignon and his curious admiration for the nosebleed heights of idiocy Pignon attains.

Jacques Villeret adroitly avoids making Pignon a caricatured object of either pity or ridicule. He gives his character a delightful presence, a bittersweet emotional life, and an understandable desire to be accepted by these new acquaintances.

Francis Veber has a history of adapting his French screenplays into American ones (La Cage Aux Folles, Father's Day, The Man With One Red Shoe). Time will tell if his planned American version is able to match the hilarity of this French original.

We do recognize that the premise of the movie is insulting and condescending. That anyone could feel so superior as to judge another as being foolish or idiotic due to their hobbies or interests speaks more to the one doing the judging rather than to the one being judged. This, of course, is the point of the film.

Someone can be unlearned and yet exhibit more wisdom and knowledge than the highest educated scholars of the land. In Acts 4, the religious leaders were duly impressed with the apostles Peter and John even though they recognized that these men were ignorant and uneducated.

According to scriptures, to call someone a fool means that the individual in question has turned away from the knowledge of God. It has nothing to do with IQ's, natural ability, or brain power.

Scriptures say:

The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God. Psalms 53:1a (KJV)

The denial of the power and existence of God is what defines a fool. Nothing else. Pignon is no more a fool or idiot than Brochant. True, Brochant is more skilled in interpersonal relations and social graces. But such things can be learned. What Pignon demonstrates (a larger and more giving heart) is something that some people will always lack.

Antitrust

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 12, 2001
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Matthew 10:8b2 Timothy 2:2Acts 4:20

Sermon / spiritual topic: Sharing God's Word; Knowledge; Information; Giving; Stewardship

Author-verified transcription

He who controls information controls the world. Such is the premise from which the high-tech thriller Antitrust is built. Borrowing from the headlines of today's newspapers, the filmmakers have taken the antitrust ruling against Microsoft and exaggerated it into a cyber-geek's paranoid fantasy.

In Antitrust, Bill Gates is rather thinly represented (in an unflattering light) by the character of Gary Winston (Tim Robbins, Mission to Mars), a self-described genius who helms the massive international computer corporation known as NURV (Never Underestimate Radical Vision). Winston, an appealing, Pringles-eating, hands-on managerial type has succeeded in his harshly competitive field by hiring the best and brightest programmers and giving them free rein to accomplish their tasks, inspiring their "creativity" with only the open-ended admonition, "Surprise me." Of course, if that fails, he can always fall back on theft and murder to get to the same results.

His answer to the public's concern regarding his unfair business practices and the stifling of any competition is his rather prophetic observation that a kid in a garage with a computer modem and a good idea could put him out of business overnight. Milo Hoffmann (Ryan Phillippe, The Way of the Gun) is such a kid.

Winston hires Milo to help him put the finishing touches on his newest multi-billion dollar project. Synapse is a software program which enables all electronic devices to be capable of picking up a satellite feed thus converging them into a unified, global communication network. Such a discovery would revolutionize the world and Milo, unable to resist the temptation to be a part of it, sets aside his own tech company startup plans to climb on board with NURV.

Once in Winston's employ, he begins to suspect that things are not what they appear. His suspicions lead to discoveries which put his very life in jeopardy.

Surprisingly enough, director Peter Howitt (Sliding Doors) almost pulled it off. With decent pacing, a good cast, and a promising setup, Antitrust had the potential to be a white knuckle thriller combining corporate espionage with the highly competitive field of digital technology. But alas, it was not to be. The film eventually disintegrates into a laughable mess as the kid from the garage brings the multi-billionaire from Seattle to his knees with a few clicks of the keyboard. If only the script were as intelligent as the characters in it.

Ryan Phillippe plays cybergenius Milo with a grave import, staring intently at the code scrolling down his computer screen. Thankfully though, when action is called for, he rises to the challenge, typing with a ferocity which hasn't been seen since Rosemary Wood left the White House.

Tim Robbins' Gary Winston is a megalomaniac in a cardigan sweater, an odd combination which somehow results in an image not unlike that which would occur if Mr. Rogers started to channel Wall Street's Gordon Gecco.

Claire Forlani (Boys and Girls) and Rachael Leigh Cook (Get Carter) play Milo's love interests and are serviceable in their limited roles.

A major contention between Milo and Winston is that of open source coding. Milo, a child of the Internet, believes that information should be made freely available to whoever can use it. Winston would horde whatever information he could if it would bring him profits.

Without being drawn into the debate revolving around the legitimacy of intellectual property claims, we can certainly draw our own comparisons when it comes to God's Word.

Freely ye have received, freely give. Matthew 10:8b (KJV)

Our God, Who is all knowing, wants nothing less than for all men to come unto a knowledge of His truth. Withholding God's Word from someone who is in need because of some self-serving interest is anathema to any man or woman of God. Certainly such an individual would not be worthy of our trust. Just the opposite... They'd only be worthy of our "antitrust."

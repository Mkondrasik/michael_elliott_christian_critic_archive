At First Sight

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 15, 1999
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Ephesians 1:18 John 9:39 2 Peter 1:4-9

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Spiritual sight; understanding; spiritual growth; teaching and training

Author-verified transcription

It must be a frightening thing... to suddenly discover a sense that for all intents and purposes was not there before. How would we know what it was we were experiencing?

At First Sight examines such a question by looking at the life of Virgil Adamson (Val Kilmer, The Saint). Totally blind since the age of three, Virgil has adapted quite well to living without sight in a visual world. Cocooned in a small town under the watchful care of his older sister, Jennie (Kelly McGillis, Witness), Virgil earns his living as a massage therapist at a nearby spa resort.

Into his life comes big city architect Amy Benic (Mira Sorvino, Mimic). Literally dissolving at his touch, Amy’s view of the world becomes enriched as Virgil teaches her how to “see” things as he does. Naturally, she wants to return the favor.

She discovers that a doctor (Bruce Davison, Apt Pupil) has perfected a new optical surgery that may work on Virgil’s condition. However, nobody is prepared for the resulting effects of that surgery. Virgil can suddenly see, but his brain lacks the associative skills needed to process the new stimuli. In other words, his eyes work, but in some ways he is more blind and dependent upon others than he ever was before.

Mr. Kilmer is very convincing as a self-assured blind man transformed into a man who can see but does not know what he sees. Miss Sorvino is adorable as the woman who inspired him to leave his “comfort zone” of blindness to move into the unknown world of sight. Miss McGillis does a splendid job as the sister who sacrificed her life and happiness to raise and protect her brother.

Newcomer Steve Levitt has written a script (based on a story by Dr. Oliver Sacks) which takes us on a roller coaster ride of built up hopes and dashed expectations. It’s formulaic and overly metaphorical, and sometimes the plot devices get a bit heavy handed (as in the unneeded scenes between Virgil and his estranged father), but director Irwin Winkler (The Juror) keeps the film from sinking to the level of a “disease of the week” television drama by keeping our attention properly focused on the relationships between the three main characters.

Of course, sight (and the lack thereof) is also a recurring metaphor in the Word of God:

Having the understanding darkened, being alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them, because of the blindness of their heart: Ephesians 4:18 [KJV]
But he that lacketh these things is blind, and cannot see afar off, and hath forgotten that he was purged from his old sins. 2 Peter 1:9 [KJV]
The eyes of your understanding being enlightened; that ye may know what is the hope of his calling, and what the riches of the glory of his inheritance in the saints, Ephesians 1:18 [KJV]

In these verses (and others) sight is symbolically linked to one’s spiritual awareness. There is a spiritual blindness that exists. A blindness that does not necessarily end when one becomes born again (a mistake often made by many evangelical outfits). To see spiritually means to have the understanding that comes from receiving and applying the knowledge of God. To do that requires teaching and training. A new born-again believer must learn how to utilize all the spiritual abilities that God makes available to us via holy spirit just as Virgil had to retrain himself how to function with his newfound ability.

It isn’t easy and it doesn’t happen overnight. It is also nearly impossible to do without support. But as Virgil disciplined his mind to accept and recognize the images his healed eyes were sending him, so can we learn to accept and recognize the things of the spirit. It takes time, a willingness to learn, and someone to teach us. But it is well worth the effort it takes to leave the comfort zone of the “senses walk” with which we are so familiar, and move to the higher plane of the “spiritual walk” which is where our heavenly Father wants us.

- Michael Elliott -

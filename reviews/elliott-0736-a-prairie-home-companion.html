Author-verified transcription

Director Robert Altman turns to National Public Radio for source material in his latest big-name ensemble film. A Prairie Home Companion is described in the film as the kind of radio variety show that died fifty years ago - only nobody ever told them.

Led by the mellifluous-voiced Garrison Keillor (who plays himself), the PHC show is a blend of satire and music and Altman's film is a sort of hit and miss tribute not only to this public radio staple, but also to the inevitability of death or passage.

There's no plot per se - only the premise that this little radio show is giving its farewell performance because its new owner has pulled the plug. Rather than say their good-byes or thank the listening public for keeping them on the air, these performers handle the situation with typical Minnesotan flair. They adopt a determined resignation and simply put on another show - one that the audience doesn't realize will be its last.

Altman films the "live" radio broadcast with a laid-back, natural ease, weaving his camera in and out, capturing not only the stage production but also the backstage drama/comedy that is taking place behind the scenes.

He has also assembled a wide array of talent for this small, independently produced movie. The cast member who can't help but stand head and shoulders above the rest is Meryl Streep who, as Yolanda Johnson - songstress extraordinaire, gives further proof that those who think her to be the world's greatest living actress might not be wrong. She is amazing - a pure and unadulterated delight to watch.

She's paired with Lily Tomlin as her sister Rhonda and the overlapping dialogues they share are as musically precise as the "old-timey" inspirational songs they sing. Lindsay Lohan plays Yolanda's depressed poet of a daughter, Lola, and is given a chance to belt out an odd rendition of Frankie and Johnny.

A few of Garrison Keillor's radio characters are fleshed out for the film. Kevin Kline plays Guy Noir, a clumsy private eye who now works security for the radio station. It's a bungled use of the character and Kline, good as he is, can't make it work in the context of the film. John C. Reilly and Woody Harrelson have better luck playing a pair of musical cowboys, Dusty and Lefty. Their show-stopping number "Bad Jokes" runs a fine line along the edge of decency for family radio.

Oscar winner Tommy Lee Jones is pretty much wasted as the Axeman who has come to watch the show before he closes it down for good. Oscar nominated Virginia Madsen finds herself in muddied waters as an unnamed "dangerous woman." Clad in a white trench coat, she floats around backstage offering comfort and philosophical advice to those that see her. It is a poorly written role that never really works.

Clearly Madsen is to represent an angel of death and death does become a dominant theme throughout the movie although it isn't clear what the 81-year old Altman wants to say about it. The message being delivered is that death is inevitable and it happens to all so there isn't anything we can do about it except accept it. It's not what we'd call breaking news. Death has been a part of life ever since the days of Adam and Eve.

Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned. Romans 5:12 (KJV)

But there is good news. Even though we may have to experience it, death is no longer a permanent condition. Death entered this world by one man's sin and it was defeated by one man's sacrifice. As we believe on him, death no longer has a hold on us.

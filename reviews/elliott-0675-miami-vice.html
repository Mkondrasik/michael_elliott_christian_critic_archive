Author-verified transcription

Another television icon is brought out of the attic, dusted off, repainted and put back out on the marketing shelf for a new generation of buyers. Miami Vice was the hippest thing on network TV in the mid to late 80s making co-stars Don Johnson and Philip Michael Thomas household names as the pastel clad cops Crockett and Tubbs.

Johnson and Thomas are replaced by Colin Farrell and Jamie Foxx but comparisons between them don't easily come. This is not an "actors' showcase." The latter pair portrays the characters as well as could be expected but the focus isn't really on them. This movie is not about storytelling in its purest sense - it is about capturing a look and feel. Style is the substance. In this context, it is the director who happens to be king.

The meager story is as simplistic as the film's TV origins might suggest. Miami cops Crockett and Tubbs are drawn into a federal investigation that is tainted with insider leaks. They agree to pose as drug runners in order to infiltrate a major crime syndicate.

While engaged in their police work, both Crockett and Tubbs allow things to get personal. Tubbs (Jamie Foxx) gets "busy" with a fellow police officer (Naomie Harris) and Crockett (Colin Farrell) gives his heart to the girlfriend/partner (Gong Li) of the druglord.

Things eventually get ugly and the last 20-30 minutes are filled with automatic weapon fire in a chaotic gangland shootout - the kind where it's difficult to see who's shooting whom what with all the lead in the air.

Director Michael Mann does manage to resurrect the 80s series with a certain degree of success. His film has a gritty realism, a great soundtrack and, despite my earlier comments, there is something of a story told with a strong visual style. It just fails to hook us emotionally. As a result we sit there appreciating what we see but disappointed that we're not feeling more. And at a running time that extends beyond the 2 hour mark, a mere visual style isn't enough to justify the time we must invest to sit through it.

Both Farrell and Foxx adopt the pained look that Johnson and Thomas used whenever they wanted to show that they were serious cops who were not afraid to let their feelings show. Gong Li is attractive but has some trouble making her English understood. She fits right in with this picture. There's so much abbreviated cop-speak and gangster lingo that much of the dialogue sails right past us without slowing down for a translation.

Crockett is the one treading a very dangerous line. Become personally involved with the enemy who he was sent to infiltrate and oppose crosses a line that shouldn't be crossed. As a character in another movie once said, "When you dance with the devil, the devil doesn't change. The devil changes you." It is a warning that has been given to man since the beginning.

And I would not that ye should have fellowship with devils. Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils: ye cannot be partakers of the Lord's table, and of the table of devils. 1 Corinthians 10:20b-21 (KJV)

All men have a choice to make in this world. Do they live in a spiritual darkness or do they stay in the light of God's love? It is our choice and our responsibility to make that choice every moment of every day. Trying to live in both worlds is impossible. It leads only to a spiritual and moral decline from which it is dangerously difficult to recover.

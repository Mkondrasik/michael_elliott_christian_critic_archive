Author-verified transcription

Talk about your product placement! Harold and Kumar Go to White Castle builds a movie around a particular hamburger - one described as being "the perfect food." While I'm sure that delights the owners of White Castle, the ones making that proclamation are stoned out of their minds so it might be questionable whether this is an advertising ploy that will ultimately increase sales.

Though crude and offensive at least Harold and Kumar Go to White Castle is an equal opportunity offender. Asians, Indians, females, blacks, Christians, skateboarders, Southerners, cops... all groups will find themselves as the butt of more than one crude and politically incorrect joke before the film comes to an end.

Keeping in line with most every "stoner film" that came before it, the plot of Harold and Kumar is inanely simple. Harold (John Cho), an Asian investment banker and Kumar (Kal Penn), an Indian pre-med slacker smoke marijuana and get a craving for some White Castle burgers and fries. And so, without a second thought (as if they were capable of forming one) they jump in Kumar's car and head off into the night in search of the only thing that will satisfy them.

Naturally, they have trouble finding the fast food restaurant and it is the adventures they have along the way that delivers the crude and sexual humor that will never be mistaken for high art or sharp wit. But then, with this title who would be expecting that?

Directed by the man who gave us Dude, Where's My Car? (and who shamelessly references the title during the course of this film) Harold and Kumar caters to the fans of the lowest form of humor imaginable. If gratuitous nudity, festering boils, racist/prejudicial references, and lame drug-related humor manage to tickle your funny bone... then by all means, drag yourself to the multiplex and take along an extra tub of popcorn in case a food fight breaks out among your fellow filmgoers.

The stars of the film are hardly household names. Early ads proudly proclaimed them as "the Asian guy from American Pie" and "the Indian guy from Van Wilder." Not what I would call the greatest box office recommendations. There are a number of more familiar names in cameo or secondary roles. They include Anthony Anderson as a burger joint drive-thru clerk, Ryan Reynolds as a male nurse, Fred Willard as a med school administrator, and Neil Patrick Harris playing himself in a bizarre role that should forever erase any wholesome memories we might have retained for the guy who once played Doogie Howser, MD.

It is difficult to give this movie any kind of serious consideration. The filmmakers do try to tack on a pseudo life lesson - something about the universe unfolding as it must and that we should have the courage to stand up for ourselves and what we believe in. Unfortunately, all these lead characters believe in are mind-altering pharmaceuticals and promiscuous sex.

Despite its phony, feel-good ending, when Harold and Kumar Go to White Castle they offer no good reason for us to accompany them. There's sure to be a more substantial and rewarding movie diet to be found elsewhere in the multiplex.

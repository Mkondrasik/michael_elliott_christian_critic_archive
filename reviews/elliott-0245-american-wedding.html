American Wedding

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 1, 2003
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 36 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

John 3:161 John 5:31 Corinthians 13:4-7

Sermon / spiritual topic: Love; Selfless Action; Obedience; Marriage; Moral Choices

Author-verified transcription

Who knew that a story about a group of over sexed, immature high school students would spawn a successful Hollywood franchise resulting in three major motion pictures? American Wedding completes the American Pie trilogy in the same crude and embarrassingly sexual manner as its predecessors.

Jim (Jason Biggs) finally works up the courage to ask for Michelle's (Alyson Hannigan) hand in marriage. The fact that it happens in a restaurant with her under the table servicing him orally when his dad (Eugene Levy) walks in is just a hint at the sexual hijinks to come.

Michelle, of course, wants the perfect wedding. Jim, who has never done anything perfectly in his life, wants to give it to her. And so he jumps through many hoops to make it happen. And he drags his friends along with him.

This being an American Pie movie, those hoops include visiting a gay bar and engaging in a "dance battle," shaving the hair off one's private area, and distracting Michelle's straight-laced parents long enough to get the bachelor party strippers out of the house unseen.

Added to that is a contest between Stifler (Seann William Scott) and Finch (Eddie Kaye Thomas) to see who can score with Michelle's younger sister, Cadence (January Jones). The twist is that because they each overheard her say something different, they adopt a personality they think she's looking for. The crude and sexually obsessed Stifler puts on a yuppie, puppy dog persona while the cultured and intellectual Finch acts the crude and dangerous bad boy.

The cast is well familiar with their characters and they portray them with considerable ease. Whether good or bad, director Jesse Dylan has captured what has made the previous two films so popular. If you liked what came before, odds are that you'll enjoy this one as well.

In addition to the regular cast (of which Jason Biggs and Eugene Levy continue to be stand outs), the film is benefited by the addition of Fred Willard and Deborah Rush as Michelle's parents as well as January Jones as the film's object of desire.

The film is rated R and for good reason. The sexual language and references are explicit. The nudity is gratuitous. The crudity is excessive. If such content offends, you're in the wrong theater because there's no small amount of the aforementioned.

As with the earlier films, there's a tacked on moral message which, I suppose, is intended to excuse the 90 minutes of inexcusable R-rated content. In this instance the message has to do with love and how it is not defined by how we feel but rather by what we do. To our shock, this foul mouthed, sex-happy film actually got this one element right. Love should not be mistaken to be a "feeling" but rather a conscious, selfless act.

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. John 3:16 (KJV)

Because God loved us to the point of saving us at the cost of His own son, we have the opportunity to love Him back. Not through a vague or nebulous feeling but via deliberate decision and thoughtful action.

For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous. 1 John 5:3 (KJV)

If we truly love God, then we should choose to shape our lives after His pattern. It is an example that, moral message aside, this film does not set.

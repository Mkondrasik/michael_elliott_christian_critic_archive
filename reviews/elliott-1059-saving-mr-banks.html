Saving Mr. Banks

Sermon / spiritual topic: Trust; Peace; Faithfulness; Letting Go

Author-verified transcription

The stories that tell the stories behind the familiar tales we all know are sometimes the most interesting ones to hear. Such is the case with Saving Mr. Banks, a look at the behind-the-scenes efforts to bring the iconic Mary Poppins to the big screen. Apparently, it took a lot more than a spoonful of sugar.

Mary Poppins was a popular book character sprung from the mind of author P.L. Travers. For twenty years Walt Disney tried to get the movie rights from her and for twenty years she resisted. When her financial situation became untenable she allowed herself to be cajoled by the Disney machine and came to L.A. in their hopes that she might be persuaded to sign over her rights to the character. But for Travers, the central issue was never about the money.

The highly opinionated and never satisfied Travers (Emma Thompson) inserted her willful insistence in every aspect of the production, much to the frustration of Walt Disney (Tom Hanks) and his talented team. No music; no animation; no Dick van Dyke; no made up words; no color red; these were just a few items among the endless stream of demands that she continually presented. That the film was made at all is quite remarkable. It took the patient tenacity of Walt Disney who finally figured out what was behind her resistance.

Emma Thompson gives a deliciously arch performance as the buttoned up English lady who created the world’s most beloved nanny who was, by all accounts, practically perfect in every way. Her throwaway disdain at the silliness she encounters upon arriving in L.A. is quite amusing and the genuine horror and desperate fear that her characters would be somehow wronged in the hands of the Americans is very deeply felt.

Throughout the film, the narrative occasionally jumps to scenes depicting Travers as a young girl growing up in Australia with a fun-spirited and free-wheeling father (played by Colin Farrell in his best performance to date). As more of her back story is revealed to us, we begin to see behind the mask she has grown to wear. Thompson plays it perfectly.

Saving Mr. Banks is a very simple story with a very large heart. Travers has invested much into her characters. They are more than a storyteller’s invention. They are representative of her own family history and, as such, offer her comfort, redemption, forgiveness, and a sort of closure. To taint or tarnish that by turning them into part of a frivolous entertainment piece developed by a cartoon factory is anathema to her.

The main issue that has been blocking a cinematic version of Travers’ vision from reaching audiences is her lack of trust that those responsible for the adaptation will stay true to the central core of her story and its characters. Most of all, she is concerned for the character of Mr. Banks. Trust, when it is lacking, is something impossible to manufacture. When lost, it is nearly impossible to recapture. In this world that so often disappoints, trust is becoming a rare thing indeed.

However, when we can find someone or something in whom or in which we can trust with our whole heart, an indescribable sense of freedom washes over us. Suddenly, we are not alone. There is someone who we can count on; something we can believe in; and that knowledge alone provides us with immeasurable strength and power.

“Thou wilt keep [him] in perfect peace, [whose] mind [is] stayed [on thee]: because he trusteth in thee.” Isaiah 26:3-4 (KJV)

There are very few things in this life that can compete with perfect peace. And we have the means to attain it simply by placing our trust in the right place.

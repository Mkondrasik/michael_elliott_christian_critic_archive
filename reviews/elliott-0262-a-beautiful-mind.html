A Beautiful Mind

Author-verified transcription

"A mind is a terrible thing to waste," the Negro College Fund reminds us. Dan Quayle's version, "What a waste it is to lose one's mind" is also oddly appropriate. Both have a certain application to Ron Howard's new film A Beautiful Mind.

Based upon the life of the Nobel Prize winning mathematician John Nash, A Beautiful Mind takes us into the intimate world of the intelligentsia, as Princeton students vie for postgraduate placement by developing new and original ideas or theories. Such is the obsession of John Nash (Russell Crowe, Gladiator) who forgoes the mundane environment of the classroom in exchange for a flock of pigeons as he looks for patterns in their random movements.

He eventually stumbles upon his "original idea" thanks to an attractive blonde in a bar. He develops a novel view of game theory which contradicts 150 years of traditional thought. Armed by this one new idea, Nash lands a highly coveted research and teaching position at MIT. The government, represented by William Parcher (Ed Harris, Pollock), soon takes an interest in Nash's uncanny ability to read and break code. The government puts Nash to work, having him pour over newspapers and magazines, looking for codes and conspiracies that may exist hidden between the lines of innocuous newsprint.

As time goes on, Nash's mental condition worsens. Paranoia sets in and delusional episodes turn reality into a guessing game. As the spy games intensify so does Nash's mental instability, making us question just how much of what we are seeing through his eyes is real.

How Nash treats his illness to allow him to cope in this world is the single thread that ties this film together. It is a thin thread indeed. Nash readily admits that he doesn't like people very much and, consequently, people don't much like him. Crowe does an amazing job in portraying Nash's peculiarities but he cannot quite manage to make us care about his character's plight. For 95% of the film, we don't much like him either.

Jennifer Connelly (Requiem for a Dream), as the beautiful coed that wins Nash's heart only to have to deal with his deteriorating mind, connects much more strongly with the audience. Her love and commitment to this brilliant but flawed man is the heart of this film and Connelly keeps it beating strongly throughout.

As Nash's roommate and only friend, Paul Bettany (A Knight's Tale) adds a touch of lightness and humor to what could otherwise have been a dull and dreary march to the shores of insanity and back.

Director Howard (Apollo 13) never gets the pacing of the film beyond a slow trot. Nash may be a fascinating character and his story may indeed be interesting... but, as filmed, it simply isn't compelling enough to arrest our attention.

The mind can be a very tricky thing. It allows us to perform highly complex calculations, devise and design great works, and study or meditate on things as enormously important as God's Word.

The mind can also deceive us and lead us to believe something is true when it is not. This is why the final arbiter of truth is not what we think is true, but rather what God says is true.

Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 Thessalonians 5:21 (KJV)

The proving comes from using the Bible, which was given by revelation from God, as a filter through which all information, stimuli, and reasoning is passed. By having a standard of truth outside of our own minds - one that has proven itself faithful - we build for ourselves a solid foundation from which to make sound decisions for our lives.

On a secular level, it is akin to what John Nash had to do for himself. Whenever we trust our own mind (opinions, feelings, rationalizations, etc.) to be the sole authority for our actions, we will inevitably be led to trouble. God gave us his Word so that we would follow it.

Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. Proverbs 3:5 (KJV)

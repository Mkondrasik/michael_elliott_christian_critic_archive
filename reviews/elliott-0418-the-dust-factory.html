The Dust Factory

Author-verified transcription

Many of us were sitting in a movie theater or in front of a TV screen the first time we felt touched by the reality of death. It might have been Bambi's mom, Simba's dad, or Nemo's siblings but the first death that touched us emotionally was most likely the death of a movie character.

In The Dust Factory writer/director Eric Small takes death from being a peripheral event in a story and moves it center stage where it becomes the sole focus of the story. In doing so, he presents a family film that provides parents an opportunity to initiate a conversation with their children regarding this quite sensitive subject.

Ryan Flynn (Ryan Kelley) is a young teen who has not uttered a word since he saw his father die in a train accident six years earlier. In that respect, he's much like his grandfather (Armin Mueller-Stahl) who, suffering from Alzheimer's, sits coma-like in the house unable to respond to outside stimuli.

When Ryan falls from a rickety bridge and plunges into the lake, he emerges to find a different reality. Most everyone has gone. His house is empty except for his grandfather who, he is surprised to find, is walking and talking. In fact, he's the most lucid grandfather a kid could ever want. Even more surprisingly, Ryan finds himself talking as well.

We begin learning the parameters of this new reality when Ryan meets Melanie (Hayden Panettiere). She's been there the longest and is more than happy to show Ryan the rules of what she calls the dust factory.

She takes him to a circus big top and they watch as people attempt the "triple flip," a trapeze maneuver that has only two possible outcomes. If the person misses, he plunges into the dust (called "going home"). If he completes the maneuver, he simply vanishes, having successfully "moved on." Oddly enough, both results receive appreciative applause from the attending audience.

Ryan, Melanie and Grandpa are all stuck in the dust factory because they can't bring themselves to "take the leap." Each of the characters has his own reason for "dawdling" in this state of in-between. What is clear is that each has a choice to make and that each one must decide when he is ready to either go home or move on.

Perhaps it is because of the themes and symbolism used in the film but The Dust Factory has the feel of being an adaptation of a classic children's story. It is a tribute to Small whose original screenplay and directorial vision combine to build a familiarity within a unique approach to a timeless subject matter.

The acting is good, notably from Ryan Kelley (Mean Creek) as the young protagonist, and the content is acceptable for all audiences.

The bridge that exists between life and death has long inspired artists, writers, and filmmakers. The Dust Factory explores how death is not something to be feared. It views death simply as another step to take on the road of one's existence.

The apostle Paul wrestled with the notion of choosing life over death, not from a sense of fear but rather from a sense of obligation.

"For to me, to live is Christ and to die is gain. If I am to go on living in the body, this will mean fruitful labor for me. Yet what shall I choose? I do not know! I am torn between the two: I desire to depart and be with Christ, which is better by far; but it is more necessary for you that I remain in the body:" Philippians 1:21-24

Should our Lord tarry, there will come a time for each of us when our bodies will no longer sufficiently sustain human life. Death will come to us all but we need not fear it for it is not a permanent destination for us. There is life waiting for us on the other side of that bridge.

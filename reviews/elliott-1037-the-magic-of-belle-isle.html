The Magic of Belle Isle

Author-verified transcription

Rob Reiner’s film, The Magic of Belle Isle, lives up to its name. It is a simple tale of loss and loneliness but so filled with warmth that the aura of redemptive hope infuses every frame. It is an almost magical experience that will leave audiences with a feel good sensation.

Monte Wildhorn (Morgan Freeman) is a noted author of western novels whose writing days are long behind him. Widowed, wheelchair bound, and drained of inspiration, he spends most of his days losing himself at the bottom of a whiskey bottle. He has reluctantly come to spend his summer at the lakeside community of Belle Isle at the bequest of his nephew (Kenan Thompson). The truth is… he’s really got no place else to go.

Next door to his rented house is a divorced mother of three girls and the unusual friendship that forms between them begins to melt the embittered heart of the grizzled wordsmith. Soon, Monte is a welcomed member of the community and rediscovers the truth that life can contain more than pain and emptiness.

Morgan Freeman wears his humanity well as the author who doesn’t really want to be left alone, despite his protestations. The connection he makes with his young sibling neighbors is quite endearing. Emma Fuhrmann as the middle daughter who pays Monte to teach her how to make up stories is especially noteworthy. Virginia Madsen does a passable job as the piano playing next-door mom who serves as Monte’s new muse.

Reiner and screenwriter Guy Thomas use a formulaic template to craft their story and, for the most part, manage to color within the lines. Their only misstep is the inclusion of a developmentally challenged youth whom Monte befriends. While this character helps to progress the tearing down of Monte’s defensive walls, the stereotypical portrayal of this “special” child is indelicate and may be offensive to some.

The value of community can never be overstated. Monte has basically shut down emotionally, abandoning the life he once embraced in favor of an isolated and anti-social existence. It takes the community of Belle Isle, with its small town gentilities, to make Monte want to engage with the real world.

Because the community welcomed Monte into its folds, Monte began to believe that he too had something to contribute. Indeed, he began to extend himself to utilize the narrative gifts he had developed over time to bless the lives of not only the trio of girls living next door but to others in the community.

“And above all things have fervent charity among yourselves: for charity shall cover the multitude of sins. Use hospitality one to another without grudging. As every man hath received the gift, even so minister the same one to another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God.” 1 Peter 4:8-10

As the community manifested its hospitality towards Monte, Monte began to reciprocate, healing himself in the process and then adding to the lives of others as he reached out to them with his unique storytelling abilities.

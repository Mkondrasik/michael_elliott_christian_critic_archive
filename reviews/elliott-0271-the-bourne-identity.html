Author-verified transcription

The title character of The Bourne Identity is the ultimate secret agent. He is, in fact, so secret that even he doesn't know who he is. Based upon Robert Ludlum's wildly successful spy novel, The Bourne Identity begins with a simple premise: Jason Bourne, a top undercover operative (Matt Damon, The Talented Mr. Ripley), suffers from amnesia and can't remember his name or what he is doing in Europe.

From there, things get progressively more complicated. First off, people keep trying to kill him. Why? He can't remember. He doesn't even know if he's one of the good guys or one of the bad. All he knows is that two weeks ago he was found floating in the Mediterranean with two bullets lodged in his back and a Swiss bank account number surgically implanted in his hip. With those clues, he quickly determines that he's no Yugo salesman from Dubuque.

After thwarting an attack against him in Switzerland, he meets and convinces a total stranger (Franka Potente, Run Lola Run) to drive him to Paris, where he believes he once lived. Being trailed by lethal parties unknown to them, they must try to outsmart, outrun, and outmaneuver their adversaries who are committed to preventing Bourne from revealing secrets which, unbeknownst to them, he can't remember.

Fortunately for Bourne, though his identity is forgotten, his skills and instincts are not. He'll need them if he's to have any hope of escaping the worldwide team of assassins who have been activated with the sole purpose of making him disappear... permanently.

To date, Ludlum's best-selling novels have not had great success in their translation to the large screen but The Bourne Identity holds up as a credible and suspenseful spy thriller. This is largely due to the taut direction of Doug Liman (Go), the crisp editing of Saar Klein (Almost Famous), and the solid performance of Matt Damon. There is a weakness to the film and it lies within the screenplay from scribes Tony Gilroy (Proof of Life) and William Blake Herron (A Texas Funeral). Their treatment contains a fair number of plot holes and leaves a number of questions unanswered.

Such inconsistencies however, are only momentarily frustrating and, as we choose to ignore them, the engrossing situation which the characters face draws us back into the story.

Damon excels as a "malfunctioning thirty million dollar weapon." He effectively plays his amnesia against his growing awareness of his obviously highly trained habitual responses. He is also totally believable in the scenes where he must demonstrate Bourne's martial arts prowess.

The supporting cast is highly accomplished, more talented even than the rigors of their roles demand. In addition to Potente as the love interest, we are treated to seeing Chris Cooper (American Beauty) and Brian Cox (The Rookie) as a pair of conspiring government officials; Julia Stiles (Save the Last Dance) as an efficient if somewhat reluctant field conduit; and Clive Owen (Greenfingers) as the assassin hired to make the Bourne problem nothing more than a fleeting memory.

Memory can be a tricky or deceptive thing. Although Bourne has no recollection of his identity or his past, his body and mind still retain the habitual patterns ingrained by years of intense training, practice and study. Such is the way that God has designed our physical frame. It is also the reason why He so often exhorts us to think, read, speak, and do His Word.

This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night, that thou mayest observe to do according to all that is written therein: for then thou shalt make thy way prosperous, and then thou shalt have good success. Joshua 1:8 (KJV)

The more we think about the truths contained in the Bible, the more we will see how those truths apply to our everyday life. As we then act upon those truths, we evidence the resulting benefits. Through repeated practice, we can make "doing the Word" a habit in our lives. It is a habit we need never break.

Author-verified transcription

“Will God forgive us for what we’ve done?” asks the central character early on in the new film, Man on Fire. The answer he was given is “no.” The truth is that God is more than willing to forgive but before one can receive forgiveness, one must first repent. At this stage in the film, the character believes that he is beyond both.

Denzel Washington stars as Creasy, an ex-military counter-terrorist who suppressed foreign threats through his assassination skills. When an undisclosed tragedy in his past causes him to fall apart and sink into alcoholism, a past associate named Rayburn (Christopher Walken) offers him an opportunity to pull himself out of his downward spiral.

We meet Creasy as he travels to Mexico to meet with Rayburn. As the film explains, because of the high rate of kidnapping taking place south of the border, most of the affluent citizens have hired bodyguards to protect their families. Rayburn has suggested Creasy to a family who is in need of an inexpensive bodyguard to watch over their young daughter Pita (Dakota Fanning). Because he’s a drunk, Creasy comes cheap.

Creasy accepts the position but approaches his new job with a reserved and distant air. Because of his past (and present) problems, he has difficulty opening himself emotionally to other people. Nevertheless, a persistent Pita manages to worm her way into his heart, giving Creasy reason to believe that the possibility of love is not completely lost. When a kidnapping attempt on Pita ends with Creasy’s failure to protect her, he declares a personal war upon the organized crime system and corrupt Mexican officials who sponsor or tolerate this form of terrorism.

Tony Scott (Spy Game) directs the screenplay written by Brian Helgeland (Mystic River) using a number of stylistic touches - quick cuts, blurred shots, subtitles of different fonts and sizes... all of which are rather needless. The performances and the story are involving enough without the gimmicks.

Denzel Washington plays a man well accustomed with violence and death. His nonchalance as he enacts his vengeance is rather chilling. And yet he does allow us to see behind the facade - first during his early scenes with the bottle and then later as Pita and Creasy form their emotional ties. It is another worthy performance for an actor whose filmography is already quite impressive.

Dakota Fanning, whose precociousness was turned loose without restraint in her last two movies (Uptown Girls and Cat in the Hat), is much more genuine here. She and Denzel form a warm and believable relationship.

Christopher Walken, in a small role, is always enjoyable to watch. Radha Mitchell, as Pita’s mother, also does a fine job in a difficult role that would have been all too easy to push over the top.

A word of caution - the violence is extreme. As Creasy tracks his way through the henchmen to reach the top man of the kidnapping cartel, he follows through with his vow to kill everyone and anyone who were involved or knew about Pita’s abduction.

He functions deliberately, systematically, and effectively. As Rayburn says about him: “A man can be an artist in anything if he’s good enough. Creasy’s art is death.”

That’s why Creasy questions whether God would forgive him for his actions... because he has been responsible for so much death over his lifetime. But God would have us know that no one is beyond salvation - no one is beyond redemption. No one was more aware of this truth than the apostle Paul.

This is a faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptation, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief. Howbeit for this cause I obtained mercy, that in me first Jesus Christ might shew forth all longsuffering, for a pattern to them which should hereafter believe on him to life everlasting. 1 Timothy 1:15-16 (KJV)

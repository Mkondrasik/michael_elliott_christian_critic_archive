Author-verified transcription

"Money is going to ruin sports." These prophetic words were spoken by sportswriter O.B. Keeler in the new film, Bobby Jones: Stroke of Genius. Keeler (Malcolm McDowell) was among the first to recognize the potential of brilliance in a young 14-year-old golf phenomenon known as Bobby Jones.

James Caviezel stars in this wholesome story whose hero is not someone with superhuman strength. Jones was simply a man with a passionate love for the game of golf, a respect for his parents and family, and a sense of obligation to the world around him.

Jones retired from competitive golf at the age of 28 in order to pursue his other interests. These interests led to 3 degrees (engineering, law, and classics), the ability to speak six languages, and the founding of Augusta National Golf Course and the Masters Golf tournament. This, of course, was done while, as an amateur, he was performing the unprecedented and yet to be repeated feat of winning all four major golf tournaments in a single year... a act that is simply known as the "Grand Slam of Golf."

The film traces the life and accomplishments of Bobby Jones from the age of eight (played by Devon Gearhart) where, as a sickly child, he first developed his love for the game. The film then jumps forward to Bobby at the age of fourteen (Thomas Lewis) where, in a field of grown men, he wins the Georgia Amateur and begins to experience his first taste of fame. It is not something that he desires and even though his fame follows him throughout his life, it is more of a curse for him than a blessing.

The film jumps forward once more and we find Jones (Jim Caviezel) as a young man studying hard, falling in love with his future wife, and playing golf with the best professionals of that time.

Caviezel, not only had to learn how to play golf but he had to learn how to swing right-handed. Normally a leftie, Caviezel manages to convincingly depict what is arguably the best known swing in all of golf.

Though the film is set amidst the backdrop of the sport which Jones loved, the heart of the film is the man himself. Though his name is synonymous with sportsmanship and integrity among weekend duffers, it is possible that many people today don't know why. This film will help to fill in some of the gaps in our knowledge of who Jones was and what drove him to excel.

It is important to note that Jones is not depicted as being saint-like in the film. He drank, smoked, and, early in his playing days, was known for having a fierce temper which was demonstrated by a rather crude vocabulary and a penchant for throwing clubs. That Jones was able to eventually control his temper and become known for his civil and cordial manner on the links is a testament to the man.

Claire Forlani plays his loving and supportive wife, Mary and Jeremy Northam seems to relish the opportunity to cut loose as the expansive showman golfer Walter Hagen.

The film is rife with biblical principles, themes and lessons which can be used to illustrate God's wonderful truths. We see that the relentless training and daily practice that Jones was disciplined enough to do would result in a swing so pure and natural that sportswriters waxed poetic to describe it. We observe his integrity of playing the game fairly and honestly in a scene where he called a stroke on himself for a penalty no one else witnessed. He lost that match by one stroke but won the admiration of all. We see him resist the temptations of the money that awaited him if he would turn pro because he wanted to keep his love of the game untainted and pure. And finally, we hear the words of sportswriter Grantland Rice, who in his article announcing Jones' retirement said, "For when the one great Scorer comes to write against your name, He writes not that you won or lost but how you played the game."

It is a sentiment that applies to us all as we continue to play the game of life.

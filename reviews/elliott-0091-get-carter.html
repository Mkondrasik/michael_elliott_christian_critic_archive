Get Carter

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
October 6, 2000
Rating
1 star
Source
Original Author Copy

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Revenge; vengeance; justice; forgiveness; trusting God

Author-verified transcription

“Get Carter” has been made before. In 1971, a film starring Michael Caine was released to instant acclaim and is still considered by many to be a classic piece of British filmmaking. It is doubtful that the remake, starring Sylvester Stallone (“Copland”), will receive the same reception.

Based on the novel by Ted Lewis, “Get Carter” features a brutal Las Vegas debt collector, otherwise known as a leg-breaker, named Jack Carter. Long estranged from his family, Jack is reunited with them when he attends the funeral of his brother who has died in a DWI accident. Convinced that his brother was murdered, Jack becomes obsessed with finding his brother’s killer.

His interest confuses those around him, notably his sister-in-law Gloria (Miranda Richardson, “Chicken Run”) and niece Doreen (Rachael Leigh Cook, “She’s All That”). “Where were you when he was alive?” is the question on everyone’s tongue. It is a question Stallone answers with a stony silence.

Jack’s investigation leads him to Internet porn king Cyrus Paice (Mickey Rourke, “Wild Orchid”), multimillionaire computer geek Jeremy Kinnear (Alan Cumming, “Titus”), and his brother’s business partner Cliff Bumby (Michael Caine, who starred in the original “Get Carter”).

The action unfolds at an excruciatingly slow pace. Carter keeps threatening to take things to “the next level.” If only he would have followed through with that threat. The various beatings, killings and car chases are not enough to counteract the mind-numbing tediousness of the script by David McKenna (“American History X”).

Director Stephen T. Kay (“The Last Time I Committed Suicide”) reaches into his cinematic bag of tricks and tries to find a style that will hide the fact that there is precious little substance to be seen. He experiments freely with camera angles, camera speed, and rapid cuts. The bleak rain-soaked streets and metallic blue hue to the film set the proper atmosphere but all is for naught. There’s no reason to “get” this Carter.

Stallone is serviceable in the role of Carter. He’s not particularly likable or even explainable, but he has a decent scowl, bleeds well, and can punch with the best of them.

Some of the supporting cast provide slightly more interesting portrayals. Alan Cumming’s turn as a spineless millionaire offers a mild distraction and Rachael Leigh Cook is touching as the troubled daughter of the murdered man. Her scenes with Stallone are the best this film has to offer.

Despite the attempts to turn Carter’s quest into a search for redemption or an act of family pride, the brutality of Carter’s actions and the absence of clear motivation makes this film more about vigilante justice than anything else. And this is a topic which the Word of God addresses:

Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord. Romans 12:19 (KJV)

Feeding on revenge will always leave a bitter taste in one’s mouth. It is a spiritually unprofitable misuse of time and energy. It would be far more advantageous for us to focus our thoughts on the positive, edifying truths of God, assured in our belief that He will handle the settling of accounts at the proper time. And that He will do so to our complete and total satisfaction.

Author-verified transcription

According to the Internet Movie Database (www.imdb.com), the screenplay for A View From The Top was written by newcomer Eric Wald while he was attending a screen writing course at UCLA. Somehow, I doubt this piece of information will cause a rush at the university's admissions office. This leaden romantic comedy about a small town girl's dream to become a first class stewardess tries to sprout wings and fly but it never gets off the ground.

Part of the problem is that apparently Mr. Wald never made a firm decision as to the genre in which he wanted his film to belong. It has the structure and some of the sincerity of a romantic comedy, but it also ridicules its own characters in a spoof-like manner. The combination does not work.

Gwyneth Paltrow (Shallow Hal) stars as Donna, a trailer park girl who has her head in the clouds... literally. Wanting to leave her dead end life, she figures the glamour of being a stewardess working first class on a transatlantic flight is everything she has ever wanted. But first, she'll have to work her way up the stewardess ladder by shuttling drunks to Fresno on a trashy commuter charter.

Along her journey to personal fulfillment, she meets and befriends a wide assortment of bland, stereotypical characters: Sherry (Kelly Preston, For the Love of the Game), a big-haired, big-bosomed stew who teaches Donna the fundamentals; Christine (Christina Applegate, The Sweetest Thing), Donna's big-haired, big-bosomed stew trainee; John Whitney (Mike Myers, Austin Powers in Goldfinger), a myopic flight attendant instructor whose character blends the "smarminess" of Stuart Smaley with the appearance of Clarence the cross-eyed lion. And it is her mentor, Sally Weston (Candice Bergen, Sweet Home Alabama), queen of the stewardesses, who guides her on her "never say die" quest to be the best in-flight hostess that she can be.

But it is when she meets and falls in love with Ted (Mark Ruffalo, The Last Castle), that she discovers she is going to have to choose between her dream and her dreamboat.

Frankly, most of the film is flat and uninspired. The one possible exception is Mike Myers, who manages to cut loose with a few moments of inspired hilarity as the visually impaired and bitter John Whitney. But even his sight gags (pun intended) are hit and miss.

Brazilian director Bruno Barreto (Bossa Nova) never gets a handle on the material which kind of drifts along from one moment to the next with a listlessness bordering on boredom. Because A View From The Top straddles the fence between spoof and romantic comedy without landing firmly on either side, it fails at both. It is a colossal waste of time and talent.

Even the underlying moral to the movie (chase your dreams until you catch them) rings false, as if the filmmakers were treating it as a setup to a joke rather than something to be applauded. Setting goals and then working hard to achieve those goals is no laughing matter. It is a principle grounded in biblical truths and is a fundamental key to success.

Brothers, I do not consider myself yet to have taken hold of it. But one thing I do: Forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead, I press on toward the goal to win the prize for which God has called me heavenward in Christ Jesus. Philippians 3:13-14 (NIV)

Donna's desire to be the best stewardess that she could be and the actions she took to see her dream become a reality is actually quite admirable. We all should have that kind of vision, drive and passion for our endeavors. It is too bad these filmmakers didn't.

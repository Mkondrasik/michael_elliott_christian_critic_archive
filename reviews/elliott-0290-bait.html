Bait

Author-verified transcription

There are many different members of the crime family. Bait, the newest film from Warner Brothers, features two very different ones hailing from opposite ends of the spectrum. On one hand, we have the highly technical, sophisticated master criminal like Bristol (Doug Hutchison, The Green Mile), capable of almost single-handedly breaking into the Federal Reserve Bank and absconding with 42 million in gold bars. On the other hand, we have the fast talking, small thinking, penny ante criminal like Alvin Sanders (Jamie Foxx, Any Given Sunday) who can't even break into a seafood plant to steal a bucket of prawns (not shrimp - that would be too small time) without getting caught.

When Alvin is temporarily placed in a holding cell with Bristol's partner (Robert Pastorelli, TV's Murphy Brown) who managed to hide the gold bars before getting caught, US Treasury investigator Clenteen (David Morse, Crazy in Alabama) decides to plant a new experimental "superbug" in Alvin and plant a story in cyberspace that suggests Alvin knows the whereabouts of the missing gold. All that is left to do is to throw Alvin into the sea that is NYC and wait for Bristol to take the bait.

But this bait doesn't content himself to sit dangling on a hook. Released from prison, he tries to reenter society a changed man. It's just that his petty crime past keeps rising up to try and pull him off the straight and narrow. The eavesdropping Treasury agents must intervene to keep Alvin out of trouble long enough for him to attract the bigger fish.

With a mixture of action and humor, director Antoine Fuqua (The Replacement Killers) fashions a moderately amusing, sometimes thrilling adventure which, though flawed, is passable entertainment.

Mr. Foxx has very good comic timing which works to his benefit here. His character's false bravado which instantly melts in the face of real incredulity on an unsuspecting Alvin is also a source of much of the humor. Mr. Hutchison portrays Bristol as a typical creepy computer geek with a cold-blooded killer's instinct.

Bait follows a strictly formulaic pattern and in that light, the acting is more than adequate. The flaws in the film can be found mainly behind the camera. Many of the action scenes were not as sharp or exciting as they could have been due to camera placement, stylized camera effects and rough editing. In addition, the storyline did tend to get more than a bit unbelievable as the film wore on.

As the Treasury agents electronically follow Alvin's every move, they begin to root for him to overcome his criminal tendencies and start making correct decisions for his life. "He'll never change," is the observation of Clenteen, figuring that once a petty thief, always a petty thief. There is a verse of scripture that seems to support his belief.

Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? then may ye also do good, that are accustomed to do evil. Jeremiah 13:23 (KJV)

First glance would indicate that, as a black man will always be black and a leopard will always have spots, an evildoer will always do evil. But there is more here than meets the eye. The key word which adds understanding to this verse is "accustomed." It is translated from the Aramaic word "limmuwd" which means to be trained or taught. We will be whatever we drive or discipline ourselves to be. If we continuously train ourselves to think and do evil, we will certainly bear the fruit of those efforts.

But God is all about change. What has been taught can be untaught. What has been learned can be unlearned. There is no behavior so ingrained that the love of God cannot melt it away. What Jeremiah teaches us is that it is up to us. We have the responsibility to train our minds to think and do those things approved of God. With that renewed mindset we may join the body of Christ to become fishers of men. And with this kind of fishing, we don't need bait.

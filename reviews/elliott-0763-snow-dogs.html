Author-verified transcription

Eleven months of mediocrity followed by a month of Oscar contenders. That could be, with a few notable exceptions, a summary of the year in film, 2001. Judging solely from the release of Snow Dogs, 2002 looks like it is going to be "deja vu all over again" (to borrow from the wisdom of Yogi Berra).

Ted Brooks (Cuba Gooding Jr., Men of Honor) is a Miami dentist with a thriving practice, a hot car with customized plates (OPN*WYD) and an inexplicable fondness for the music of Michael Bolton. Word that an inheritance awaits him in Alaska comes as a bit of a surprise, although not as great as the bombshell that follows: He's adopted.

Upon arriving in Alaska, this Miami fish-out-of water receives the bulk of his birth mother's estate which happens to include her team of sled dogs, led by a particularly nasty Siberian Husky named Demon.

The crusty old Thunder Jack (James Coburn, Affliction) wants to claim the team as his own so he can enter them in the next Alaskan Challenge, an annual sled race. Ted is far more interested in discovering his roots, no matter how ice-encrusted they may be. Soon he is trying to prove himself to Jack and the other townspeople by showing that he can mush with the best of them.

Snow Dogs has a total of five credited screenwriters, which may be the first time a film's writing team has outnumbered the amount of laughs they were hired to put into the script. They obviously wanted to target a young audience but seem to have miscalculated a bit. Even those of a single digit age will be able to recognize that this story is too goofy... even for Disney.

Cuba Gooding Jr. tries to apply his charm and charisma to this role, but his contribution is pretty much limited to being chased by dogs; being bitten by dogs; being dragged by dogs; and being outsmarted by dogs. Let's hope he got paid more than the dogs. His charisma doesn't work here because there is little effort made to develop much of a character or relationship between the other characters.

James Coburn is more successful in building a recognizable character arc, but to no avail. His talents are wasted. Nichelle Nichols (Star Trek) is stately as Ted's adoptive mother and Joanna Bacalso (Dude, Where's My Car) gives the film its PG sex appeal as the Alaskan bartender who falls for Ted.

Director Brian Levant (The Flintstones in Viva Rock Vegas) simply is not able to lift this project above the mundane and at moments even manages to sink it a few levels lower.

What is surprising, especially from a studio which normally excels in developing personalities for its films' non-human characters, is the lack of definition among the members of the seven-dog team. With the exception of Nana (sweet and lovable) and Demon (mean-spirited and angry), the other dogs have no distinguishing attributes to separate them from the others.

What Ted must learn and master in this story is the fact that on a dog sled, only one creature can be "top dog." That one has to be the human driver whose commands are to be obeyed by his team. That is, in truth, the natural order of things because it is how God initially designed life.

And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. Genesis 1:26 (KJV)

God created all and then gave all to man for his use. Having dominion means having supreme authority or control. Of course, if man continues in forcing members of the animal kingdom to appear in silly movies like Snow Dogs, our right to that supreme authority may be called into question.

Author-verified transcription

Filmmaker Michael Moore turns up the heat in his blistering attack upon president George W. Bush in the heavily biased Fahrenheit 9/11. Less a documentary than a political manifesto, Moore wears his agenda like a badge of honor. He's after the bringing down of a president. And who knows? He has made such an incendiary film that it could conceivably have that effect.

Using a combination of archived film footage, sarcastic narration, and an indignant tone throughout, Moore traces the events from Bush's unusual presidential victory, through his first months in office until the screen goes black and we her the sounds of the planes hitting the World Trade Center on September 11, 2001.

It is an event that has obviously changed the world. The depiction of it changes our perception of the film as well. Before and after, Moore uses sharp and biting humor to make his points and underscore his observations but in the face of such tragedy, his humor may ultimately generate more outrage than laughter.

Proving the truism, "a picture is worth a thousand words," Moore is most effective when he relies on the remarkable footage he has obtained. President Bush reading from My Little Goat in front of an elementary school classroom after hearing the news of the attack on New York City is a searing image made worse as Moore adds a time clock and a voiceOver that guesses what might be going through the president's mind and the minutes tick by and he continues to sit.

Moore is less successful with the presentation of some of his factual evidence. He spends a great deal of time questioning why members of the bin Laden family were allowed to leave the United States after 9/11 without first being interrogated. He rehashes the poor handling of Florida's election results. In both cases, his conclusions are unconvincing.

He also inserts some of the typical Moore shenanigans: He rents a Mr. Frostie ice cream truck and drives it through the streets of DC, reading the controversial Patriot Act over its loudspeaker as he drives past congressional office buildings. He accosts members of the House and Senate on the streets of DC and asks them to sign a petition that would send their children to Iraq. Such scenes have little place in a serious debate regarding the actions and policies of our government.

Perhaps the most manipulative segments involve Lila Lipscombe, a Flint Michigan mother who happily sends her children into military service because of the benefits and opportunities it provides. She is obviously devastated when her son is killed after his black Hawk helicopter is downed in Iraq. Her grief and anger are real and heartbreaking.

Moore raises important points that need to be part of the political discussion. There are issues raised that need to be addressed and questions asked that should be answered. But let us not make the mistake of being emotionally manipulated into accepting his accusations blindly. Moore has make an effective piece of propaganda. It can't be mindlessly dismissed but neither should it be mindlessly embraced. For all the humor in contains, its subject matter is far too serious.

Author-verified transcription

"Indians ain't supposed to smile... Get stoic. Look like you just came back from killing a buffalo."

Such modern day Indian advice is what sets Smoke Signals apart from the customary Hollywood treatment of our "red-skinned" neighbors. Touted as the first movie about native Americans by native Americans, Smoke Signals is a refreshing and often amusing look at characters who just happen to live on a present day reservation.

The above quoted advice comes from Victor Joseph (Adam Beach, Song of Hiawatha), an Indian with a major chip on his shoulder. His father (Gary Farmer, Lilies) left under mysterious conditions when Victor was much younger and he has never come to terms with it.

The beneficiary of that advice was Thomas Builds-The-Fire (Evan Adams, Toby McTeague), who is one of the oddest and uniquely original characters we're likely to meet this year. Everything about Thomas is slightly different: his clothes, his ever-present smile, his unusual manner of speech, and his penchant for telling stories. Oh, yes... Thomas is a master storyteller.

Smoke Signals, when boiled down to its essential qualities, is simply a road picture. Victor receives word that his wandering father has died, so Victor and Thomas leave the reservation, bound for Phoenix to collect his remains. But, as in all good road pictures, the story is in the journey (both the literal journey as well as the figurative one that Victor takes as he searches for internal peace and reconciliation.)

First time director Chris Eyre tells the story ably and even manages to inject some memorable visuals at the same time. The image of baby Thomas, wrapped in a blanket, flying through the air as his parents throw him out of a second story window of a house on fire; the flames dying down to burning embers, and then to ash as the older Thomas now narrates the story.

Evan Adams takes the character of Thomas and uses it to steal the movie. There is something strangely compelling about him that draws our focus upon him. Perhaps it is the stories he tells.

Indians have long had a practice of keeping an "oral tradition." In order to pass on the history of their people or tribe to future generations, they tell stories. Feats of victory or defeat; acts of courage or cowardice. Through the stories, the historic events come to life.

So it was with the early Old Testament believers. God's Word was not given in a written form until the days of Moses. But before Moses, there was Adam, Seth, Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Issac, Joseph, and many other great believers. How did they keep the truth of God burning in their hearts? One way was by orally passing down the knowledge of God from generation to generation.

Part of a father's responsibility was to see that his children were instructed in the ways of the Lord. Without a written standard, the ways of God could only be communicated orally... by telling their offspring all that God had done for them as a people. And my oh my, what stories they could tell...

In those days, men lived longer than today. Adam lived long enough to be able to teach many generations directly. He would have been able to instruct Noah's father of the many things God had directly taught him while he was still dwelling in the garden of Eden. We should never discount the importance of the impact of the words of those who were there when events happened. Adam was able to teach Lamech, who in turn was able to teach his son, Noah. What great impact those teachings would have had.

Whether the stories Thomas tells have that kind of impact is immaterial. The purpose of oral tradition is to convey historical records so they may be preserved in order to present them to future generations.

Smoke Signals gives us an inside look at life in and around a modern Indian reservation. God's Word gives us an inside look at the very essence of life itself. Both deal with the importance of verbally communicating the knowledge of one generation to the next generation so that knowledge does not become lost on the winds of time.

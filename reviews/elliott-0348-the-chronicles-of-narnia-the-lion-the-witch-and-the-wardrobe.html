The Chronicles of Narnia: The Lion, The Witch, and the Wardrobe

Author-verified transcription

Among Christian film-goers, the most anticipated release of 2005 would have to be The Chronicles of Narnia: The Lion, The Witch, and the Wardrobe, based upon the classic children's stories by C.S. Lewis. Fans should rejoice as their beloved book is wonderfully realized by director Andrew Adamson (Shrek), a team of computer animators, and a quartet of perfectly cast English children.

While London is being shelled by German planes, the four Pevansie children are sent to a country estate where, it is believed, they will be safe. But when the youngest, Lucy (played with wide-eyed wonder by Georgie Henley), ventures into a wardrobe during a game of hide-and-seek, she enters a wintery world where beauty and danger lie around every corner.

She eventually gets her siblings to join her in the magical land of Narnia which is being threatened by the White Witch (Tilda Swinton). A prophecy states that two sons of Adam and two daughters of Eve (i.e. humans) will one day arrive to free Narnia of the White Witch's oppression. The Pevansie children are seen to be the fulfillment of that prophecy and Aslan (voiced by Liam Neeson), a lion who is rightful king of Narnia, returns to help the children face their fears and their foe.

Ten-year-old Georgie Henley steals the picture and our hearts as Lucy. It is a delightful performance of childish glee and joy.

William Moseley, as Peter, the eldest son, successfully bridges that gap between boy and man as he is called upon to rise to a position of leadership and responsibility. Both Anna Popplewell, as Susan, and Skandar Keynes, as Edward, also fit their respective roles nicely.

Tilda Swinton is appropriately icy-cold as the self-proclaimed Queen of Narnia. While her costuming might be a bit over the top, she is devilishly restrained in her performance which makes it that much more sinister.

The fantasy world that Adamson and his crew construct out of Lewis' imagination is a wonderful blend of the familiar and make-believe. The CGI work is not as accomplished as Lord of the Rings or King Kong but comparisons are always a bit unfair. The film works as a fantasy adventure and the special effects serve to illustrate a land that exists only in the mind.

Of course, Christian themes resonate throughout the movie and it isn't hard to pick them out. The universal war between good and evil; the importance of forgiveness; and strength of the family unit are al woven through the narrative as are symbols recognizable in Christianity. (example: Aslan the lion as King of Narnia/Jesus Christ as the lion of Judah, king of kings). The concept of sacrifice and redemption also plays a large part in the telling of this story.. as does the idea of resurrection. Although this is a family film and essentially a children's fantasy tale, there is a considerable amount of PG-rated warfare and death depicted. But there is a power greater than death and this film provides one of the best excuses to expound upon this truth in post-movie discussions since The Passion of the Christ.

By reading the scriptures, we can see that the idea of a resurrection of the dead has long been a stumbling block for people - yet it is one of the key beliefs that lead us to an eternal life. Any opportunity to teach it to others is most welcome.

Why should it be thought a thing incredible with you, that God should raise the dead? Acts 26:8 (KJV)

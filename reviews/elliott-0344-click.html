Click

Sermon / spiritual topic: Time

Author-verified transcription

Men and their remote controls... it must be a Freudian thing. In Click, Adam Sandler and company take this relationship to the nth degree by giving a guy the ultimate universal remote control... one that can control his entire universe.

Mike Newman (Sandler) is a good man with a great family who is making a common mistake. In order to provide for them, he's been working so much that he doesn't have any time to spend with them. On top of that, life just seems to be getting more and more complicated. For instance, Mike never seems to know which remote operates which device. Frustrated, he embarks on an evening visit to Bed Bath & Beyond in search of a solution. He finds one in the "Beyond" section.

A tinkering craftsman named Morty (Christopher Walken), sympathizes with Mike's situation and grants his wish. The remote he gives him not only controls every electronic device in the house.. it controls every aspect of Mike's life. It will fast forward through an argument; rewind to a specific time in his past; even pause to allow him time to alter reality in his favor.

But the device has a down side. It eventually programs itself based on previous usage. Soon Mike is no longer in command of his life and is fast forwarding through the years with the speed of a TIVO subscriber.

There's a lot about Click to recommend to audiences but not enough to justify a positive review. I like the relationship that is established between Mike and his family. It is a wholesome, loving environment peopled by individuals who truly care about one another.

Adam Sandler is the heart and soul of the film. His dorky everyman persona continues to strike a chord with American audiences.

Oscars may not be in his future but multi-million dollar picture deals will continue to come his way. Kate Beckinsale is adorable as Mike's wife, Donna. Attractive, supportive, and understanding beyond human comprehension, she plays an ideal without making it seem unrealistic. Henry Winkler and Julie Kavner both have poignant moments as Mike's parents.

David Hasselhoff as Mike's boss and Jennifer Coolidge as Donna's friend play much broader and are less successful in matching a more mature tone that director Frank Coraci seems to be striving for.

The script splits the middle. It seems to want to delve into serious (albeit well-worn) matters regarding life and family but, at the same time, it relies on sophomoric humor to keep the pace moving along. Because of this, the film becomes rather one dimensional and ultimately dull.

The central theme of the movie is one of time. Namely, how are we to make best use of it? Mike allowed himself to be completely absorbed by his work, forgoing his parental and marital duties (and joys). His experience in this story taught him a valuable lesson which we should all take to heart. Time is unforgiving.

Therefore we must utilize it wisely and well.

Whereas ye know not what shall be on the morrow. For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away. James 4:14 (KJV)

God tells us to redeem the time that we've been given. It is a commodity to be spent, and spent well. It is all too easy to lose ourselves in the hustle and bustle of this world only to find out too late that we have neglected the most important parts. When we finally come to that realization, we are the poorer for it.

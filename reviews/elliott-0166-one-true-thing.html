One True Thing

Michael Elliott

Rating
2 stars

Original Author Copy

Author-verified transcription

Mark Twain has been quoted as saying: "When I was fourteen, my father was so ignorant, I could hardly stand to have him around. When I got to be twenty-one, I was astonished at how much he had learned in seven years."

The irony of that statement is, of course, that it is the child who has grown to recognize the wisdom that was always present in the parent. In One True Thing, Ellen Gulden has an opportunity to make a similarly astonishing discovery regarding her parents.

Ellen (Renee Zellweger, Jerry Maguire) is asked by the father she idolizes to leave her job to come home and take care of her mother who has been diagnosed with cancer and with whom she had never felt close. After all, her father was a published author, a respected college professor - now chairing an entire department. He was smart, funny, charming. Her mother just stayed at home... There was nothing at all thrilling about her life.

Yet, as her mother grows weaker, and Ellen takes on more and more of her responsibilities, especially in meeting the demanding needs of her mother's husband (the father she once idolized), her respect and admiration for her mother grows, even as she begins to become aware of her father's weaknesses and faulty humanness.

One True Thing is based on the novel by Anna Quindlen and is a family drama in the truest sense of the word. The other characters in the movie are sketchily drawn and inconsequential. The focus of this film is on the dynamic between the family members, particularly between Ellen and her parents.

That being said, this movie is "owned" by Meryl Streep (Dancing at Lughnasa). Her portrayal of the cancer-stricken Kate Gulden is absolutely magnificent. Few actresses completely embody a role as does Ms. Streep.

Whether Kate is magnanimously offering her time to the community or lying on her death bed giving final words of advice to her daughter, there isn't a false note to be seen. A wonderful job by Ms. Streep.

The rest of the movie pales in comparison, although Miss Zellweger does have some fine moments as the daughter who starts seeing her parents in a completely different light.

William Hurt (Lost In Space) does a commendable job in a very difficult role. As George Gulden, he loves his wife dearly. Almost as much as he loves his place in the literary world... a place which sadly exists more in his mind than in reality.

What hurt the film artistically is the structure chosen by director Carl Franklin (Devil in a Blue Dress) and screenwriter Karen Croner (Scattered Dreams). The story is told in a series of flashbacks rather than in a conventional mode. What that structure did was to somehow depersonalize the events... reducing the empathy we should have been feeling for the characters.

There are moments that shine through, however. Kate, gray as a ghost, ravaged by the cancer that was destroying her body, tried one last time to teach her daughter... "It is so much easier to be happy... Much easier to choose to love the things that you have instead of always yearning for what you're missing... Or think you're missing."

Wise words from a remarkable character. They could have been borrowed from God Himself Who said:

In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you. 1 Thessalonians 5:18 [KJV]

Notice that God said "IN everything", not "FOR everything." We aren't thankful FOR the sicknesses or illnesses or accidents or tragedies that beset us in this life. But we can be thankful IN the face of those sicknesses, illnesses, accidents and tragedies. We can continue to believe that God loves us and will deliver us.

All sickness can be considered as a temptation of the devil... a temptation to stop believing the promises of God, Whose will for us is that we "prosper and be in health." The ending of the film disappoints and does an injustice to the character of Kate. The final scenes do not describe an act of courage or strength, but of giving in to the devil's temptation.

Author-verified transcription

The Door in the Floor takes us inside the lives of two emotionally broken people and then introduces into this dysfunctional environment an innocent who is subsequently drawn into their disturbing drama.

Jeff Bridges stars as Ted Cole, author and illustrator of children's books. His perverse behavior and odd perspective of the world would, if widely known, cause his fans to empty their shelves and libraries of his works and drawings. His attractive wife, Marion (Kim Basinger), has never recovered from the accidental death of her two teenage boys. She is a sad woman who allows her despondency to occasionally bring her to a near catatonic state. Neither of them are particularly good parents to their one surviving child, 4 year old Ruth (Elle Fanning). It isn't surprising to learn that their marriage is not doing well and that they have begun a trial separation.

Into this dysfunctional environment comes Eddie (Jon Foster), a prep school student who wants to be a writer and has offered himself to Ted to be his summer assistant. Eddie is eager, shy, inexperienced and quickly smitten with the beautiful Marion.

He will have plenty of opportunity to fantasize about her. It turns out that Ted doesn't have much use for an assistant apart from his needing a driver to shuttle him around Long Island as he sketches any woman willing to pose nude for him. Instead of finding and depicting the beauty of the female form, Ted goes out of his way to demean and humiliate his subjects. His most recent model is Evelyn Vaughn (Mimi Rogers).

Marion is drawn to Eddie because he resembles one of her sons. She allows her grief and pain to pervert the maternal love she might have felt as she takes advantage of the young man's fantasies and offers herself to him sexually.

None of these characters are easy to like. Even the "innocent" Eddie is all too easily corrupted and is shown to have shallow moral roots. It is to the actors' credit that they present their characters, unsympathetic as they are, as entirely believable.

Jeff Bridges continues to prove himself to be a serious actor whose work is largely unrecognized or unappreciated. As Ted, he presents a character with severe psychological defects. And yet his character is so rooted in emotional truths we can recognize them even as we condemn them.

With duds like Blind Date and The Real McCoy, it is easy to forget that Kim Basinger is an Academy Award winner (for L.A. Confidential). She has shown that with the right material and right director she can deliver the goods. She does so here. Her portrayal of a mother consumed with guilt and grief is heartbreakingly real. We don't really associate with her character nor can we condone her actions but the actress allows us to understand what her character is thinking and experience some of the emotions that she is feeling.

In one scene Ted, Eddie and another character played by Bijou Phillips discuss degrees of morality and how everyone is able to feel morally superior to someone else. It's a dangerous rationalization they use to excuse their own immoral actions. Morality is not something that comes in degrees. Instead of comparing ourselves to one another to measure our moral compass, God commands us to adhere to the standard of truth which he has established. Our morality is defined by our obedience to God's authority... nothing less.

And it shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God, as he hath commanded us. Deuteronomy 6:25 (KJV)

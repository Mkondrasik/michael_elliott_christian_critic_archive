"A general who became a slave. A slave who became a gladiator. A gladiator who defied an empire." This quote from the press release effectively and succinctly describes the story of Gladiator, the stunning new epic-sized production by Ridley Scott (Blade Runner).

Russell Crowe (The Insider) is General Maximus, the great military leader responsible for bringing to Rome the victories needed to build its empire under Marcus Aurelius Caesar. With no more enemies to fight, Maximus wishes only to return home to his wife and son and live the simple life of a farmer. His emperor, however, has other plans for him.

Tired of war and the corruption which has poisoned Rome, Marcus Aurelius intends to return the rule of Rome to the people. He expresses this desire to Maximus and asks him to be the protector of Rome until the framework of the new republic can be properly installed and put into operation.

When Commodus (Joaquin Phoenix, 8MM), the emperor's son and natural heir to the seat of power, hears of his father's plan, he takes matters into his own murderous hands. With Marcus Aurelius dead, Commodus becomes the new emperor of Rome while Maximus is disgraced and given a death sentence.

After falling into the hands of a slave trader, Maximus is sold to Proximo (Oliver Reed, The Three Musketeers), an ex-gladiator turn promoter. For Maximus, fighting in the arena is a matter of survival, not choice. His skill, leadership, and principled behavior combine to make him extremely popular with the audience as well as with the other gladiator slaves. It is only a matter of time before he rises to a position to challenge the evil emperor and be vindicated.

Ridley Scott has brought together the various elements of the film masterfully. The production values are of the highest caliber. From the stirring score by Hans Zimmer (The Thin Red Line) to John Mathieson's cinematography, the attention to detail is evident in every frame. It is to Mr. Mathieson's credit that the very violent battle scenes (and they are violent) are not so gratuitously gory as to be unbearable.

Russell Crowe is magnificent in the role of Maximus. His is a character of both physical and moral strength... A cross between Judah Ben-Hur of Ben-Hur and William Wallace of Braveheart.

Joaquin Phoenix is almost too effective in his role of a weak man who mistakes fear for respect and tries to command his people to love him. His Commodus is indeed odious, and we can gauge Mr. Phoenix's success by the bile which rises in our throat each time he appears on the screen.

The supporting cast was selected with great care: Richard Harris (Cry the Beloved Country) as the aging Caesar, Connie Nielsen (Mission to Mars) as Lucilla, daughter to Marcus Aurelius, Derek Jacobi (I, Claudius) as Senator Gracchus, and the late Oliver Reed to whom the film is dedicated.

Spiritually, for me, there was too much emphasis placed upon the pagan belief of Elysium, or the afterlife. A constant theme throughout the film, it became an intrusion to me as I continually had to remind myself that its inclusion paralleled the devil's first recorded lie in Genesis 3:4.

If we overlook this aspect of the film, we might be able to see the many other spiritually edifying truths reflected in the story. Chief among them was the obvious fight between good and evil seen in the conflict between Commodus and Maximus. Commodus was so envious of the love and adoration expressed towards Maximus that he went to great lengths attempting to acquire it for himself.

What he was too blind to see was how Maximus earned that love (which he neither sought nor desired.) That it was bestowed to him was a response to the quality of life he demonstrated. Love is not a goal to be selfishly attained for its own sake, but rather the resulting fruit of a man living life based upon the immutable truths of God.

But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. Galatians 5:22-23 (KJV)

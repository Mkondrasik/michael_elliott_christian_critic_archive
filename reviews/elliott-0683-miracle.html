Author-verified transcription

If you were alive in the 1980s, you're most probably familiar with the exuberant cry heard around the world, "Do you believe in miracles?!?" It was uttered by sports commentator Al Michaels in the unbelievably supercharged closing seconds of the 1980 Olympic hockey game between the US and Russia. Miracle relives that experience and shows us what it took for twenty amateurs to defeat what was, at that time, the best in the world.

For decades leading up to 1980, the Russian hockey team was, by all accounts, invincible. They won the gold medal in the previous four Olympics. They were strong, experienced, fast, and unmerciful. But American Herb Brooks (Kurt Russell) had a vision and a plan. He would be given the opportunity to use both when he is selected to coach the US hockey team during their bid for Olympic glory in the 1980 games being held in Lake Placid, NY.

His team would be comprised of amateurs, admittedly not always the best players of those from whom he had to choose. Brooks explained that he wasn't looking for the best players - he wanted the right ones. He wanted players who would show him that they had the heart and willingness to play the game the way he knew it had to be played.

With his team assembled, he begins a series of brutal practices, reshaping the traditional American bruising, banging style of play with a faster, sharper style. By focusing on speed, passing and teamwork, Brooks hoped to be able to beat the Russians by being aggressive and putting them on the defensive.

Directed by Gavin O'Connor (Tumbleweeds), Miracle captures the unbelievable feel of the 1980 experience by casting young hockey players in the roles of the Olympians. O'Connor figured that he'd rather try to pull a performance from a skater than have to teach an actor how to skate at this level. It was a risk that paid off handsomely. In fact, one of the characters, Buzz Schneider, is being played by Billy Schneider, his son.

Because they are unknowns, the audience is more inclined to view the US players as a team or single unit rather than as a collection of individual talent. This, of course, parallels Coach Brooks' vision. From the beginning, his emphasis was on teamwork and unity.

Kurt Russell portrays Herb Brooks, showing the zeal, commitment and drive which led the US coach to have the success he had. Not afraid to alienate his players, he drove them tirelessly. He was not there to be their friend. He was their coach. As a result of his rather cold demeanor, the boys turned to each other for support and encouragement and cemented the bond that would serve them so well on the ice. It was the bond that Brooks wanted them to have.

Throughout the film, during practice, the coach would ask his players who they played for. Each one would respond with the name of the team or school for whom he played. With each response, the coach would push them harder. It wasn't until Mike Eruzione (Patrick O'Brien Demsey), who later became the team's captain, shouted out "I play for the United States of America!" that Brooks finally heard the response he was waiting for.

"You are still worldly. For since there is jealousy and quarreling among you, are you not worldly? Are you not acting like mere men? For when one says, 'I follow Paul,' and another, 'I follow Apollos,' are you not mere men?" 1 Corinthians 3:3-4 (NIV)

Coach Brooks knew, as the apostle Paul before him, that for his team to succeed, all ego, pride and individualism had to be put away. The focus and the energy had to be directed towards like-minded unity. Team USA had to think of themselves as one man. That is why they won. We, who are in the body of Christ, must reach that same mature level of togetherness if we are to succeed in our goals.

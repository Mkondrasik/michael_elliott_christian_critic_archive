Author-verified transcription

Whipped is a movie for which a whipping would be too lenient a treatment. Better that this film be dragged behind the theater and beaten senseless. Then again, considering the complete lack of intelligence evidenced on screen, perhaps someone has already beaten us to the punch.

The film tells the story of four men and the woman who comes between them and their "scamming" ways. Scamming is the rather artless practice these men have in seducing, conniving, or otherwise maneuvering women into their bed for the purpose of having sex.

They meet at a diner each Sunday morning and over their eggs and sausage tell their sorry tales to each other, using a kind of short hand slang which is demeaning to women and insulting to the rest of us.

Brad (Brian Van Holt, A Very Brady Sequel) is a young stockbroker who tries to charm the pants off of girls using his good looks and the pretense of being the brother of an old college friend of whoever his"target" is in that moment. Zeke (Zorie Barber in his feature film debut) hangs out in artsy coffee houses and fancies himself as being an enigma with an "air of mystery" which, in his own mind, makes him irresistible. Jonathan (Jonathan Abrahams in this feature film debut) spends most of his time alone with porn magazines and an assortment of hand lotions.

Joining these three pathetic losers is Eric (Judah Dome, Spanking the Monkey). He was once a scammer who specialized in "throwing himself on the grenade" (hitting on the ugly girl in a group of beauties) so his friends could score with the others. He married one of those "grenades" and now can only live vicariously through the perversely pathetic antics of Brad, Jonathan, and Zeke.

One week, conversation over the eggs and sausage halts as each guy had met "someone special" for whom scamming just isn't appropriate. Of course it turns out that they each have fallen for the same woman. Mia (Amanda Peet, The Whole Nine Yards) is the girl of their dreams. When each of them try to build a relationship with her, their longtime friendship is jeopardized as their pact of "pals before gals, bro's before ho's" is quickly forgotten.

The four male actors fill their performances with crude mannerisms and foul language that appears rough and unrehearsed. Ms. Peet provides a more polished performance although her characterization is no more appealing than those of the guys.

Obviously the content of Whipped is highly sexual in nature although there is no prolonged or overt nudity. Peter M. Cohen, who wrote, produced and directed this film, has called this work semi-autobiographical which is not something I'd point to with any pride or satisfaction. There isn't a single healthy relationship or mature minded individual portrayed on screen.

The title refers to how this one woman has been able to beat down the machismo posing of the three main guys. Being whipped is the opposite of the assertive, aggressive male behavior they choose to display when trolling for trollops.

The thing is this: the love relationship between a man and a woman that was designed and crafted by the God who also created the heavens and earth is not seen reflected anywhere in this movie. Ephesian goes as far as to compare the marriage relationship to the relationship between Christ and his church. Proverbs describes at length the attributes of a virtuous woman and assesses her worth as being "far above rubies." These are the examples to hold in our mind when thinking of forming a relationship with the opposite sex.

The first line of the film sums up the fundamental error in Mr. Cohen's view of modern day sex. Mia, in a voice over, says, "Everybody (expletive deleted) everybody else."

Perhaps that is Mr. Cohen's experience in the world in which he and his friends live. But it is not true in mine nor among those with whom I choose to associate.

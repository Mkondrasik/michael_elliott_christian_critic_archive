Author-verified transcription

Talk about a rude awakening. After December's plethora of quality films, most of which are vying for Oscar consideration, here comes January's first entry: My Baby's Daddy. It is a lame, thoroughly insulting "black" comedy about boys in the hood learning to become men by first becoming parents.

The trio of "brothers" include Anthony Anderson as G, Eddie Griffin as Lonnie, and implausibly, the oh-so-white Michael Imperioli as Dom. These three losers, known for their wild and woolly parties, manage to simultaneously get their girls pregnant so that all deliver babies on the same day. None of these relationships are what we'd consider to be conventional.

Lonnie's girl is a foul-mouthed, streetwise gal who has absolutely no emotional attachment to the father of her baby. The baby, to her, represents a meal ticket. Dom's girl was a one night fling with a coworker which resulted in a "present" that neither expected. She had other plans for her life that did not include him... or anyone else of his gender. G's girl genuinely loves him, despite the fact that her family is disappointed in her choice. The problem here is that he is still holding onto the dream of becoming a professional boxer..., this despite the fact that he shows little skill or proficiency in the sport.

When the babies arrive, these three guys are confronted with the harsh reality of what they have done and because their fathers had pretty much abandoned them when they were young, they have no role models to emulate or build upon as they approach their new roles and responsibilities.

As much as I would like to say that this film has some redeeming quality that merits attention, I really don't see where the script is deserving of much consideration.

These three "fathers" are completely lame-brained and clueless until the time they need to step up to the plate and take on responsibility for the lives they helped to bring into the world. And even then, their ability to contribute to the positive upbringing of their children remains questionable.

There are segments of this film which are totally "cringe-worthy." And no directorial slights of hand or redemptive dialogue help to assuage the discomfort these scenes evoke.

Take it from someone who has seen it up close and personal. Fatherhood is a blessed event. I'd like to say that this special relationship is what drives this particular movie, but I'm not sure that's true. There's too much inane, sophomoric behavior taking place to give it any kind of plausible credibility.

Fatherhood is more than a genetic relationship. The spiritual responsibilities that God places upon a father are indeed serious. And for good reason... it is the relationship He chose to have with us. Who better than God can define what it is?

For all the attempts at good intentions, this film fails miserably in conveying the definition of a "good" father. A far better description can be found in the Scriptures:

Or what man is there of you, whom if his son ask bread, will he give him a stone? Or if he ask a fish, will he give him a serpent? If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him? Matthew 7:9-11 (KJV)

To become a father and to bring a new life into this world presents a responsibility and obligation that is difficult to put into words. Only those who have been there truly understand.

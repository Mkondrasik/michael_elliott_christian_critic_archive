Collateral Damage

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 8, 2002
Rating
1½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Proverbs 28:20Psalms 112:4-101 Corinthians 4:2

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Faithfulness; responsibility; heroism; duty

Author-verified transcription

Hollywood, take note. Since September 11th 2001, America has learned how to recognize the true measure of a hero. The cartoon, comic book versions we are given in films like Collateral Damage will no longer satisfy us.

Arnold Schwarzenegger (True Lies) stars as Gordy Brewer, a beefy L.A. firefighter who, in a tragic instant, loses his wife and son to a terrorist bombing. Although the U.S. Intelligence forces know who is responsible, there is little that can be done due to the political machinations which surround the event.

For Gordy, politics don’t mean a thing. All that matters is making the person who killed his wife and child pay for what he has done.

Gordy inserts himself into South America and makes his way upcountry into the stronghold of “The Wolf” (Cliff Curtis, The Piano), Columbia’s terrorist leader who has declared “war” against America’s innocent citizens to further his own cause. There, in an ironic chance meeting, he helps the wife (Francesca Neri, Hannibal) and child of the man whom he intends to kill.

Keeping one step behind Gordy is Brandt (Elias Koteas, The Thin Red Line), a CIA operative who cannot pursue The Wolf to a full extent due to political and budgetary restraints. Gordy is obviously not bound by the same red tape which has effectively tangled any legitimate response the government might make and so Brandt watches with a mixture of frustration and envy as Gordy succeeds in single-handedly achieving what the strongest nation in the world is unable to do.

Schwarzenegger shows his sensitive side by stoically grieving the death of his character’s wife and child but the film soon reverts back to action mode with plenty of explosions, stunts, and CGI action with Arnold in the thick of it all.

Two supporting cast members turn in polished performances of note: John Turturro (The Luzhin Defense) as a mechanic who contracts for the Columbian warlord; and John Leguizamo (Moulin Rouge!) as a cocky lieutenant within the terrorist regime. The rest are a bland mix, indistinguishable amid the cacophony that is a Schwartzenegger action film.

Director Andrew Davis (The Fugitive) throws the action at us in spurts. The bombing sequences are adeptly handled and Gordy’s escape through the Columbian jungles is thrilling. But the lulls between the action scenes slow the film’s pace to a crawl and the political statements, if indeed there were any to be made, are too muddled to matter. This may have more to do with the screenplay by newcomers David and Peter Griffiths which is sloppily constructed, alternating between being too obvious at some moments and too incredulous at others.

At the center of it all is Arnold as a stone-faced fireman, who takes his bulging biceps and becomes a one man army against terrorism. This kind of “hero” pales in comparison to the men and women who have demonstrated their bravery (and continue to do so daily) by the simple act of doing their jobs.

That’s the true measure of a hero. Someone who can be counted on to do what he is called to do, day in and day out. Someone who takes his training and puts it to use. Someone for whom the job takes priority over any other selfish interest that may exist. A hero can be a fireman, a policeman, a father, a mother, a president, a teacher... just about anyone who faithfully carries out his duties to the best of his abilities no matter what.

A faithful man shall abound with blessings. Proverbs 28:20a (KJV)

Given the choice between a man who is ready and willing to take on the world or a man who is ready and willing to take on his responsibilities in the world, it is the latter who is more of a hero in my book.

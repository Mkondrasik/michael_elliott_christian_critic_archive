Shattered Glass

Sermon / spiritual topic: Truth; Integrity; Deception

Author-verified transcription

A movie about journalistic integrity may not have universal appeal... but it should. It is an issue that affects us all, whether we are aware of it or not.

Shattered Glass tells the true story of Steven Glass (Hayden Christensen) who, in the late 90s, was a rising star and the youngest staff writer for The New Republic. In 1998, he was discovered to have fabricated many of his stories out of whole cloth.

The film expertly depicts the intoxicating feeling of having one's words read by important people. At the time, the DC-based New Republic billed itself as being the "in-flight magazine for Air Force One." As the film shows, Stephen Glass found the lure of fame more powerful than the ethical standards of the profession.

Using charm and self-effacing tactics, he managed to pull the wool over the eyes of his coworkers and editors. Finding a loophole in the magazine's fact-checking system, Glass was able to write juicy, provocative pieces that were, in a phrase, nothing more than bald-faced lies.

As much as we would like to think this as being an isolated occurrence, one only need be reminded of Jason Blair whose recent actions at the New York Times seem remarkably similar.

In the film, Glass is portrayed as an emotionally needy and manipulative young man who counted on his faux humility to propel him to greatness. And so it did, for a time.

He managed to snow his first editor, played very sympathetically by Hank Azaria. Even his second editor, Chuck Lane (Peter Sarsgaard), seemed willing to give Glass the benefit of a hundred doubts... right up to that point where the mountain of lies which Glass had built began to cave in on itself. That's when the kid gloves came off as Lane begins to fight for something more important than bylines and scoops. Truth.

Christensen plays the part perfectly. In his depiction, Glass is a scheming sycophant whose major talent seems centered in his own imagination. Yet he is not altogether obnoxious. A strong imagination, an affable personality and no integrity whatsoever combine to make Stephen Glass the media darling he was to become. What is fascinating is how easily it occurred.

Director Billy Ray, working from his own screenplay which was based upon a Vanity Fair article written by H.G. "Buss" Bissinger, addresses questions not often handled in today's films. Our news and entertainment media have commingled to such an extent that it can be hard to discern where one ends and the other begins. This was certainly the case of Stephen Glass for whom fact and fiction were woven so indiscriminately.

That's the trouble with going through life without a standard of truth or a gauge to help tell the difference between right and wrong. It becomes clearly obvious that Glass has no such standard, and therefore has nothing to anchor him at times of conflict. There's no one to council him at times when a decision is needed. There's nothing to protect him during adversarial attacks. His lies are built upon other lies and they will eventually go the ways of all lies... collapsing under the intense strain of scrutiny.

The getting of treasures by a lying tongue is a vanity tossed to and fro of them that seek death. Proverbs 21:6 (KJV)

There are times when we will be tempted to take "shortcuts" to success. We must resist that temptation. Any shortcut which does not follow the highway that our God has paved for us is nothing more than a dead end.

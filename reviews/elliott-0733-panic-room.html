Author-verified transcription

Four concrete walls and three inches of reinforced steel combine to make an impenetrable fortress in The Panic Room. It is a place designed to be a safe haven in times of terror. But, as we see in this film, such a room won't necessarily cause the terror to leave.

Jodie Foster (Contact) stars as Meg Altman, a recently divorced woman who, with her young, smart-aleck daughter Sarah (Kristen Stewart, The Safety of Objects) move into a huge Manhattan brownstone once owned by an eccentric millionaire. Among its features is a "safe room." With its separate ventilation system, phone line, and surveillance monitors, it was intended as the ultimate security device against home invasion. As we should have expected, Meg and Sarah will have need to use it on their very first night.

When three burglars break into the brownstone in search of something the millionaire left behind, Meg and Sarah manage to seal themselves in the safe room. Unfortunately, the object the crooks are after is sealed in the room with the girls. Thus the standoff is formed: An immovable object (the safe room) meets an irresistible force (the greed of the crooks). What follows is grounded by the admirable performance of Jodie Foster.

Fincher's highly stylized camera work which freely roams though ceilings and walls serves to emphasize the restraints those physical barriers place upon the central characters who are forced to think of more conventional means by which to overcome their confinements and reach their respective goals.

Once the hook is set, the tension never lets up. The reason the tension is so strong is because we care about what happens to these characters. Foster is wonderful in a "Die Hard" kind of role. Her Meg is an intelligent, resourceful woman who is smart enough to be afraid yet brave enough to continue to function despite her fear. Her close-knit relationship with her daughter is recognizably real.

The movie is further helped by the casting of the triad of bad guys. Burnham (Forest Whitaker, Ghost Dog: The Way of the Samurai), a gentle giant of a man, is the brains of the outfit as well as the most sympathetic of the three. He appears to be operating from a genuine though unexpressed need. Junior (Jared Leto, Requiem for a Dream) is more excitable, greedier, and the least intelligent of the three. The wild card is Raoul (country singer Dwight Yoakam), a last minute addition to the caper. Armed with a ski mask, automatic weapon, and a quiet psychotic manner, the threat of violence is never far away as long as he is in the scene.

Screenwriter David Koepp (Stir of Echoes) blends all the essential ingredients together in such a way that our interest is immediately captured. Nothing seems far-fetched or unreasonable. Though Meg occasionally must leave the "safe room" and put herself at risk, the reasons Koepp gives her are fully believable and in keeping with her character.

We are told that "safe rooms" are becoming more and more popular in these fear-filled and crime-laden days. The desire for a sense of peace and security that comes from having a safe haven in times of trouble has led to the development of this multibillion dollar industry. We who believe in the promises of God know that our safe haven has already been purchased for us.

The name of the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe. Proverbs 18:10 (KJV)

There is no place safer; nowhere more secure than under the protective shield of our heavenly Father. Turning to Him in times of trouble as well as in times of joy activates the greatest security system ever designed. When in the safety of His "safe room," fear or terror is completely eradicated, crowded out by the pureness of love that fills every inch and corner of our hearts.

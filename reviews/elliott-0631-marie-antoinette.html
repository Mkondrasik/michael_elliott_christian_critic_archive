It is very difficult to depict boredom on the screen without subjecting one's audience to the same fate. Sofia Coppola is the most recent director to accept this challenge. Like so many others before her, she fails.

Marie Antoinette is her attempt to breathe a bit of life to this 18th century historically-maligned figure. Based on the book by Antonia Fraser, Antoinette (Kirsten Dunst) was a mere child who, at fourteen, was thrown into the deep end of a pool of socio/political waters without a single swimming lesson.

She was fourteen when she left her Austrian home to commence her arranged marriage to young Louis (Jason Schwartzman), prince of France and heir to the throne. They were still children when the king (Rip Torn) dies, leaving the inexperienced kids in charge of France.

Louis, more interested in key-making and hunting than in performing his duties as king or husband, is not much of a presence in her life and virtually invisible in the bedroom. The latter is not good news for a foreign-born queen whose sole responsibility is to produce an heir.

Watched by the royal court at every turn, but ignored by her husband, the young girl had little to do except eat and shop which she did regularly and which we get to watch repeatedly.

Kirsten Dunst does little with a role that demands little. Though the set design and art direction are superb, capturing the look and feel of Versailles at the time of the American Revolution, Dunst and many of her co-stars look like modern players plopped in front of an antique setting.

Schwartzman plays Louis as a boy-king pretending to be a man. He's befuddled by the sexual thing, confused by matters of state, and numb to the trappings of the royal office. He makes some interesting choices but needs a driving force to pull him along and Coppola doesn't provide him one.

The anachronistic soundtrack, which includes selections by Bow Wow Wow, The Cure and Adam Ant, is glaringly inappropriate and stops us from being drawn into the movie's world. At first, I thought I might have entered the wrong theater as the lights went out and some rock anthem began to play. Coppola wanted to connect us with the teenage perspective of her main character but it was a poor choice.

Coppola begins her screenplay with Antoinette's arrival in France and ends it with her being forced to leave the palace. There's much more to the story and leaves us with the odd feeling of wanting both less and more - less of the story we've just seen and more of the story she has chosen not to tell.

Leadership is a quality that is not necessarily age-dependent. Solomon assumed his throne at the still tender age of twenty and was widely known for his wisdom and the prosperity of his land. The apostle Paul gave his young student Timothy these words of advice:

Let no man despise thy youth; but be thou an example of the believers, in word, in conversation, in charity, in spirit, in faith, in purity. 1 Timothy 4:12 (KJV)

In his second letter to Timothy, Paul followed up with this reminder:

Flee also youthful lusts: but follow righteousness, faith, charity, peace, with them that call on the Lord out of a pure heart. 2 Timothy 2:22 (KJV)

Even though scripture says that "foolishness is bound in the heart of a child," one's age is no excuse for being foolish. We can rise above and assume our responsibilities (be they royal duties or household chores) by renewing our minds to what God says we should do.

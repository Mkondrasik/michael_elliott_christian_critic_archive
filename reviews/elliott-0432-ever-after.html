Author-verified transcription

Here's an idea whose time has come... Fairy tales without fairies. Ever After tells the "real" story of Cinderella. There's no pumpkin coaches, no mice that turn into white stallions, no casting of spells by a kindly fairy godmother. Just a simple story of a sweet and beautiful peasant girl who happens to fall in love with a prince.

Sooty little Danielle is devoted to her father who marries the baroness Rodmilla and then promptly drops dead, leaving Danielle with a new stepmother and two new stepsisters that she barely knows.

Ten years later, we find Danielle waiting tables and doing laundry as a servant while the three "steps" now sit as the mistresses of the manor.

Enter the man who would NOT be king, the disillusioned Prince Henry. Unhappily bound by his royal duty to marry the daughter of the king of Spain, he is literally knocked off his horse while first meeting Danielle, then figuratively swept off his feet after the second meeting. The thing is, he believes her to be a part of the privileged class, a role Danielle was playing in order to free one of her fellow servants sold by Rodmilla to pay off a debt.

Henry, having been given a deadline by his father (if he hasn't found a wife by the time of the masked ball, he will have to marry the Spanish princess), fixes his heart upon Danielle's fictional countess who, he says shows more passion in one memory than he has experienced in a lifetime.

Drew Barrymore plays Danielle with the same sweetness and earnestness that worked so well for her in The Wedding Singer. She has been quite effective in redefining herself as a viable romantic lead.

Anjelica Huston avoids the "evil" stepmother stereotype by giving Rodmilla some unexpected depth. There's a genuine air of sadness that hangs over her character that, while not excusing her actions, helps us to understand them.

Newcomer Megan Dodds plays the spoiled and selfish stepsister, Marguerite, who is being groomed by Rodmilla for a good, noble (and lucrative) marriage. In a twist of the original story, the second stepsister, Jacqueline (Melanie Lynskey, Heavenly Creatures) is sympathetic to Danielle, having been similarly dismissed by her mother in favor of her more attractive sister.

Dougray Scott (Deep Impact) finds the proper ennui for the prince who is revitalized by the energized life of this young peasant girl. His accent and demeanor are not consistent with the 16 century setting but, for that matter, neither is Miss Barrymore's.

Director Andy Tennant (Fools Rush In) is a bit heavy handed with the camera, and the pace of the movie is unnecessarily slow, but still the sweetness of the tale comes through, making for a light and inoffensive film that the whole family can enjoy.

What may be amazing to some is the fact that the story loses little by eliminating al the "magic" and fantasy aspects from the tale. As a matter of fact, I found it to be a welcome change. A story grounded in reality is far healthier than a story encouraging mysticism and magic. Yet, we continually add these elements for the benefit(?) of our wide-eyed and impressionable children.

Even when explaining spiritual matters, many parents resort to "fairy tales." Christmas becomes a time for flying reindeer and playful elves.

Easter finds a giant rabbit hiding brightly colored eggs. Such folklore tales do both God and our children an injustice.

But refuse profane and old wives' fables, and exercise thyself rather unto godliness. 1 Timothy 4:7 [KJV]

We have the ability to build and strengthen our children's minds with the power of God's truth.

In Danielle's story, they can see and emulate the godly attributes of goodness, integrity, and perseverance. Or, they can emulate Cinderella's action in the fairy tale version and wait for their godmother to wave a magic wand. Both versions will entertain, but which version is more beneficial?

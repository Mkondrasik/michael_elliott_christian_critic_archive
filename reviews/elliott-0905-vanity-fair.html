The danger of adapting classics for the screen is the inevitable comparison that takes place between the finished production and the familiar literary source. Vanity Fair neatly skirts that issue with little effort because, quite frankly, few people nowadays bother to read the admittedly acquired taste of William Makepeace Thackery and so are not familiar with the tale.

His early 19th century serialized story, Vanity Fair, was published with the subtitle, "a Novel without a Hero." It can also be said that it doesn't really have a villain either. Each of the characters in this story is for the most part, their own worst enemy. Driven by such lowly traits as greed, status, vanity, pride and pettiness, every character makes plenty of mistakes and must learn to deal with the consequences.

At the center of the story is Becky Sharp (Reese Witherspoon), an orphan from parents who are less than working class. They were artisans. Still, she aspires to be accepted into higher society - an ambition considered to be beyond reach.

While growing up at Miss Pinkerton's Academy for Young Ladies, where she is treated more as an indentured servant, she meets her only friend, Amelia Sedley (Romola Garai), the daughter of a wealthy merchant. They leave the academy together - Amelia to marry well and live a life of privilege; Becky to begin her new job as the governess for the children of Sir Pitt Crawley (Bob Hoskins) a gentleman clearly on the bottom rung of acceptable lineage.

From there, Becky begins to scheme and plot her way to a higher social status. As one character puts it, she's not just social climbing - she's mountaineering. As she ascends into the not-so-polite society of upper class England, she leaves in her wake a stream of broken hearts, wagging tongues, and disapproving looks.

Reese Witherspoon is very good in communicating the intelligence of Becky and the unflagging ambition that drives her while at the same time keeping her personally appealing to the audience. Despite her flaws and negative traits, she remains sympathetic.

She is supported by an ensemble cast which is uniformly excellent. In addition to Romola Garai and Bob Hoskins, the cast includes the estimable Dame Eileen Atkins, Academy Award winner Jim Broadbent, Gabriel Byrne, Rhys Ifans, Geraldine McEwan, and Jonathan Rhys Meyers.

Director Mira Nair (Monsoon Wedding) and her crew exceed expectations in how well they portray early 19th century England (as well as India, Germany and Brussels). The script, cowritten by Gosford Park scribe Julian Fellowes, neatly condenses Thackery's 900 page epic to its key elements and although it does whitewash some of the more unsavory aspects of the tale, those decisions are well within the Victorian practice.

"Ah! Vanitas Vanitatum! [Vanity of Vanities!] Which of us is happy in this world? Which of us has his desire? Or, having it, is satisfied?" With these words, William Thackery ends his novel and leaves us with a worthy question to consider. It is a question wiser men than I have contemplated.

Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity. Ecclesiastes 1:2 (KJV)

In Ecclesiastes, we follow a man's journey as he seeks the meaning of life itself. He learns that seeking personal satisfaction from this world is a vain and futile exercise. The satisfaction that brings peace and rest to the soul is not available from this world. It is only available to those to set their sights higher.

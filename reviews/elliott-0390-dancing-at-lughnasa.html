Author-verified transcription

The problem of dancing with wild abandon is that awkward moment when the music stops and one must once again come face to face with reality. For the Mundy sisters of Dancing at Lughnasa, the harsh reality of their lives in 1939 Ireland is not something that can be so simply danced away.

The five Mundy sisters live together along with 8-year old Michael, the illegitimate son of Christina (Catherine McCormack, Dangerous Beauty), who is the youngest of the Mundy girls. Michael is the narrator of the film and the one through whose eyes the story is told.

Eldest daughter Kate (Meryl Streep, The Bridges of Madison County) assumes the matriarch's role as head of the house, which she rules authoritatively. Her none too secure position as a schoolteacher provides the bulk of the household's income.

Kathy Burke (Elizabeth) plays Maggie, who somehow manages to hold this tenuous household together on the meager income that the five sisters share. Brid Brennan (Saint-Ex) plays the soft-spoken Agnes whose deliberate quietness belies an inner strength. Sophie Thompson (Emma) is Rose, the slow, dim-witted sister, whom the others love and protect.

The title Dancing at Lughnasa comes from a celebration honoring Lugh, the pagan god of light and music. It is a strange title for a movie featuring a family of Irish Roman Catholics, but, as adapted from the award winning autobiographical play by Brian Friel, it is a strange little tale that it tells. (Or better said, tales, for there are more than one.)

One of them concerns their brother Jack (Michael Gambon, The Wings of the Dove), an ordained priest who's been called home after a long stay in Africa where he was serving as a missionary. In the eyes of the church, it could not have been a very successful mission as the only conversion that appears to have taken place was Jack's conversion to the natives' pagan rites and rituals.

Other story lines include the surprise return of Michael's free-spirited father, the married Danny Bradley's "courtship" of Rose, the opening of a factory in their small town which will have a negative impact on the sisters' meager livelihood, and the possibility of Kate losing her job as well.

Director Pat O'Connor (Inventing The Abbotts) and screenwriter Frank McGuinness weave these story lines together neatly, thanks largely to the ensemble play of their cast. All five actresses give us rich, unselfish, and thoroughly engrossing performances. Ms. Streep continues to impress us with the completeness with which she can inhabit a character. Ms. Thompson is brilliant as the simple and single minded Rose. Mr. Gambon is appropriately befuddled as the priest who has returned from Africa indelibly changed.

His character's situation is not all that unique. Certainly, it has happened in the past. It happened as early as the first century Christian church. Many of our Christian "traditions" today come from pagan origins as the early believers allowed these influences to mingle with the church doctrine. The holiday of Christmas, for example, is loaded with pagan aspects. The Christmas tree, the Yule log, the exchanging of gifts were all pagan practices which Christianity simply adopted as their own. Even the date of December 25th was chosen by the church because it coincided with the pagan festival of Saturnalia.

God gave instructions and warning to those who go into the world to share His Word:

And whosoever shall not receive you, nor hear your words, when ye depart out of that house or city, shake off the dust of your feet. Matthew 10:14

The unbelieving world has little to offer us. We have everything to offer them. But if they choose not to accept what we have, we are commanded to depart from them lest we allow their unbelief to weaken our spiritual resolve. Father Jack forgot a very basic commandment of God's:

Thou shalt fear [respect] the LORD thy God, and serve him, and shalt swear by his name. Ye shall not go after other gods, of the gods of the people which are round about you; Deuteronomy 6:13-14 (KJV)

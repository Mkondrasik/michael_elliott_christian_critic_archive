Birth

When a person loses something dear to them, the emptiness it causes can be so great that he is willing to consider the improbable and believe the impossible if it might mean recovering the hope of filling that void. It is the only explanation for what happens in the unusual new drama, Birth.

Nicole Kidman stars as Anna, a woman still emotionally grieving for Sean, her dead husband. Ten years ago he collapsed while jogging and died of a heart attack. Joseph (Danny Huston) has patiently been waiting for her to resume her life and with the persistence of an insurance salesman, finally gets her to agree to marry him.

He did not expect a ten year old boy (Cameron Bright) to appear and disrupt their wedding plans by announcing that he is the reincarnated soul of Anna’s first husband. He solemnly orders Anna not to marry Joseph.

Discounted at first, the boy’s claims nevertheless have an impact on Anna’s emotions as they dredge up the past and remind her of the life she once had with her beloved Sean. As time passes and this new Sean demonstrates knowledge of a history with Anna that he should not remember, she finds herself more and more willing to consider the possibility that he’s telling the truth.

Director Jonathan Glazer (Sexy Beast) encases his film with a certain gravitas that sets a creepy mood and atmosphere within which the characters never seem quite comfortable or settled. While it can hardly be considered a paranormal film, the atmosphere he creates certainly works for this odd and often uncomfortable material. The conclusion is a disappointing as Glazer opts for a convenient escape from the metaphysical dilemma of his characters.

The eeriness of the film is further enhanced by little Cameron Bright (Godsend) who adopts an adult seriousness which contributes to throwing the grown-ups around him off balance.

Not that they aren’t a bit unstable already. Everyone seems to be walking on eggshells around Anna lest they trigger an emotional reaction. It’s as if they figure that if the only thing she can feel is sadness, it’s better for her not to feel anything.

Thus, most characters we meet have a practiced and superficial manner about them as they interact with Anna. Clearly, the film’s focus is upon her and Nicole Kidman gives a calculated and rather icy performance as the emotionally vulnerable Anna. In one extended scene, the camera rests on a close up of the actress as she listens to a symphonic orchestra. She hardly twitches a facial muscle and yet conveys volumes of internal dialogue.

The film is rated R for sexuality and there are scenes between the 10 year old Bright and the 37 year old Kidman which, though filmed with considerable restraint, are sure to draw controversy and criticism.

The key to the film is Anna’s grief which serves as the catalyst for her actions and the decisions she makes. Because of her intense grief, she idealizes the memory of her dead husband and their relationship together. In her mind, she changes the truth of what was and that affects her perceived reality.

Having a source of truth that exists outside of our selves - one that is not dependent upon our experiences or our emotions helps us to make wise decisions even at times of high stress. Truth must be unchanged and unchanging. In that way, we guard against the very real possibility that we may deceive ourselves because of what we want the truth to be.

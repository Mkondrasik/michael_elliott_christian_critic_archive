“You’re a child... that’s what makes you so scary.” It is a line delivered near the end of The Last King of Scotland which, despite its title, is about the murderous reign of Idi Amin, onetime president of the African nation of Uganda.

Using the device of a fictional character, director Kevin MacDonald and screenwriter Jeremy Brock manage to take us inside the political court of this madman perched upon a seat of power. It is a chilling and brutally graphic look at a charming killer who strutted upon the world’s stage presenting one face to the media while behind the scenes he was orchestrating the deaths of 300,000 of his countrymen.

The fictitious character is Nicolas Garrigan (James McAvoy), a Scottish medical student who, after his med school graduation, realized that he didn’t want to settle down in a boring family practice. On a whim, he heads for Uganda and begins to assist in the many medical needs of the natives there. By chance, he is called to tend to an accidental wound of Idi Amin (Forest Whitaker), the new president.

Amin is taken by the brash foreigner and soon makes him an offer he can’t refuse: the position of presidential physician and close advisor. Garrigan accepts the offer and is initially charmed by this gregarious man although hints of something behind the man’s smile and affability are clearly evident lurking just beneath the surface.

Forest Whitaker gives a tour-de-force performance as the man-child turned brutal dictator. It is a fine line that Whitaker walks but he walks it without a single misstep. He, and director Kevin MacDonald, wisely show us Amin through the eyes of the naïve Garrigan, keeping the atrocities from us (and him) until the final third of the picture.

James McAvoy, as Garrigan, is impressive only in hindsight. During the film he seems serviceable but it is only until after the film has completed that we understand the complexity of his role and the adeptness with which it was handled.

The rest of the cast (including Kerry Washington, Gillian Anderson, and Simon McBurney) fill supporting roles and, though they fill them ably, it feels like they are simply taking up space and time until Amin and Garrigan reappear to continue their odd couple relationship.

It should be said that there are moments of severe brutality, torture and gruesome images. While such scenes are withheld from us until the final moments, when they arrive they are no less potent or disturbing.

The duplicity of Idi Amin is chilling because we too are taken in by the man’s superficial charm and boyishness. Like Garrigan, we at first see no evidence of no do we suspect that this emotionally needy man is capable of such barbaric acts of pure evil.

But we shouldn’t be surprised by them. Evil must hide its face because no rational man or woman would accept it otherwise. Our spiritual adversary does not operate in the open but rather lurks behind the scenes, marshaling and coordinating his attacks in secret.

Hide me from the secret counsel of the wicked; from the insurrection of the workers of iniquity: Who whet their tongue like a sword, and bend their bows to shoot their arrows, even bitter words: That they may shoot in secret at the perfect: suddenly do they shoot at him, and fear not. They encourage themselves in an evil matter: they commune of laying snares privily; they say, Who shall see them? Psalms 64:2-5 (KJV)

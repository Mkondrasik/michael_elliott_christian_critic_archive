Bridge to Terabithia

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 16, 2007

3 stars

1 hr: 35 min

Author-verified transcription

I was sandbagged. Never having read the Newbery Award-winning Bridge to Terabithia by Kathleen Paterson, my preconception of the film was based solely on the trailers that advertised the movie. Based on them, I was expecting a CGI-heavy children's adventure romp through fantasyland. That is not what this film is about. Not by a long shot.

This coming-of-age tale follows a young middle-schooler named Jess Aarons (Josh Hutcherson) who is experiencing the typical middle school woes. Bullies pick on him; he is forced to wear his sister's hand-me-downs because his working class parents can't afford new clothes; he has a little sister that loves to tag along; and he's developing his first puppy love crush on his music teacher...

Jess has two escapes from his life. He likes to draw pictures from his fertile imagination; and he likes to run... fast. After training hard to win the fastest kid in school race, Jess loses at the wire to the new girl in town, Leslie Burke (AnnaSophia Robb). The product of liberal artistic types, Leslie dances to a different drummer and Jess, at first, is put off by her odd outlook on life. But he slowly comes to recognize that she shares the same vivid imagination as he and they become the best of friends.

Much of their time is spent on the other side of the creek in an unexplored section of wood they nickname Terabithia. There, their imagination is allowed to run free although elements of their school life reality will often infiltrate into their play world.

It is when reality becomes too strong to ignore that the story takes a turn that provides parents with an opening to address some serious subjects with their teens and pre-teens. I mention this only because a film that is this good at what it does should not be marketed as a kid's science fiction adventure. The kids expecting such a film will be disappointed and their parents will be ill-prepared to capitalize on the opportunity the movie actually presents.

Child actors Josh Hutcherson (Zathura) and AnnaSophia Robb (Because of Winn Dixie) light up the screen like a couple of pros. Their chemistry makes this film work in ways that the producers could only dream about but never predict. Kudos to the casting agents that managed to put these two together.

Other actors who share the screen include Robert Patrick as Jess's blue collar dad who is trying to keep his son's head out of the clouds; Zooey Deschanel as a music teacher who captures Jess's adolescent fancy; and Bailee Madison as Jess's precocious little sister.

There's a great deal that I could say and relate to the scriptures but the filmmakers have requested that we not reveal certain elements of the plot. To honor their request, I'll steer clear of the subjects that have them concerned. Instead, I'll address a non-revelatory side-issue that does present itself during the course of the film.

Both Jess and Leslie are bothered by an upper grade female bully (Lauren Clinton) whom they successfully (though surreptitiously) retaliate against. When their plan succeeds too well, they are forced to actually come face to face with the person behind the bully. There, they learn that all is not what it may seem. That is a lesson that God would have us all learn.

Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such a one in the spirit of meekness. Galatians 6:1a (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

Some of the hottest young actors working in Hollywood today share the screen in the action/suspense/comedy Suicide Kings. As the elongated descriptive suggests, this is a hard movie to categorize.

The plot is ingenious. The daughter from a wealthy family is kidnapped and her ransom is set at two million dollars. Her brother, knowing that the family doesn't have that kind of money, enlists the aid of his friends to launch a daring scheme that involves the kidnapping of a prominent mob figure. Their rationalization is that he will either be able to pay the ransom or use his underworld contacts to find the girl. Either way, their problems are solved. At least, that's what they'd like to think.

On the surface, the plot would suggest to be a straightforward suspense thriller. That is until we realize that these rich boys are not your run-of-the-mill kidnappers. Then again, their victim is not your everyday hostage.

Henry Thomas (E.T.), Sean Patrick Flanery (Powder), Jay Mohr (Picture Perfect), Jeremy Sisto (Moonlight and Valentino), and Johnny Galecki (I Know What You Did Last Summer) play the boyhood chums turned bumbling criminals as they execute their plan with something considerably less than precision. Christopher Walken (The Deer Hunter) takes on the role of Carlo Bartolucci aka Charlie Barrett, the retired crime boss turned captive, who strangely takes a bemused liking to his young captors. Also on hand is Denis Leary (Wag the Dog), who nearly steals the movie in his role of Lono Veccio, Bartolucci's driver, bodyguard, and footwear aficionado.

The language used throughout the film is rough. Gutter talk and street lingo (especially that dreaded "F" word) are liberally splayed about. The comedy is dark, based on a perverted sense of right and wrong as held by obviously amoral characters. Because of the baser elements depicted in the film, some may find it offensive.

The acting, however, is superb. Mr. Walken turns in his best performance in years as a criminal kingpin who has apparently gone straight (or, at least, straighter than he was). Though he spends the majority of his screen time duct-taped to a chair, his solid characterization is what helps to keep the movie grounded while moving forward at a steady clip. Messrs. Thomas, Flanery, Galecki, Sisko, and Mohr all do superior ensemble work under the sure and confident hand of Peter O'Fallon, who is making his feature film directorial debut.

The clever title, Suicide Kings, has a double meaning. It is a phrase used in poker to describe the king of hearts, oftentimes called as a wild card in certain games. Of course, the phrase could also describe a group of young men foolish enough to mess with the likes of a Carlo Bartolucci. Mobsters tend to have an adverse reaction to being strapped down in a chair while their ring is removed from them (along with the finger upon which it sits). To even attempt such an ill-advised enterprise could be considered an extreme form of suicide. Surely one would have to expect there to be swift and permanent reprisals.

However, to the movie's credit, what one expects and what one gets are quite different. Suicide Kings has its share of surprises and plot twists that keep us guessing up until the final scene.

Certainly the killing, the violence, the language, and the other acts which these characters perform are nothing to extol. But within the context of the story line, we can perhaps find a few nuggets of truth upon which we can dwell.

At one point, as kidnapped Carlo Bartolucci plays poker with his young abductors, he gives them this piece of advice: "The difference between winning and losing is being able to read your opponent." That certainly holds true in the spiritual competition of which we are all a part. The more we know about the devil and how he operates, the better prepared we are to stand against him - to counter his moves and attacks. This is one reason why the study of the Word of God is so vitally important for our lives.

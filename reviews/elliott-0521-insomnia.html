Author-verified transcription

Director Christopher Nolan follows his impressively original hit Memento with, of all things, a remake. Insomnia is based upon a 1997 Norwegian crime drama starring Stellan Skarsgaard and follows a murder investigation taking place in the endless daylight of a small Alaskan town.

Detectives Dormer (Al Pacino, Any Given Sunday) and Eckhart (Martin Donovan, Pipe Dream), on loan from the LAPD homicide division, come to Nightmute, Alaska to help the locals find a killer who beat a young high school student to death. Their assignment has less to do with interstate cooperation than simple survival. The LAPD's Internal Affairs Division is busy trying to stir things up back home to uncover whatever dirt might be lying under the surface of Dormer's impressive arrest record so leaving town under the pretense of a murder investigation seems the prudent thing to do.

In Alaska, Dormer is surprised to learn that Eckhart is preparing to make his own deal with Internal Affairs. It is a deal that will spell trouble for the good but unconventional Dormer who, in the pursuit of justice, has been known to break a rule or two.

When a trap set for Walter Finch (Robin Williams, Death to Smoochy), the suspected killer of the Alaskan teen, misfires, Eckhart is accidentally shot and killed... or was it an accident? Did Dormer shoot his own partner to prevent an Internal Affairs investigation? Finch seems to think so as he contacts Dormer to strike his own deal. He'll keep quiet as to what he witnessed if Dormer will help him pin the murder of the teen on someone else.

Such a cut and dried moral question might seem an easy one to answer but sleep deprivation can have some strange effects on one's reasoning process. Dormer's condition progressively worsens as he spends sleepless night after sleepless night. Is it the fact that at this time of year, the sun never sets in Alaska, or could it be Dormer's conscience that is keeping him up?

Al Pacino, who starts the film by playing Dormer as a weary, beleaguered soul, doesn't look like he's given himself much room to take his character through the physical and mental deterioration to come as a result of the days of sleeplessness ahead of him. Still, Pacino finds the nuances he needs to keep Dormer's insomnia from sapping the energy from his performance. Once again, he is riveting to watch.

Robin Williams abandons his frenetic, rapid-fire persona to play Fitch as a slightly geeky and definitely creepy misfit. While he doesn't make any missteps in his portrayal, he fails to put any kind of distinctive stamp on the role. It is a subdued and rather bland performance, one that could have been turned in by a competent actor working for scale. Offering able support is Hilary Swank (Boys Don't Cry) as a young, inexperienced local cop who is enamored with Dormer's reputation and looks to learn from him.

Nolan uses the eerie Alaskan landscape to great effect and as the movie progresses, he employs flashes of light and repeated quick cuts to suggest the growing erratic behavior of Dormer's sleep-deprived mind.

However, it is the moral question that most arrests our attention. Not that the question is in any way original. It is a question that has been asked and answered for centuries upon centuries: "Do the ends ever justify the means?" To arrive at the answer one only need recognize a simple truth. The means, or actions we take, are within our control. The ends, or the results of our actions, are not. The ends are the fruit which grows as a result of our actions. Godly actions will always reap godly fruit. To get try to get the fruit any other way will result in failure and disappointment... much as Dormer discovers.

For a good tree bringeth not forth corrupt fruit; neither doth a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit. For every tree is known by his own fruit. For of thorns men do not gather figs, nor of a bramble bush gather they grapes. Luke 6:43-44 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

Juno is a quirky comedy about a young teen who irreverently deals with issues that, as she admits, are far beyond her maturity level.

We meet Juno MacGuff (Ellen Page) as she is taking her 3rd self-administered pregnancy test after downing her body weight in Sunny D. The outcome doesn’t change. The smart-talking, too cool for school 16 year old is with child – the result of a one time encounter with her best friend Bleeker (Michael Cera).

Her decision not to terminate the pregnancy and instead deliver the baby so she might tender it to a couple looking to adopt is the core or heart of the story. Screenwriter Diablo Cody shows a great insight into the rebellious teenaged mind and it shows in her cleverly-written screenplay.

Cody’s greatest achievement is avoiding the stereotype while simultaneously acknowledging the stereotypical profile. She plants her characters in the real and multi-dimensional world from which stereotypes derive. We recognize Juno’s “type” immediately, but as the film progresses, we see a depth and vulnerability that we don’t associate with that type. In other words, Cody keeps her characters human and very real.

Juno’s parents (J.K. Simmons and Allison Janney), instead of being clueless, self-centered, one-dimensional cutouts, are caring, hip, though slightly ill-equipped, adults doing the best they can during an unplanned hiccup in Juno’s life caused by a foolish mistake.

Michael Cera plays the boyfriend/father as the kind of teenager I remember from my high school days. Bleeker is still developing – more observing life than really participating in it. He always seems somewhat befuddled – not sure of anything and perfectly happy to stay quiet until he figures it out.

Jason Bateman and Jennifer Garner as the adoptive parents completely “get” their characters and let us discover them slowly – revealing bits and pieces as the storyline unwinds until we get to understand them and the choices they make.

Director Jason Reitman scores a major hit with this film, finding the tone and tenor that brings Cody’s vision to cinematic life and makes it memorable. The film moves along effortlessly - carried by a brilliant star performance from Ellen Page.

Ellen’s delivery of Juno’s snarky remarks, be they part of the dialogue or the ongoing voiceover narration, are pitch-perfect and serve the film just as much as the words that are provided by Cody.

Juno is being touted as being part of a recent trend in films that show young, unmarried women deciding to bring their pregnancies to term instead of opting for abortion. Waitress and Knocked Up are two recent films that also fall into this category.

Juno, to her credit, accepts the full responsibility and endures the stares and derision of those who cannot help but notice her glaringly obvious condition. She devises a plan to make the best of the situation even though that plan may not be the easiest course to follow.

Making mistakes is part of the human experience. The mark of character is in how we react in the face of our mistakes and the aftermath of their consequences. The only way to effectively recover from a mistake is not to dwell on it… we must move past it in order to recover from it.

Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before. Philippians 3:13 (KJV)

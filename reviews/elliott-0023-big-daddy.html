BIG DADDY

Those who named Adam Sandler’s new film, Big Daddy, should have spent a bit more time brainstorming before settling for such a boring and misleading title. Considering the sheer volume of bodily fluids which flow freely throughout this movie, Big River would have been a much more appropriate title. And Big Polluted River would be more accurate still. With Big Daddy, Mr. Sandler (The Waterboy) reestablishes his stranglehold lock as the king of puerile “humor.”

The largely unfunny and totally unbelievable script (written by Mr. Sandler, longtime associate Tim Herlihy, and newcomer Steve Franks) tells the story of Sonny Koufax, a thirty-something year old man who redefines the word “loser,” as he escalates laziness, selfishness and slothfulness to something akin to an art form. Living off of a fraudulent insurance settlement, Sonny spends his days sleeping in, watching cartoons, and reporting to his one-day-a-week job as toll collector. Even though he has graduated from law school, he refuses to take the bar exam.

His slacker lifestyle is beginning to threaten his relationship with his girlfriend Vanessa (Kristy Swanson, The Phantom.) When five year old Julian lands on his doorstep, the surprise result of a sexual dalliance of Sonny’s roommate, Sonny decides to keep the lad as his adopted son and prove to Vanessa that he’s really a grown-up.

However, before Sonny can spring his newfound maturity upon Vanessa, she shows up with a new grand fatherly beau, leaving Sonny to raise the child on his own. The intended “humor” comes from Sonny’s unusual “whatever the kid does is OK” child-rearing techniques which include the covering of any fluid accidents with sheets of newspaper, letting the child urinate on the wall of any public establishment refusing them access to their bathroom, and teaching him how to help “initiate the conversation” when picking up chicks. The movie takes the expected schmaltzy turn when the boy’s custody becomes a legal issue. The resulting courtroom scene turns out to be both embarrassingly bad and deathly dull.

Adam Sandler fans will be happy to learn that he brings all his irritating shtick with him: the whining delivery, the ill-mannered behavior, and the inappropriate comments are all represented in force. An example of one of the tiresome running gags has to do with Sonny’s roommate being engaged to an ex-waitress from... Get this... Hooters! (I’ll pause while you double over with laughter.)

Brothers Cole and Dylan Sprouse (TV’s Grace Under Fire) who share the acting credit for the role of the “urinally-challenged” Julian, show very little personality and for this reason, the bond which forms between Sonny and Julian is contrived and unbelievable.

Rob Schneider (The Waterboy) and Steve Buscemi (Armageddon) appear briefly in rather inconsequential roles and Jon Stewart (The Daily Show) and Leslie Mann (George of the Jungle) are given one-dimensional characters with little to do.

The only bright spot in the film is the genuine sweetness of Joey Lauren Adams (Chasing Amy) who serves as the love interest for Sonny while he learns how to be a parent. Her work is reminiscent of Drew Barrymore’s in The Wedding Singer or Renee Zellwinger’s in Jerry Maguire.

Sonny’s parenting philosophy of “let the child do what he wants” is, of course, pure insanity. Children require both nurturing and admonition. They need to be corrected, taught, reproved, taught, encouraged, taught, and taught some more.

A parent’s primary responsibility is to teach his or her child the proper standard for life and living. In other words, the difference between right and wrong. Everything else pales in comparison. Without a true standard, life erodes into chaos and confusion, two qualities that are definitely described as being devilish.

Big Daddy never comes close to illustrating the true responsibilities of a parent. Thankfully, this daddy will not be remembered come Father’s Day.

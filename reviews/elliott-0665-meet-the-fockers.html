Author-verified transcription

The number one rule of comedy should be to know when to move on. The folks behind the making of Meet the Fockers must not have read the rule book and so we get the same joke (or variations of it) delivered time and time again until it long ceases to be amusing.

Focker is the much maligned family name of Gaylord (Ben Stiller) who after many embarrassing moments has managed to ingratiate himself into the "circle of trust" established by the Byrne family patriarch, Jack (Robert De Niro). Now with Gaylord's wedding to Jack's daughter Pam (Teri Polo) just around the corner, there's only one last detail to take care of... meeting the parents of the groom.

Gaylord and the Byrnes pile into Jack's new armor-plated RV and head to Coconut Grove to meet the Fockers who manage to live up to their colorful name. Bernie (Dustin Hoffman) is an outgoing, Florida-tanned ex-lawyer turned stay-at-home Dad. Roz (Barbra Streisand) is an outspoken sex therapist specializing in senior sexuality and apt to dole out free advice at any given time. Their free-spirited zest for life is a direct contrast to the more conservative (uptight) Byrnes.

It doesn't take long for tensions to rise, for embarrassments to occur, and for us to realize that comedic value of the name Focker definitely has its limits.

Despite some brilliant casting and a decent concept this sequel to the 2002 hit Meet the Parents is a disappointment. Whereas the first film was a hilarious comedy of errors, Meet the Fockers is full of obvious wordplay, sexual innuendo and crudities inserted into the script as a poor substitute for wit.

The bright spot in an otherwise below par effort is the inspired pairing of Dustin Hoffman and Barbra Streisand as Focker's parents. While they aren't given much of a character arc to develop they do work well together and leave us wishing the film were about them instead of featuring them as supporting cast members.

A third generation is represented in the film as Spencer and Bradley Pickren alternate as Little Jack, Pam's breast-fixated nephew.

De Niro deadpans his way through an easily forgettable performance. As the stern, ex-CIA operative with a soft spot for his family, he isn't as intimidating as he was in the first film which lessens much of the comedic payoff.

His character's "circle of trust" is ultimately exposed to be a sham. As one of the characters explains, "The circle doesn't work if you don't trust the people who are in it."

Jack's "trust" only extended to those who were willing to submit their will to his own. A true trusting relationship is a two-way street. It requires both parties to be cognizant and aware of the needs and concerns of the other.

Coercion by intimidation or fear may bring consent but it will never build trust or love. Instead of establishing a strong familial bond, all Jack succeeded in doing was to weaken the ties that should have brought his family closer together.

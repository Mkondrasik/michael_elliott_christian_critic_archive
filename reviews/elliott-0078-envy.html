Envy

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 30, 2004
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Envy; covetousness; contentment; thankfulness

Author-verified transcription

The green-eyed monster returned to the theaters last weekend and no, we’re not talking about Shrek or the Incredible Hulk. Envy, a new comedy from Barry Levinson, opened nationwide but the emotion it evoked from the few who saw it was nowhere near envy. It was closer to boredom.

Tim Dingman (Ben Stiller) and Nick Vanderpark (Jack Black) are best friends, neighbors, and co-workers. Tim is on the fast track to middle management while Nick is being held back due to his chronic lack of focus. He’s too much of a dreamer to be management material.

When one of Nick’s ideas actually pans out, he is catapulted into the rarified stratosphere of the filthy rich. Because of his close kinship to Tim, he decides not to move. Instead he builds his mansion next door and inadvertently rubs his success in his friend’s nose on a daily basis.

Tim, who ridiculed his friend’s money-making idea of an aerosol spray that causes dog waste to disappear, is constantly reminded that, had he supported and invested in the invention, he too could be living the life of Vanderpark.

The script, by Steve Adams whose only other writing credit appears to be for a SNL-like TV comedy series, consists primarily of different variations of the same fecal-inspired joke stretched out over 90 minutes.

The talent assembled for the film is impressive and should have resulted in something more entertaining than what we get. Both Jack Black and Ben Stiller have had their comedic hits. Envy may be something of a prophetic title because surely all involved must realize that this film isn’t the equal to the sum of its parts.

The trick to situational comedy is that once the situation is set up, which Envy does very nicely, it is supposed to be followed by comedy. Time after time, Envy misses the mark, letting the set up to what should be a humorous payoff simply die on the vine.

Stiller does his slow, agonizing burn while Black resorts to his wild-eyed, impish and overly emotional ways. Too often, they seem to have been left to their own devices - vamping their way through improvised dialogue which lacks wit and direction.

As their wives, Rachel Weisz and Amy Poehler are barely used and offer little in terms of support or characterization. Weisz is especially cast adrift with nothing to anchor her character. Poehler is given a bit of a bone, playing a Nick’s dim bulb wife who decides to run for the “little” Congress (she means the State Senate) in order to give something back. It is a character thread that inexplicably unravels halfway through the film.

Only Christopher Walken seems able to rise above the material, as an eccentric barfly who attaches himself to Dingman. Yet, as the film draws (thankfully) to a close, he is dispatched ignominiously, wasting yet another set up for humor.

We don’t have to look far in order to find a biblical or spiritual analogy to the film. God is aware of all human frailties and addresses them in his Word. Envy is a negative trait that, as we see in the film, can eat away at a person’s soul and affect every aspect of his life.

A sound heart is the life of the flesh: but envy the rottenness of the bones. Proverbs 14:30 (KJV)

The avoidance of envy is so fundamental to a righteous mindset that God included it in the first set of laws he recorded. He told us that we should not covet (desire or take pleasure in) anything that belongs to our neighbor.

We don’t need to look at what others have to determine what we “need.” God promises to be our sufficiency and to meet all of our needs. We must learn to be content and thankful for what we have. Only then will we be in the right frame of mind to receive more.

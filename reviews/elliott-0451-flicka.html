Author-verified transcription

Flicka not only speaks to the horse lover that may reside somewhere within each of us, it triggers a yearning to sometimes break the rules - when the rules go against our deeply held beliefs.

Adapted from the 1941 story by Mary O'Hara, Flicka follows a stubborn, strong-willed teenage girl named Katy McLaughlin (Alison Lohman). Though enrolled in a private school to prepare her for college, she can't keep her mind from drifting back to her father's ranch and his herd of horses.

As soon as she is on break, she returns to the ranch and immediately begins riding. One morning, Katy encounters a mountain lion that spooks her horse and would have killed her had a wild mustang not come along to scare it off. From that moment on, unbeknownst to the animal, the mustang she names "Flicka" (Swedish for pretty young girl) becomes hers. All she has to do now is catch her and tame her.

None of this pleases her father (Tim McGraw) who pragmatically sees Flicka as a danger to his family and his herd. He forbids Katy to have anything to do with the mustang. When she disobeys, he sells Flicka to a rodeo for them to use in their crowd-pleasing wild horse races. Flicka relies on the same time-tested formula as Lassie, Old Yeller, and any other kid and his/her beloved pet movie we could bring to mind. The formula does work although Flicka doesn't always mix the ingredients as well as it might.

Alison Lohman (actual age 27) does a credible job as a teenage cowgirl. Although she sometimes pushes the emotional meter into the extreme ranges, she seems to be doing it because the script (by Mark Rosenthal and Lawrence Konner) doesn't always do it for her. It would have been a much flatter and unrewarding experience if the actors had not brought so much extra to their characters.

Country singer/actor Tim McGraw does some decent work as the patriarch who sees his way of life slowly vanishing from the societal landscape. And yet it is a lifestyle he is not ready to let go. He's prepared to hand the ranch over to his son, Howard (Ryan Kwanten) who isn't all that excited about receiving it. Howard wants to go to college back east but isn't sure how to tell his dad.

In an ironic scene, the father shares with his boy how every animal knows how to communicate what it needs. We just need to learn how to listen to them so we can understand. Those are wise words that, for most of the film, aren't practiced. The father is blindsided when the son finally reveals that he hates ranching. Even after this news, the father remains slow to see how much his daughter lives for it and possesses the kind of independent spirit needed to be successful at it.

Most parents want the best for their children and will work hard to try to provide it for them. In doing so, we should ask ourselves if we are helping them to find and fulfill their own dreams or simply forcing our dreams upon them. As parents, we learn to teach, inspire, lead, and encourage our kids. The hardest thing to do is to learn to let go. And yet, if we've done our jobs well, holding on too long can destroy all the work we've done up to that point. Letting go and watching our children take full control of their lives is one of the most rewarding experiences a parent will ever have. They may surprise us with how much of us they take with them.

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it Proverbs 22:6 (KJV)

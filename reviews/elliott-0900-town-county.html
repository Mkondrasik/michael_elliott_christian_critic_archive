Author-verified transcription

It appears that infidelity is alive and well and on full display in Town & Country, a new sex comedy starring Warren Beatty and Diane Keaton which oddly feels like any number of old sex comedies.

After 25 years of faithful marriage, Porter Stoddard (Warren Beatty, Bulworth) is about to learn a hard lesson as to why it is always better to "just say no" to sexual temptation. His trip down the rocky road of adultery begins with the shocking discovery that his best friend, Griffin (Garry Shandling, Hurlyburly), has been caught having an affair with a young redhead. He'd be even more shocked if he knew that the redhead wasn't a woman, but Griffin can't seem to work up the nerve to tell him the whole truth.

The revelation of Griffin's affair opens the prudish Porter's eyes to the wide array of available (or at least agreeable) women there are in the world. It isn't long before he finds himself in bed with one of them... an attractive and also married cellist (Nastassja Kinski, The Claim). Though he immediately admits to himself that it was a mistake, he nevertheless succumbs to a series of dalliances with others: He beds Griffin's wife and longtime friend, Mona (Goldie Hawn, The First Wives' Club); pursues a flighty woman he meets on a plane (Andie MacDowell, Groundhog Day); and plays slap and tickle with a bait shop sales clerk (Jenna Elfman, Keeping the Faith).

Of course, he must try and keep news of his indiscretions from reaching his trusting wife (Diane Keaton, Annie Hall) and their teen aged children.

Easier said than done as Porter learns that hiding in closets, hanging from rooftops and repeatedly rehearsing his alibis aren't enough to keep the truth from eventually coming out.

The accomplished and talented cast do what they can with this rather weak and insignificant picture. One problem is that there is no strong central performance to ground the production and give it a foundation from which to build. Beatty's character is too weak-willed and Keaton's character, though stronger, is clueless for much of the film. As a result, the picture has something of an anchor-less or scattershot feel.

The secondary characters are designed to be quirky and fun but they are too superficially linked to the plot to make much sense. For instance, the scenes with Andie MacDowell and her eccentric parents, played by a gun-toting Charlton Heston (Ben-Hur) and a foul-mouthed Marian Seldes (Duets) in a motorized wheelchair, are so extraneous that there is no good reason for them to be in this film.

Director Peter Chelsom (The Mighty) obviously wanted to make a lighthearted comedy about extramarital affairs which alone begs the question: Why? The topic isn't all that light and the film isn't all that funny.

Town & Country breaks no new ground and has no deep insights to make regarding male/female relationships. Knowing that adultery will destroy the trust and unity that two people have built over the course of a marriage is something of a no-brainer. The actions taken by Porter are those of a totally selfish, unthinking man who has not bothered to consider the high cost of giving in to temptation. Scriptures almost beg us to consider our ways and look at the whole picture before acting upon a decision that once made, can never be undone.

"And why wilt thou, my son, be ravished with a strange woman, and embrace the bosom of a stranger?" Proverbs 5:20 (KJV)

Why indeed? Why risk all that marriage is, or can be? Why throw away a relationship that has been designed and blessed by God Himself? Why sacrifice the sweet contentment and soul strengthening that is built into the "one flesh" union of a husband and wife? Why invite pain, confusion, and bitterness to visit those who should mean the most to us... our families and friends?

What Porter comes to realize is that if he had it to do over again, he never would have taken that first step towards infidelity. The cost was far too high.

No Reservations

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 27, 2007
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: God's Word

Author-verified transcription

Who knows? Maybe one day The Food Network will take over the entertainment world. It certainly seems that way as yet another feature film centered on the culinary arts hits the theaters. No Reservations is a romantic comedy where love is just one of the ingredients simmering in the pot.

Catherine Zeta Jones stars as Kate, chef extraordinaire who is as skilled in the kitchen as she is socially inept outside of it. A typical A-type personality, she runs her culinary domain with an iron skillet.

But when she unexpectedly has to take charge of her young niece, Zoe (Abigail Breslin), during an emotionally devastating time, she finds that her well-ordered existence is beginning to crumble around her like a day-old coffee cake. Think Raising Helen starring a humorless Julia Child.

Added to her personal crisis is a professional one. Her restaurant's owner has hired a sous chef who may be every bit her equal. Nick (Aaron Eckhart) has a loose and easy going manner that enamors everyone and, for the owner, is a welcome respite from the archly superior Kate.

Of course one doesn't need a recipe book to see where this film is going. The two chefs share more than a kitchen counter; the grieving little girl schemes to unite them and then reunites them in a much more honest fashion after their own foolishness drives them apart; and so it goes... on and on in a formulaic fashion until its utterly predictable end.

But isn't that the way of romantic comedies? We don't necessarily want plot twists and surprise endings to get in the way of our romances. We just want to enjoy the ride. With No Reservations, we can honestly say that we enjoyed part of it. It's an uneven film that is too bogged down in tragedy to be lighthearted and too frivolous in character to be taken seriously.

Still there are some moments where Jones, Eckhart, and Breslin are allowed to shine in often subtle ways. Breslin is especially gifted in her expressions which communicate far more ably than words could ever hope to attain.

Jones, a beautiful woman by any standard, doesn't rely on her attractiveness here. But she also doesn't dig as deep into Kate as we would want to see. Her "therapy sessions" with her doctor (Bob Balaban) are merely filler and, as such, a wasted opportunity to flesh out her workaholic character to a more satisfying degree.

In one of those sessions she complains that there should be "a cookbook for life." As someone who follows recipes for a living, she could understand and appreciate having a few such cut-and dry guidelines in her personal affairs. The "feel-good" response she received is forgettable and ultimately meaningless. Had the doctor (or screenwriters?) been better prepared he might have been able to offer some real advice.

There is a cookbook for life - written by the ultimate chef who concocted the universe and all it contains. When we follow his instructions, our path is made clear and our lives become a "sweet-smelling savour." His is a true recipe for success — one that the wisest will always follow.

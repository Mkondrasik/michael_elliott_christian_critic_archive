V for Vendetta

Sermon / spiritual topic: Ends and Means; Evil; Justice

Author-verified transcription

V for Vendetta is a most unusual film - and not only because it paints a terrorist as its featured hero in this day and time. It's an eloquent comic book adaptation that has a great deal to say about the state of the world; about the dangers of political abuses; and about responses to tyranny.

Screenwriters Andy and Larry Wachowski (The Matrix) have taken the graphic novel by Alan Moore and tweaked it ever so greatly to present an avowedly polarizing film. Many have called it a scathing reflection of the Bush administration. Others, with less of a political agenda, have expressed a wider and more historically encompassing view.

Set in the all too near future, the film centers around Evey (Natalie Portman), a studying actress and studio go-fer, who becomes embroiled in a plot to bring down the oppressive government.

Manipulation, and inflexible rule have become SOP (standard operating procedure) for those in power. The population is aware of it but too numbed to recognize that their voice can matter.

A new masked revolutionary, V (Hugo Weaving), emerges to awake the people from their dangerous, figuratively imprisoned slumber. Adopting his methods from the terrorist handbook, V destroys a major landmark and broadcasts to the English people his intent to do what Guy Fawkes failed to do in the 17th century: blow up the Parliament on November 5th.

Evey, who is rescued from a couple of government goons by the knife wielding V, is torn between emotions. She has appreciation for his intellect as well as his obvious concern for her. But she is also aware that his dangerous nihilistic views of how to fix the country's ills would bring chaos to the world.

Hugo Weaving, acting behind a mask for the entire length of this two hour film, conveys the proper bearing of a cultured man who has chosen the only course of action that seems logical to him. His eloquent and poetic speech, including an amazingly ambitious attempt at artful alliteration as he announces the appellation by which he is to be addressed, is adroitly amusing.

Portman, working without a mask, has the harder job. She is the audience's mirror, wrestling with the moral question about which is the greater evil: V's vendetta or the government he is attempting to single-handedly overthrow.

The film's closing moments will leave the audience with much to think about and discuss as they leave the theater.

Of course the film is biased and obviously wants us to favor V and his efforts. The government depicted is undeniably cruel and malicious; intolerant and abusive. This only serves to underscore the film's central question: do the ends ever justify the means?

Based upon the scenario presented in V for Vendetta, it is tempting to say yes. In this case violence would be justified. Believing that however would steer us away from the standard of truth as set forth in the scriptures.

See that none render evil for evil unto any man; but ever follow that which is good, both among yourselves, and to all men. 1 Thessalonians 5:15 (KJV)

The example set for us is Jesus Christ, who, despite having the spiritual ability and every excuse in the world, did not resort to violent means to resist those who abused and vilified him. The impact of his life and legacy is eternal. He ended Satan's tyrannical rule over man - not by the power of the sword but by the power of God's love which he came to deliver.

Author-verified transcription

Is there anything more disappointing to watch than a great concept which remains unrealized due to poor execution? That just about sums up the experience awaiting audiences of The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen. These men (and woman) may be extraordinary... the film is not.

The great concept belongs to Alan Moore and Kevin O’Neill who developed a comic book after thinking about which super heroes might have been around in the 19th century. They recognized that before Indiana Jones, there was Alan Quartermain. Before Bruce Banner and the Hulk, there was Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde. Before Batman and his Batcave, there was Captain Nemo and his Nautilus.

Moore and O’Neill searched through the fantasy literature of the 19th and early 20th centuries to find literary characters which might, under the right circumstances, be considered as super hero material.

The year is 1899 and someone with the ability to manufacture advanced technological weaponry is trying to push the entire world into war. The British government has no choice but to turn to individual men whose unique abilities set them apart and equip them to save the world from this new threat.

Quartermain, played by Sean Connery (Finding Forrester), is the central figure in H. Ryder Haggard’s King Solomon’s Mine and is known for his bravery and marksmanship. Jules Verne invented Captain Nemo, played by Naseeruddin Shah (Monsoon Wedding), a brilliant Indian who has made a few technical advancements of his own.

They are joined by other literary figures: Oscar Wilde’s Dorian Gray (Stuart Townsend, Trapped); Bram Stoker’s Dracula victim, Mina Harker (Peta Wilson, Nikita); H.G. Wells’ Invisible Man (Tony Curran, Blade II); Robert Louis Stevenson’s Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde (Jason Flemyng, From Hell); and Mark Twain’s Tom Sawyer (Shane West, A Walk To Remember), now a grown up Secret Service agent.

This unlikely group must band together to track down and stop whoever is trying to force his will onto the entire world and plunge all mankind into the horrors of a modern war.

Considering that each of the central characters is the subject of a classic work of fiction, there’s a considerable wealth of character development already done for the filmmakers. The film is also peppered with other literary references: Phineas Phogg, Ishmael, Moriarity, M, among others.

Oddly enough, director Stephen Norrington (Blade) opts not to explore or exploit these rich and vibrantly described characters. They, for the most part, appear flat and uninspired in LXG - something of which they have never been accused in the context of their original stories.

Additionally, the action scenes have been filmed and edited (or rather chopped) in such a way to make viewing them difficult. As a result, the film feels rushed and often disjointed. Much of the final third of the film is simply confusing.

What is it that makes a man or woman extraordinary? A simple definition would be that which goes beyond what is usual or common. Whatever you may think of Jesus Christ, there is no doubt that he lived an extraordinary life. It is a life we have been invited to share. When a person chooses to live as Jesus Christ did, in service to God the Father, he not only aspires to the extraordinary... he attains it.

Author-verified transcription

2002 was quite a year for director Phillip Noyce. First he brought us the highly acclaimed Rabbit Proof Fence which was deservedly critical of the Australian government and its policies towards its Aboriginal population. Now, finally being released after having been shelved for reasons of political sensitivity, comes The Quiet American, an intelligent film adaptation of Graham Greene's 1955 prescient novel about the internal political struggle taking place in the tiny country of prewar Vietnam.

Michael Caine (Austin Powers in Goldmember) stars as Thomas Fowler, a cynical British journalist who has grown quite content living under the allure of French colonial life that Saigon offers. While the French and the Communists vie for control, Fowler simply observes. "I'm a reporter," he explains, "I don't get involved."

However, Fowler's comfortable world threatens to be upset by a series of events. First, his paper wants him to return to England, seeing as he hasn't filed that many stories to date. Then, Alden Pyle (Brendan Fraser, Gods and Monsters) arrives on the scene. Pyle, a wide-eyed idealist, immediately falls in love with Phuong (Do Thi Hai Yen, Vertical Ray of the Sun) who is Fowler's young mistress and perhaps the only person or thing for which Fowler still has feelings.

To keep both his job and the girl, Fowler investigates an event which took place up river and uncovers the massacre of a village. The Communists are being blamed but Fowler knows better. His suspicions turn to Pyle, whose idealism may be a mask for something far more treacherous.

The Quiet American is an excellent film, filled with moral complexities and ethical questions which continue to have relevance in our day. The ending of the film is deliberately ambiguous, forcing us to decide for ourselves if the actions taken by the characters were motivated by political beliefs or personal desires.

Noyce recognizes that the strength of this film is to be found in the characters and so he wisely underplays his directorial hand and allows the actors to have the full spotlight of our focused attention.

Michael Caine gives a seemingly effortless performance as the world weary Fowler who doesn't realize how much he stands to lose until he has almost lost it. Brendan Fraser plays naiveté very well and uses that ability to great effect here, as a man who would very much like people to make the mistake of seeing him as being overly naive.

Screenwriters Christopher Hampton (Dangerous Liaisons) and Robert Schenkkan (Crazy Horse) have pared Greene's novel down to what is essentially a love triangle set in a world fraught with political intrigue. On that level, thanks to a strong cast, the film works wonderfully.

Of course, the subject matter of the political aspect - America's interference or covert meddling in the affairs of other nations is something that can be discussed and debated ad nauseam. If the depicted CIA activities in The Quiet American have any basis in truth, there can be no justification for it. Supporting one despot in favor of another is, quite frankly, a devil's bargain and unworthy of a nation founded upon Christian principles.

It is an abomination (to God and men) for kings to commit wickedness, for a throne is established and made secure by righteousness (moral and spiritual rectitude in every area and relation.) Proverbs 16:12 (Amplified)

Certainly, a king or ruler must take action against another nation if the protection and well-being of his people is at risk. It is not wickedness to wage war against those who would do us harm. But such action may be done in full view and with the support and approval of the ruler's kingdom, country or protectorate. Trying to determine an outcome through deception, unseen manipulation, and the deaths of innocent lives can be considered nothing less than wickedness and should rightfully be denounced.

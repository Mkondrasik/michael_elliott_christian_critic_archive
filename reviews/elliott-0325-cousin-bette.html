Author-verified transcription

This ain't no kissin' cousin.

Set in Paris of 1846 for no reason other than to give the actors an opportunity to play "dress up," Cousin Bette tells the story of a woman scorned. She plots and manipulates events to bring ruin to those who, in her eyes, have done her wrong.

Here's the basic idea, taken from the novel by Honore de Balsac: A middle-aged spinster rescues a young starving artist from death and supports him financially while imagining there to be a romantic relationship between them. Her niece, Hortense (Kelly MacDonald, Trainspotting), after hearing Bette describe the young man, decides to steal the imaginary boyfriend and translate her aunt's fantasy into her own reality. That drives the aunt to seek revenge, using the beguiling charms of courtesan Jenny Cadine (Elisabeth Shue, Leaving Las Vegas).

As directed by Des McAnuff (Monterey Pop), Cousin Bette aspires to be another Dangerous Liaisons, but lacks the necessary intelligence to make it work. The story line is oftentimes muddled, rendering it unclear. The characters will take actions which are unexplained (or perhaps unexplainable). One central figure in particular is glaringly miscast.

All of the characters behave unseemly. They are either plotting someone's ruin, or trampling on someone's feelings, or stealing someone's hope and dreams. There is not one character in this entire film that exhibits an ounce of meekness or remorse for their actions.

Jessica Lange (A Thousand Acres), as the title character, gives a credible performance. The husky throated Miss Lange puts on enough of a hardened edge that we choose to overlook the fact that as Bette, she is supposed to be unattractive and unappealing. Miss Lange, of course, is neither.

The talented but contemporary Miss Shue is completely out of her element in this period piece. She was amusing in Adventures in Babysitting and compelling in Leaving Las Vegas, but she is embarrassingly bad as a 19th century courtesan/showgirl with a penchant to show her bare bottom on stage. Miss Shue demonstrates neither the voice of a showgirl nor the wit of a courtesan. She does show her bare bottom quite often but (Movie Note #66) a shapely derriere does not a character make.

Bob Hoskins (Who Framed Roger Rabbit) fares a bit better as Cesar Crevel, one of the men who gets tangled up in the periphery of Bette's schemes.

Aden Young (Under Heaven) plays the young artist, Count Wenceslas Steinbach, who unwittingly becomes the catalyst and then the pawn for the many lies, deceptions, and manipulations wrought by Bette, Hortense, and Jenny. Hugh Laurie (The Man In The Iron Mask) is the Baron Hector Hulot, Bette's cousin and patriarch of the house. It is his financial misconduct that paves the way for Bette's mischievous handiwork to take effect.

Debt was an ugly thing in 1846 and it remains an ugly thing today. Living beyond one's means is never a good or godly idea. It indicates a lack of discipline, a lack of stewardship, and a lack of believing. The consequence of living beyond one's means can be put off for a time but it eventually must always be faced. It is never a pleasant experience as the Baron Hector Hulot can certainly testify.

It is Cousin Bette who actually holds the godly key to prosperity. After she rescues the young artist and strengthens him through care and nourishment, she develops a work schedule for him. She plans how long he will work, when he is to take his breaks, when he sleeps. She tells him, "If you follow this plan and are productive, your life will change for the better."

Hard work, discipline, planning, preparation and believing are all keys to success. These keys are biblically based, forged by the breath of God. They will open the door to genuine prosperity and victory. Because they are God's design, they are guaranteed to work for whoever employs them - saint and sinner alike. That's a good thing for Cousin Bette, because there are no saints in this movie.

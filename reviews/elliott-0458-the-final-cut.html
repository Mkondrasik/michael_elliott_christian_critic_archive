Author-verified transcription

Memories. They can be a blessing or a curse. But one thing used to be certain — they belonged to you and you alone. Not any more. In The Final Cut, technology has invaded even this most intimate human experience.

Alan Hakman (Robin Williams) is the best cutter in a business that is described by one character as requiring him to be part mortician, part priest, and part taxidermist. What Hakman makes available for people who have the money, is an organic implant (called Zoé) that, once inserted into a newborn baby, will record every memory of that individual’s life.

Once such a person dies, Hakman will take the implant containing an entire lifetime of memories and judiciously edit them to make a movie length tribute of the person’s life. This final edit is known as a rememory.

The practice, popular among the affluent, is not without its critics. In addition to the questionable morality of seeing a life from the eyes of the one who actually lived it, many also take issue with the revisionist history that cutters promote. They are commissioned by the bereaved to edit out the unsavory, unbecoming, or otherwise inflammatory details of the deceased in order to present them in the best possible light.

It is a process that Hakman calls “sin- eating” and does better than most. His ability to emotionally detach himself from the images he screens enables him to approach his job with the eye of a dispassionate auteur. He could, that is, until he sees something that triggers one of his own memories — something which causes him to question what he considers to be the single most important moment in his life.

Robin Williams once again displays his dramatic ability as the emotionally repressed and guilt ridden Hakman who feels more comfortable in front of his computer screens than he does interacting with people. It’s not a role that shows the full range of which he’s capable but it is a role and a characterization that serves the story well.

Mira Sorvino costars as Hakman’s on- again, off-again love interest that is growing weary of competing for his attention. James Caviezel adds a touch of suspense as an ex-cutter who is now actively protesting the use of the technology.

Writer/director Omar Naim makes an auspicious debut with this intriguing film that works as both a thriller and a springboard for the discussion of moral or ethical questions. Perhaps a more experienced filmmaker might have handled some of the philosophical issues with a bit more flair or finesse but Naim strikes a nice balance in keeping the film both thought-provoking and entertaining.

Hakman’s explanation of the way a “sin-eater” functions should remind us that in a very real sense the service has already been performed for us. All of our actions, words and thoughts are being observed and recorded. But God, in his great mercy, is willing to act as our “cutter.” As we, in all humility and repentance ask for his forgiveness, he is willing to not only forgive us our sins... he says that he will forget they were ever committed.

For I will be merciful to their unrighteousness, and their sins and their iniquities will I remember no more. Hebrews 8:12 (KJV)

He wields the ultimate delete key and all he asks is that we try to live our lives building positive memories of which we need not be ashamed and that will not be erased. We can make memories that will live on forever in the minds of those who will look at our lives as great examples of believing and righteousness.

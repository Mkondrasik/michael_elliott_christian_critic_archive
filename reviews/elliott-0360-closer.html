Author-verified transcription

Sex sells. It also buys trouble for those who become obsessed with it. In Closer, director Mike Nichols uses four unlikable characters to explore sex, love, and relationships - showing how an unhealthy emphasis on sex can lead to the ruination of everything else.

Based on the play by Patrick Marber, who also penned the screen adaptation, Closer follows Dan, Anna, Larry and Alice in the steamy soap opera they call their lives.

Dan (Jude Law), a writer of obituaries and would be novelist, is involved with Alice (Natalie Portman), a young but experienced American stripper looking to start life anew in London. Even though she is both literally and figuratively knocked off her feet because of him, the selfish cad cannot stop himself from making a move on Anna (Julia Roberts), an American photographer hired to take his headshot.

Initially rebuffed, he turns to an Internet chat room where he has a bit of fun pretending to be Anna for the sexual fantasy of a randy dermatologist named Larry (Clive Owen). When Larry and Anna actually meet and start their relationship, Dan cannot let the matter rest and begins to pursue Anna with an obsession that rivals Captain Ahab's. When she eventually succumbs to his insistent charms, Larry and Alice find themselves out in the cold but things will heat up between them soon enough.

The four continue their tangled sexual square dance until just about all possible pairings have been done and all relationships destroyed or damaged beyond any hope of salvage.

The movie itself seems relatively pointless but then again perhaps that is the point. The act of loveless sex is a selfish and rather meaningless event and to watch four individuals self-destruct and ruin whatever potential for real love that they have is pitifully sad.

The acting is superior. All four main characters are well defined and while we may not like them or approve of their actions, the fact that we feel like we understand them is a tribute to the talents of the accomplished cast of actors.

Though the film is about sex and its effect upon people and their relationships, we don't really see much of the sexual act itself. We do hear about it quite a bit - in often crude detail. It is primarily for this reason that the film has been rated R by the MPAA for adult/mature audiences.

The film has been called a "modern day love story" by some. It's a misnomer if ever I've heard one. Love does not drive these characters. Most of the time, it doesn't even come along for the ride.

Alice, who in some ways may be the most honest character in the film, confronts one of her lovers with this observation: "Where is this love? I can't see it. I can't touch it. I can't feel it. I can hear it. I can hear some words, but I can't do anything with your easy words."

The love we have to give others is not delivered through our words but rather through our actions.

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. John 3:16 (KJV)

God so loved that he gave. His love was manifested, evidenced and defined by his action. If we desire for love to mean more than a crude euphemism for sex then we must follow his lead. The giving of one's self without the selfish expectation of receiving anything in return is a pure act of love. Unfortunately it is an act that none of the characters in Closer have practiced much less perfected.

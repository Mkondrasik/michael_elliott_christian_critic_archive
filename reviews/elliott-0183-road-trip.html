Road Trip

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
May 19, 2000
Rating
½ star
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: NIV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Example; youth; purity; Christian conduct; moral choices

Author-verified transcription

If I was counted among the target audience for the morally repugnant film Road Trip, I'd be insulted. These filmmakers obviously have little esteem for the intelligence or taste of the teens for whom this picture was made.

As this sorry excuse for a comedy opens, Barry, a 7th year undergraduate of Ithica University, is conducting a pathetic campus tour. This is merely a screenwriter's excuse to tell a story of love, deception, sex, drugs, and self-destructive behavior from a testosterone-filled, hormonally unbalanced view.

Josh (Breckin Meyer, Go) is in love with Tiffany (Rachel Blanchard, TV's Clueless). He has been since the 4th grade. Now that they are in college, (he in Ithica, NY and she in Austin, TX), the distance between them is becoming more than geographical.

Enter Beth (Amy Smart, Varsity Blues). Smart, attractive, and willing to jump into bed with Josh at the blink of an eye... her willingness even extending to the videotaping of the experience.

When that video tape is inadvertently mailed to Tiffany in Austin, our unfaithful "hero" has no choice but to forgo his final exams and head off to Texas to retrieve the tape and salvage his relationship. Showing the kind of dedication and ethical behavior being instilled in our future leaders, three of his classmates decide to blow off their classes as well. Just because. In the words of one of them, "What do you want me to do? Stay here and learn?" And so the four of them pile into a car (it doesn't really belong to them, of course) and the "Road Trip" begins.

Road kill would be more like it. This film has about as many redeeming qualities as a flattened armadillo. By depicting unlawful or immoral acts without showing the negative consequences of those acts, Road Trip indirectly encourages infidelity, promiscuity, drug use, destruction of property, theft, wanton disobedience, lying, and slothfulness... to name just a few.

What humor is attempted in this comedy is puerile and for the most part unfunny. It is also highly demeaning of women and male/female relationships.

The acting is above average but the characters are so reprehensible in their behavior that the actors suffer from the association. Breckin Meyer is Josh, a nice guy who has cheated on his girl friend. Seann William Scott (Final Destination) is E.L. whose thought processes originate a few inches below his navel. DJ Qualls (Cherry Falls) is Kyle, a nerd who finds the courage to stand up to his father by destroying his car and sleeping with a black woman. Paulo Costanzo (in his film debut) is Ruben, an intellectually and philosophically superior pothead. Tom Green (Superstar) is Barry, the slacker who is more interested in the feeding of a snake than graduating from college. The "nicest" character on screen is Tiffany and she's more talked about than seen, as too much decency might ruin the mood of this Road Trip.

I'd certainly like to think that today's young people are capable of developing a more refined taste and appreciation for genuine wit, satire, or farce. Of course, that would be giving our teens more credit than the makers of Road Trip seem to be willing to extend. While we should be encouraging the younger generations to excel and grow in all categories of life, this film does the opposite.

Don't let anyone look down on you because you are young, but set an example for the believers in speech, in life, in love, in faith and in purity. 1 Timothy 4:12 (NIV)

The only examples we can find in Road Trip were the negative ones... What not to do. This is a trip that is best not taken... A road that should remain less traveled.

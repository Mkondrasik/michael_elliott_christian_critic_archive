Author-verified transcription

In Master and Commander: The Far Side of the World, director Peter Weir revisits a world that has long since passed into history. Based upon the 20 book series written by renowned author Patrick O’Brian, the film tells a nautical tale of courage, leadership, strength, friendship and patriotism set during the Napoleonic Wars of the early 1800s.

While Napoleon was dominating the battlefields on land, the British Empire controlled the seas. Captain Jack Aubrey (Russell Crowe), nicknamed “Lucky Jack” by his crew, has been given the task of intercepting the French frigate Acheron which has been terrorizing the “south seas” off the coast of Brazil.

Early in the film, the faster, heavier, and better equipped frigate makes an unexpected attack, nearly crippling the British warship, ironically named the HMS Surprise. Aubrey, able to slip away under the disguising cloak of fog, decides to refit at sea and pursue the frigate instead of returning home. The odds of victory are against him but duty compels him to remain. Or is it pride that drives him on a fool’s mission?

That’s a question only Aubrey’s close friend, confidante and ship’s surgeon, Stephen Maturin (Paul Bettany) dares ask. For the rest of the crew, thousands of miles from home with no means of communicating with anyone else, a naval captain aboard his vessel is just one step beneath God.

Weir does an amazing job of conveying the look and feel of a life that no longer exists. The film opens slowly, with the HMS Surprise at sea - gently rocking; wood creaking with every wave that passes under her bow. It is a slow, methodical introduction which informs us of the attention to detail that Weir and company have meticulously applied.

Russell Crowe is a fine choice to play the larger than life Capt. Aubrey. Expert seaman and strategist, he also enjoys a good pun now and again as long as it is washed down with an ample supply of grog. As he did in Gladiator, he plays a man that other men will follow without hesitation or complaint. And he plays it well.

His costar from A Beautiful Mind, Paul Bettany, is cast as his more sensitive and intellectual companion. More a man of science than he is a man of war, Stephen Maturin is often at odds with Aubrey’s decisions, especially when they conflict with his own naturalist pursuits.

However, the real “star” of the film is the setting itself. We really get a sense of what life must have been like aboard a tall ship circa 1805. It was a life where discipline was not just expected, it was necessary for everyone’s survival. The key to discipline, as Aubrey instructs one of his young midshipmen, is respect. “Without respect, true discipline goes by the board.”

True discipline does not stem from a fear of retribution nor can it be wrested from us against our will. The discipline which builds strength and endurance is one that is offered willingly and faithfully. If we do not believe that something is worthy of our time or effort - in other words, if we do not have respect for it - we won’t discipline ourselves to it. Our discipleship in Christ must begin with our love of and respect for God.

And this is love, that we walk after his commandments. This is the commandment, That, as ye have heard from the beginning, ye should walk in it. 2 John 1:6 (KJV)

A Christian walk is indeed a disciplined one. But much more than that... it is also a walk of love.

Author-verified transcription

It has been said that there are two kinds of rich people. Those who have lots of money; and those who have no need. In The Family Man we see a glimpse of both types... being played by the same actor.

Borrowing freely from many movies, (It's a Wonderful Life, A Christmas Carol, and Trading Places among them), The Family Man gives us Jack Campbell (Nicolas Cage, Leaving Las Vegas), a man who is on top of his particular world. He's a successful financial wunderkind who drives a Ferrari, owns his own Wall Street firm, and is on the verge of closing a multibillion dollar merger. It is also Christmas Eve and he has no reason to go home. Thirteen years ago, he made a decision which led him away from a serious relationship with a woman and towards a driving ambition to succeed in business.

After he tells a mysterious stranger (Don Cheadle, Mission to Mars) that his life contains everything he could ever want, he awakens Christmas morning to find that his once familiar life has been turned upside down. Instead of being an affluent, single Wall Street business executive, he inexplicably finds himself in suburban New Jersey with a wife, two kids, and job selling tires at a retail outlet.

Nicolas Cage is very believable, both as a high powered financial shark and as a fish out of water. His shock and confusion as he first finds himself in the middle of a married-with-children life is quite amusing.

But then, his initial disappointment in his "new" life gives way to a growing awareness of the richness of being a family man. He makes the transition smoothly and lets us see his character's internal conflict of desires.

Téa Leoni (Deep Impact), in a welcome return to the screen, plays Kate, Jack's "wife" and the girl he left 13 years ago. Sweet and sassy, it is she that makes the love relationship between the characters as believably special as it appears to be. When Jack, remembering the financial perks of his other life, moans about wanting "to have the kind of life other people envy," she simply and compassionately reminds him that "we already do."

As their precocious six year old "daughter," Makenzie Vega (The Geena Davis Show) is capable of stealing hearts as well as scenes. She is precious.

Don Cheadle, as the "angel" who provides Jack with this glimpse of what his life could have been like, brings an energy and knowing awareness that helps to move the film along. Jeremy Piven (The Crew) uses his blue collar persona to good effect as the married Jack's best friend and confidante.

Director Brett Ratner (Rush Hour) lets the schmaltz run a bit thickly at times but as this is a holiday story about love and second chances, he can be forgiven. Overall, it is a warm and funny romantic comedy which leaves the audience glowing in appreciation.

The only disappointment is that The Family Man is not really a family picture. Scenes containing implied sexuality/nudity, profanity, and alcohol use will give many parents pause about allowing their children to see it. It is a shame because the core message is a valid and valuable one to communicate.

The movie makes the point that there are many different kinds of treasures in life. Jack goes from being a man who had everything money can buy to being a man who has something that money can't buy. The question Jack must answer for himself is: which is more valuable? It is a question which can be answered by turning to the scriptures.

Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies. Proverbs 31:10 (KJV)

Money is a tool. An instrument to be used by man as the means to an end and not the end itself. The love of a good woman is far more than that. The fruit born of a strong love relationship will bring a healthy satisfaction unmatched by the result of any balance sheet or ledger book. Love of money will only lead to an insatiable greed. The love of a virtuous woman, however, is continually satisfying and rewarding.

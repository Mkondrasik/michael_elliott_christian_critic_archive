Holy Man

Author-verified transcription

I never saw Holy Man in the theaters. I read the reviews printed by critics who did. Based on those reviews, I had the lead to this column written before I ever watched the movie (now available on video tape): “Holy Man is not as much ‘holy’ as it is full of holes.”

OK, so Shakespeare, I’m not. But the point is, based on the abundance of negative reviews I read, I anticipated a dreadful experience. I am happy to say that I was pleasantly surprised.

While being far from a perfect movie, there is a sweetness and genuineness to this film that is quite appealing. Perhaps the subject matter lends itself to a smaller screen. Or perhaps the average critic is a bit too jaundiced, rendering him unable to relate to “nice” people. Whatever the cause, don’t let the bad reviews you’ve read be the thing that keeps you from renting Holy Man.

Eddie Murphy (Doctor Dolitte) plays “G”, a spiritually-minded man who is on a pilgrimage that appears to require his walking along the highways of Los Angeles stopping every so often to kiss the ground.

Enter Ricky Hayman (Jeff Goldblum, Jurassic Park: The Lost World) and Kate Newell (Kelly Preston, Jerry Maguire), employees of the Good Buy Network, a television station airing nothing but infomercials. Ricky’s job as program director is being threatened by the new owner (Robert Loggia, Big) because sales have been “flat.” Kate’s job as consultant is to help Ricky increase station revenue.

That’s where G comes in. Ricky and Kate discover that people seem to respond to his totally centered and peaceful character. Putting him on the air to hold forth his message of love and peace and good will, enables them to sell the garbage that has been stockpiling in the studio’s warehouse.

The infomercials satirized in the movie are amusing, whether it is Betty White demonstrating an erotic perfume, Morgan Fairchild hawking an electronic face tightener, or Soupy Sales selling glue guns, we laugh with the knowing recognition that as bad as these products are, we have seen (or will soon see) worse.

Mr. Murphy, in a starring role, assumes an almost supporting role perspective. This movie isn’t really about his character, and he knows it. The movie is about how people respond to his character. By underplaying the part, the focus naturally shifts to his costars who share the screen with him.

Mr. Goldblum appears at times to confuse personality with character. He tries so hard to be genial, he sometimes forgets to be real. Still, he does manage to present a likable character and his line delivery remains a most unique and interesting element of his acting “style”.

Miss Preston was handed a relatively do-nothing role and therefore shouldn’t be blamed for doing nothing with it. But Mr. Loggia really should have been told to bring his character down a decibel or ten. His out-of-control, angry and evil boss characterization was not remotely believable.

The fault for that must lie with the director who is ultimately responsible for everything that appears on screen. Stephen Herek (Mr. Holland’s Opus) allowed too many inconsistencies to remain in the finished product. The overall concept was excellent, the script was adequate, the talent collected for the film was capable. That it didn’t completely gel must lie at the director’s feet.

However, I have to say that the contributing elements were enjoyable enough to recommend giving this film a try at home on your VCR.

The way people responded to G’s message serves to indicate how desperately this world wants to find solutions to today’s problems. They want to believe in something that will make things better. We call it a “God-hunger.”

You see, God designed us. And He designed us to desire Him. As life goes on, people oftentimes will fill this desire with error. They accept temptations or lies designed to keep us from the truth. But the devil’s lies will never satisfy. They cannot take the place of God’s presence in our lives.

When we “find” God and place Him in our hearts where he belongs, G’s message of love and inner contentment makes perfect sense.

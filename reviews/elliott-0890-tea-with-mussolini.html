Author-verified transcription

What kind of spark lights the inner fuel that allows an artist to function? Whence does the artist's vision come? I suppose the answer to that question must be different for every individual. Franco Zeffirelli (Romeo and Juliet) answers, to some degree, this highly personal question with his highly personal new film, Tea With Mussolini.

Based on his autobiographical novel, it tells the story of a young boy, Luca (Charlie Lucas in his film debut) growing up in Florence, Italy without the benefit of a mother or father.

He is, instead, cared for by a group of expatriated British ladies who, appalled at the boy's plight, take on the responsibility of his upbringing.

This charming coming of age story takes a somewhat serious note as it is set in the framework of wartime Europe. At first, Italy's dictator Benito Mussolini is admired by all, including the ever so opinionated grande dames of Britain who are respectfully referred to as "the Scorpioni." Why? "Because they bite," we are told. To them, Mussolini is simply the gentleman who's got the trains running on time. But as years go by, the environment turns ugly and dangerous as their comfortable little world begins to crash around them.

The acting, without a doubt, is wonderful. Mr. Zeffirelli has amassed some of the greatest female screen talent this side of a Shakespearean adaptation. Joan Plowright (Dance With Me), as the secretary of the boy's father, heads the group of feisty old English ladies who assume the responsibility of the young bastard child in wartime Italy. She is assisted by eccentric art restorer Arabella (Judi Dench, Shakespeare in Love), flighty American heiress Elsa (Cher, Moonstruck), and the all too apparently lesbian archeologist Georgie (Lily Tomlin, Krippendorf's Tribe.) The upper crust of England, in the guise of ever-so-proper Hester (Maggie Smith, Sister Act 2), makes her silent disapproval known most definitively with a mere sideways glance or though pursed lips.

These ladies go about their day... having tea, saving Italian art from the Italians... seemingly unconcerned with the rise of fascism that is taking place around them, until one day they are rounded up and placed under guard. Now teen-aged, Luca (Baird Wallace, also in his film debut) returns to rescue the women who were so integral to his early development.

The movie fails to engage us as deeply as the subject matter would suggest. Although the individual performances are outstanding (Oscar material perhaps?) they simply don't gel as an ensemble. It doesn't help that Cher's turn, as the ex-Ziegfeld Follies' star, comes across as too modern-day for this period piece.

The cinematography, however, is spectacular. The film was shot on location in Florence, Rome and San Gimignano, Italy. The settings are magnificent and give a flavor to the film that a studio set could not possibly duplicate. My wife, who is Italian and majored in art history, absolutely loved this film. I, a Mayberry RFD kind of American who majored in theater, did not share her enthusiasm. Perhaps a knowledge and fondness for things Italian might boost one's appreciation for this film.

The misplaced trust that Hester has in Mussolini (based solely upon her once sharing tea with El Duce) places all of them in danger as they stay in Italy longer than they should, given the situation at hand. Time after time, history has proven that men (and women) will lie. Mussolini was no different.

There is only One worthy of our trust. Only One within Whom we can have complete and utter confidence. Trusting in God's Word will never cause us to be ashamed or disappointed, for God is not a man that He would lie. He never changes, and His heart is always that we prosper and be in health. That is a promise that cannot help but come true as we stand solidly upon His Word, obeying His commandments, and living by His standard.

The spark that ignites our "inner fuel" must be the rightly divided Word of God.

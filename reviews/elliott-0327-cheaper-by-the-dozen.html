Cheaper by the Dozen

Michael Elliott

December 25, 2003

1 hr: 38 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Family; children as a blessing; priorities; unity

Author-verified transcription

The bad news is that the family comedy, Cheaper by the Dozen, is not a new and improved version of the 1950 original. It's just new. And although it doesn't break new ground or add insight into family relationships, it might provoke a smile (or two) of recognition and familiarity. Audiences looking for family entertainment could do worse.

Steve Martin and Bonnie Hunt star as the parents of a large brood of children. He's a coach being given a shot at his dream job... head football coach of Illinois Polytechnic University. Because of his new job, the family packs up and moves to Chicago. As might be expected in a family with twelve children (ranging in ages from twenty-two to five) the transition is not without incident.

Complicating the situation is the fact that Mom has just received a publishing contract and must temporarily leave the family for her nationwide publicity tour. Dad must not only handle the pressures of a new job, he must cope with the enormous responsibilities of managing his larger than average household.

In the original film, the father was a bit of an efficiency expert, devising inventive ways of coping with the demands of having so many children. In the updated version, the father is anything but expert. Things quickly descend into family chaos and situations which he is ill equipped to handle. A bit "slapstickish" perhaps, but it makes for more than a few humorous scenes.

Steve Martin and Bonnie Hunt develop a nice, comfortable chemistry which communicates a loving and long-lasting relationship. Of course, as there are only two adults in the family, it is easy to keep track of them. There are so many kids that it is impossible to give them enough screen time to develop their characters fully.

Of the twelve, there are a few which distinguish themselves: Hilary Duff as the #3 child (ranked by descending age) is the hip, culture-conscious one. Piper Perabo (the #1 child) is the selfish one who is the first to escape the insanity of the family homestead. And little Forrest Landis (the #7 child) is the one who seems to be overlooked or forgotten. It is his story that serves as the major catalyst for the priority shifting which the family comes to realize is necessary.

Ashton Kutcher has a small part as the boyfriend of the eldest daughter. It's an amusing role for him, playing a shallow, would-be actor who knows that talent lies primarily in his face.

As the film characters would be the first to tell you (and, interestingly enough, the original film was based upon a true story), a large family is neither a curse nor a burden. It is a blessing. It may be challenging at times but it is never unrewarding. God says as much in His Word:

As arrows are in the hand of a mighty man; so are children of the youth. Happy is the man that hath his quiver full of them: they shall not be ashamed, but they shall speak with the enemies in the gate. Psalms 127:4-5 (KJV)

Being a part of a large family, whether it is an earthly family or the spiritual family to which we've been called, it is helpful to recognize that no member is less or more important. Each member is a unique individual and is needful in order to complete the family unit.

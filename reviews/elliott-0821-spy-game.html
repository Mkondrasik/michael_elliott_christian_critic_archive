Spy Game

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 21, 2001
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
2 hr: 7 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Mentoring; Teaching; Spiritual Legacy

Author-verified transcription

Contrary to what James Bond would have us believe, the "spy game" is not all about gadgets and sexual innuendoes. Spy Game gives us a less fanciful look into what it might be like to be a CIA operative in a world where it is becoming increasingly difficult to tell the good guys from the bad.

Robert Redford (The Last Castle) stars as Nathan Muir, a CIA agent poised for retirement, who hears that his young protégé, Tom "Boy Scout" Bishop (Brad Pitt, The Mexican), has been captured by the Chinese. Not wishing to upset the balance of delicate trade negotiations, US officials are only too happy to look the other way while the Chinese torture and execute Bishop for international espionage. Fortunately for Bishop, Muir's loyalties extend beyond the political to reach a much deeper personal level.

Muir has less than 24 hours to mount a rescue operation and save his friend and fellow agent. It will be a nearly impossible mission seeing as he is on the opposite end of the earth, he doesn't know where the Chinese are holding Bishop, and he can't let his own government know what he is doing.

The structure of the film demands of us more than a bit of concentration. Largely told in flashback form as Muir is debriefed by his CIA bosses, the film tells the story of the 16 year relationship that was forged between Muir and Bishop. Meeting in Vietnam, where top field agent Muir recruits the young sharpshooter, the film later switches to Berlin and then Beirut as we see the developing relationship between these two CIA agents take form. Such scenes are interspersed with shots of the Chinese prison where Bishop is being held and CIA headquarters in Washington DC where Muir covertly plots his release.

Spy Game is an intellectual thriller much more than it is an emotional roller coaster. Screenwriter Michael Frost Beckner (who originated the new TV series The Agency) certainly has insight as to the workings of the CIA and some of the moral questions that arise from men who work as undercover agents in hostile territories.

The structure of the screenplay does lend to some difficulties in comprehension. The frequent use of flashback also prevents the film from building the proper emotional tension that might have come from a more linear approach.

Teaming Robert Redford and Brad Pitt (who once worked together on A River Runs Through It) is something of a casting coup. Redford repeatedly gives off an aura of knowing more than the other guy as well as the wherewithal to use what he knows. This quality is particularly suited for Muir who must outthink the entire intelligence community. Pitt, who has the potential to follow the same kind of career path that Redford took, is equally matched to his role. They are at the top of their game and their scenes together are highly enjoyable to watch.

Catherine McCormack (The Tailor of Panama) costars in the pivotal role of Elizabeth Hadley, a humanitarian aid worker who causes a rift between the two lead characters.

The close relationship shared between Muir and Bishop is neither unique nor new. It is, however, worthy of our attention. Having an older, more experienced man teaching all that he knows to someone of the next generation is how the Word of God has been passed down through the centuries.

And the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also. 2 Timothy 2:2 (KJV)

Jesus Christ had his disciples. The Apostle Paul had Timothy. Each of us have a responsibility to share what we know with those who will follow us, even as we must learn from those who are our spiritual elders. Only by our building upon the accomplishments and knowledge gained by the generation before us are we able to increase in knowledge and understanding.

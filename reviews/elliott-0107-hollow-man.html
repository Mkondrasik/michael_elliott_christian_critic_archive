Hollow Man

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 4, 2000
Rating
1 star
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: God’s design; the human body; creation; workmanship

Author-verified transcription

Paul Verhoeven’s contribution to the “Invisible Man” mythology is the horror/thriller, Hollow Man. It uses all the advanced technical know-how and state of the art computer graphic imagery available to generate the special effects that fill its screen. All the more pity that everything else that is needed to make a successful movie is as invisible as the central character. Hollow Man is worse than hollow, it is rotten to the core.

Sebastian Caine (Kevin Bacon, Stir of Echoes) is a brilliant and egocentric scientist heading up a top-secret, government-sanctioned project on invisibility. Having achieved early success in rendering their test animals invisible, they are stuck on the problem of reversing the process.

With one stroke of his self-described genius, Sebastian solves the ponderous dilemma and proclaims the project ready to progress to the next level... A human subject. And guess who wants to be first?

Naturally, the human body proves to be far more complex than their previous animal subjects and the reversion to visibility doesn’t work. Sebastian is stuck in his transparent guise until the kinks in the formula get worked out. Of course, as every moviegoer knows, the longer one stays invisible, the more unstable one’s mind gets. It’s just a basic horror movie fact that apparently needs no further explanation.

It isn’t long before Sebastian starts using his invisibility as a cloak to mask his evil activities, which includes molestation, rape, and murder.

Mr. Bacon does an acceptable job as the scientist turned mad killer, though he is probably thankful that he can’t be seen during the final third of the film except when immersed in water, walking through smoke, doused with a fire extinguisher, or flamed by a blow torch. The other actors aren’t as blessed. Their faces are seen throughout.

Elisabeth Shue (Molly) never gets a good handle on her character. She plays Linda McKay, the ex-girlfriend of Sebastian who is secretly involved with Matthew Kensington (Josh Brolin, The Mod Squad), another project scientist. But really, the actors, though they do not come off looking particularly well, should not be saddled with the blame of this rather poor showing. The unintentional laughs this film evokes (and there are many) comes from ridiculous and hackneyed dialogue matched with motivations and illogical actions usually found only in spoofs of the genre.

Director Paul Verhoeven (Starship Troopers) doesn’t bring a sense of tension (or believability) to his film which starts out decently enough but soon denigrates to nothing more than a series of B-level “slice and dice” slasher movie clichés.

Despite the very effective promotional trailer pieced together to sell the picture, the truth is, the only aspect of the film which is actually worth seeing is the awesome special effect which occurs when a subject is phasing from visibility to invisibility or vice versa.

The first time it happens, the scientists inject the serum into the invisible arm of a gorilla. We see the serum traveling through the invisible vein to the heart. From the heart the serum begins spreading through the arteries and capillaries. As it spreads the area reached by the serum begins to become visible. Watching this, one cannot help but be amazed by the intricacy of the body’s design.

I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvelous are thy works; and that my soul knoweth right well. My substance was not hid from thee, when I was made in secret, and curiously wrought in the lowest parts of the earth. Psalms 139:14-15 (KJV)

The phrase “curiously wrought” literally means “embroidered,” as in woven together. God, by design, formed and fashioned the body of man. “Wonderfully” and “marvelous” are woefully inadequate to describe the awesomeness of God’s work. At the same time, Hollow Man is woefully inadequate to deserve those same adjectives to be used about it.

Where the Money Is

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 14, 2000
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 29 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Love of Money

Author-verified transcription

Can one man totally redeem a movie which is saddled by a weak script and uninspired direction? As the filmmakers of Where The Money Is have discovered, the answer is no... not even when that one man is the inimitable Paul Newman (Message in a Bottle).

The title of the film is an overt reference to the reply given by famed bank robber Willie Sutton when asked for the reason as to why he robbed banks. (Answer: That's "where the money is") For bank robber Henry Manning, the answer might be just as simplistic. It is only the methodology that has gotten complicated.

Finally caught in a bank vault whose timing mechanism had sprung due to a fluke power outage, the aged criminal, Manning, is set to spend the rest of his life behind prison bars. That is, he was set... up until he has a stroke that has apparently left him in a catatonic state. Confined to a wheelchair and transferred to a nursing home, he falls under the care of Carol Ann McKay (Linda Fiorentino, What Planet Are You From?), ex-prom queen and small town beauty who is bored with her life.

Carol suspects that Manning is playing possum and makes it her mission in life to prove it. It doesn't take long. With one mission completed, she concocts another: To pull a bank job with Manning's help. The career criminal, reluctant at first, is eventually drawn to this amateur's plan because... Well... That's where the money is.

The unimpressive execution of the script is not the fault of the clearly impressive 75 year old actor, who proves yet again that he is one of the best film actors of his or any generation. Even while playing a character in a vegetative state, he manages to command our rapt attention and convey an inner life which is at once interesting and sexy. In scenes where he is more animated, such as when he explains to Carol and her husband how he is able to "fake" his condition, he is downright mesmerizing.

His costars do not fare as well. Linda Fiorentino is missing the depth needed to round out and make her character, Carol, a fully realized individual. As a result, many of her scenes play flat and listless.

Dermot Mulroney (My Best Friend's Wedding) plays Wayne, Carol's husband and reluctant third wheel to the caper devised by his wife. Mr. Mulroney makes all the right choices as an actor, but the character he plays, a contented man whose ambition ended at the same time as his high school prom, is written so blandly that he adds little to life of the story.

Director Marek Kanievska (Less Than Zero) takes a slow and almost lazy approach to the material which he allows to unfold at its own easygoing pace.

Where The Money Is is not as clever as Mr. Newman's caper film, The Sting. It does not have the same enjoyable banter as Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid. It doesn't even have as strong a grasp of its subject as did The Hustler or The Color of Money. What it does have in common with these fine films is Paul Newman. And that is almost enough.

Morally speaking, its message is skewed, reflecting the world's viewpoint. Far too much emphasis is placed on the acquisition of money. Not that money itself is evil. But should we elevate its importance in our minds, we begin down a path which leads to poor and ungodly decisions.

For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows. 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV)

It could be argued that Carol is acting not out of a love of money but out of a sense of ennui. She is bored with her small town life and becomes enamored with the titillating excitement afforded by breaking the law. By acting upon this impulse, she puts her marriage, her job, and her very freedom at risk. It is not what we would call an intelligent trade off. The film does itself and its audience a disservice by presenting it as one.

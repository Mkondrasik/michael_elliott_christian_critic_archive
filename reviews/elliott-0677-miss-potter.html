Author-verified transcription

Sometimes, sweet and simple is enough to tell a tale that will captivate an audience. In Miss Potter, nothing of earth-shattering consequence occurs - except to those individuals living the story.

Beatrix Potter (Renee Zellweger) is best known for her series of illustrated children's books (The Tale of Peter Rabbit among them) which, at the film's start, she is having difficulty peddling. Finally, a publishing house decides to accept her as a client if only to give the owner's youngest son a project that carries little consequence in case of failure.

The son, Norman Warne (Ewan McGregor) turns out to be a godsend. Enchanted by both Miss Potter and her stories, Norman works tirelessly to bring Potter's vision to the printed page and meets every exacting demand she places upon him.

The final work becomes a success and, much to Potter's surprise, her simple children's stories become the talk of the town. But there's more story to be told for Potter, a 30+ year old spinster, is beginning to have strong affections for her young publisher... and vice versa. But in early 20th century England, a union between a tradesman and a woman of means and social standing was deeply frowned upon.

And so we have the drama/romance of Miss Potter. Nothing other than the conventions of the time separates our lovers and even for them they seem minor inconveniences. Credit director Chris Noonan and his cast for evoking a time and place where such social niceties take on monumental repercussions.

Renee Zellweger is taking a risk by accepting this role which will draw comparisons to her Bridget Jones character. And while she is not altogether successful in delineating her very different English characters, she does project an innocence and appeal that works for the film.

Ewan McGregor continues to fight for the "everyman Englishman" title that Colin Firth has laid claim to for so long. Here, McGregor is every bit the proper gentleman and allows the requisite charm and boyish glee to shine through the propriety.

Noonan uses some animation to highlight Potter's unusual connection with the woodland creatures she draws. It's cute but doesn't go far enough to develop the mindset of his main character. Losing it would not have detracted from the story. As it is, his use of this convention is a distraction and, while understandable, doesn't effectively serve the story.

The greatest lesson to us in watching the film is in the way people handle class distinction. The logic of man sometimes defies explanation. Beatrix Potter's parents received their wealth through the inheritance of their family members (who were tradesmen). But because of their wealth, they looked upon their daughter's pending betrothal to a tradesman with dismay.

We are blessed that we have a God who does not have the same mindset. He does not first check our bank balance before deciding whether or not we are worthy of salvation. He does not care who our parents were or in what social circles we spin. All He cares about is who we are and what we believe. And He admonishes all of us to treat each other equally.

For there is no respect of persons with God. Romans 2:11 (KJV)

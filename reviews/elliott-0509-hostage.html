Author-verified transcription

In Hostage, Bruce Willis returns to the genre which launched his movie career so many years ago. But instead of the wise-cracking "yippee-ki-yay" cop of Die Hard, he plays Jeff Talley, troubled LAPD hostage negotiator.

As the film opens, Talley is hard at work trying to reason with an unreasonable man holding his wife and son at gunpoint. The tragic conclusion of that episode leaves Talley a broken man and strains the relationship he has with his own family. He leaves LA to take a less stressful job as police chief of a sleepy suburban California community.

When a trio of malevolent teens target an affluent accountant's SUV, they unwittingly launch a series of events which draw Talley back into the role he had left behind. Although the teens are holding the accountant's children hostage behind the walls of his heavily fortressed home, that is the least of Talley's concerns. The accountant's unsavory clients, concerned that their illegal activities may be revealed, have taken Talley's wife and child (played by Willis's real life daughter Rumer Willis) hostage and have threatened to kill them unless Talley recovers an encrypted CD from the home.

Thus, Talley is fighting against three "enemies:" the teenage sociopaths; the unnamed mob clients of the accountant; and the FBI who wants to handle the situation according to their playbook. There's also a fourth enemy - time... which is quickly running out on the increasingly desperate Talley.

Willis shines in these kinds of films and Hostage is no exception. The guilt and condemnation the character supposedly carried from the opening scene wasn't played consistently through the film but when Talley enters action hero mode, little else matters.

The subplot with the anonymous but well-connected mobsters also contained considerable flaws or gaps of logic. The filmmakers wisely kept focus upon the more interesting conflict - that of the teens who find themselves well over their heads and whose every decision makes matters worse for all involved. Jonathan Tucker and Marshall Allman play the brothers who foolishly align themselves with a dark and deeply disturbed kid named Mars played by Ben Foster.

Director Florent Emilio Siri cut his directorial teeth on video games and it shows in this film. Lots of attention has been paid to setting up the situations that the characters are in but the resolution of those situations are too easily achieved and not altogether believable.

The violence and the language are both graphic and extreme in nature, resulting in the film's R rating.

The big mistake the two brothers make in the film is in forming a relationship with Mars in the first place. He is obviously one perversely sick individual. By choosing to follow him they take the first steps that lead to their inevitable destruction.

God warns us several times in His Word of the dangers of associating with evil men.

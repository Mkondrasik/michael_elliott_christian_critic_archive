Author-verified transcription

The story of Esther, as told in the Old Testament record of the same name, is a wonderful tale of God's deliverance and power in the face of overwhelming adversity. It is therefore a pity that Christian filmmakers feel compelled to embellish this God-given record by fleshing out His story with their version of a Hollywood script. Unfortunately, the fleshing out that occurs in One Night with the King has more fat than muscle.

Esther (Tiffany Dupont) is a nice Jewish gal (born with the name Hadassah) who is chosen by the ruling king of Persia (Luke Goss) to sit upon the throne as his queen. Raised by her uncle Mordecai (John Rhys-Davies), she was instructed by him not to reveal her origins and to disguise her Judean ties from those in the palace.

When the king's evil advisor, Hamen (James Callis) decrees war against the Jews (to settle a long held grudge over an event that took place generations ago), Esther finds herself in a position to save an entire race of people from annihilation.

The fact that the Jewish holiday of Purim celebrates this event should give us some indication of the eventual outcome. But the film doesn't really do justice to the story or to the salvation that God made possible.

Producers Matthew and Laurie Crouch, director Michael O. Sajbel, and screenwriter Stephan Blinn took too many liberties with the record to appeal to biblical purists and weren't artistically proficient enough to appeal to secular movie buffs. As a result, One Night with the King will go down in the minds of most as another marginal effort of Christian filmmakers to break into the mainstream.

The relatively unknown Tiffany Dupont has moments that explain why she was cast as Esther. Unfortunately, they come halfway through the film. With a production that seems to want to draw our attention to its attention to detail, Tiffany seems a bit anachronistic. She's just a modern day gal dressed in the garb of an ancient. It is a bit distracting. As the film progresses, we buy into her character more but it remains a hurdle over which we must jump.

John Rhys-Davies is the best thing on the screen as Esther's uncle/advisor Mordecai. He's got a nice sense of time/place about him and adapts his acting style accordingly.

We shouldn't buy into the hype of Peter O'Toole and Omar Sharif "starring" in this film. O'Toole appears (as the prophet Samuel) for less than a minute. Sharif has a larger part (as Prince Memucan) but you might go through the entire two hour film without realizing you're watching him.

Still, it is a credit to Crouch and company to try and bring biblical records to life. My recommendation would be to read the book of Esther first, then watch the film. The book is short and only takes a few moments to read but will help you to discern the facts from the fiction.

The story itself is a story of deliverance. It describes how God placed a believing woman in a position of power and influence so that she might save His people. It is a story of revelation because He acted through Mordecai to prepare Esther in such a way that she would be successful.

To fully understand the scriptures (both Old and New Testaments) requires an understanding of the manifestation of holy spirit through which God communicates with man. Without an awareness of how "word of knowledge" and "word of wisdom" operates (both of which are revelation manifestations), the book of Esther has limited meaning.

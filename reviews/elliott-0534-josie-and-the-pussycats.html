Author-verified transcription

It is a good thing that cats are said to have nine lives. The three leads of Josie and the Pussycats would then have 27... and they'll need every one of them. The jokes in this satirical comedy bomb so frequently, the actors should receive "hazard pay."

Based upon the popular cartoon characters who were first introduced to the public via the pages of Archie Comics in 1963, the film follows the sudden shot at stardom given to an all-female garage band called "The Pussycats." Lead singer Josie (Rachael Leigh Cook, She's All That), bass player Valerie (Rosario Dawson, Light It Up) and airhead drummer Melody (Tara Reid, American Pie) are best friends and rock star wannabes even though the closest they have come to the big time is their poorly attended gig at the neighborhood bowling alley.

Into their lives enters Wyatt Frame (Alan Cumming, Spy Kids), a high level executive for Mega Records. Wyatt, whose last clients (the boy group DuJour) were tragically lost in a plane crash that he orchestrated, needs a new band... preferably a clueless one whose records he can use to place subliminal messages which influence the popular fads of the day, thereby controlling the purchasing decisions of the world's teenagers.

Thanks to Wyatt and his brainwashing machine, "The Pussycats" (renamed Josie and the Pussycats) become overnight sensations. This threatens not only their friendship, but also the freethinking future of the world's youth.

Writers/directors Harry Elfont and Deborah Kaplan (Can't Hardly Wait) simply miss the mark with this effort. While their target is a worthy one, their aim is faulty. The rock music industry, especially the obvious commercialization of certain group bands, is a prime candidate for satire and parody. Their other target is the marketing manipulation in which manufacturers engage to make their products irresistible. Here too, their pointed barbs land flat. Someone really ought to make a movie to humorously skewer both of these subjects. Josie and the Pussycats just isn't up to it.

Perhaps this is because it seems to indulge in the very practices that it supposedly spoofs. This film contains more product placements than found on a Nascar driver. Hopefully, it will make back its production costs on this high profile endorsements because it is doubtful that it will do so at the box office.

The three "pussycats" have such minimal character development, it is no wonder that they come across as rather bland. Tara Reid is probably the most enjoyable to watch but even her bubble-brained antics eventually prove tiresome.

The only cast member able to rise above the material and offer something resembling a redeemable performance is Alan Cumming who plays the scheming record label executive with a snaky charm that oozes duplicity. In contrast, Parker Posey (The House of Yes) plays the CEO of Mega Records as a muddled mess of conflicting characteristics. Her scenes are downright painful to watch.

The soundtrack should enjoy more successful sales than the film which spawned it. Filled with an upbeat punk rock mixture co-produced by Kenneth "Babyface" Edmonds (Waiting to Exhale), it boasts contributions from Adam Duritz (Counting Crows), Jane Wiedlin (The Go-Go's), Adam Schlesinger (Fountains of Wayne), and Kay Hanley (Letters to Cleo).

It is a pity that the film was not more effective in lampooning its targets because the materialistic mindset of today's youth is a troubling issue. The continual bombardment of commercial messages only serves to feed an insatiable compulsion to buy the newest and trendiest product on the market. This mindset often leads to debt, disappointment and despair.

God wants us to know that He will supply all our needs when we get honest with ourselves as to what those needs are. But when we desire "stuff" out of a sense of selfishness, greed or envy, we generally get what we deserve... nothing.

Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts. James 4:3 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

"First of May" is the moniker allegedly given to every new circus employee because, we're told, all the wonder of [circus] life still lies ahead of them. So say the makers of The First of May, one of the entries in the Florida Film Festival (June 1 - 20).

Unrated by the MPAA and still in search for a domestic distributor, The First of May is a sweetly told, family-oriented film about a boy nobody seems to want and his relationship with all those who ultimately welcome him into their lives.

Cory Wainwright (Dan Byrd) is an eleven year old boy who has been bounced from foster home to foster home. As he tries to acclimate himself to his fourth set of foster parents, he meets the aged but still feisty Carlotta (Julie Harris) who sees through his "invincible" facade and connects with his heart.

Soon the two are off on an adventure together. He is running away from his foster home before they can send him to an institution and she is escaping from the institution in which she has already been placed.

During their journey they manage to start a business, evade truant officers looking for Cory, and eventually join a circus. They also learn to support themselves, depend on each other, and love one another.

Newcomer Dan Byrd, as the young foster boy, is a major gem of a find for the filmmakers. He is allowed to be somewhat over-expressive at times (it's called 'mugging') but still he manages to be adorably appealing.

Julie Harris (Bad Manners) is wonderful as Carlotta, the lonely ex-circus performer turned nursing home resident. The relationship formed between these two isolated individuals is very believable. What makes this relationship work is that they each fill a void in the other's life.

Also in the cast are Mickey Rooney (Babe: Pig in the City) as "Boss Ed" and Charles Nelson Reilly (Babes in Toyland) as Dinghy. Tom Nowicki (The Waterboy) and Robin O'Dell (From the Earth to the Moon) have pivotal roles as Cory's newest set of foster parents.

The late Joe DiMaggio makes a brief appearance as himself, giving batting advice and a boost of confidence to young Cory after a dismal turn at the little league plate. While it appears to be a coup for the filmmakers to have landed Mr. DiMaggio (his last feature film appearance was in Angels in the Outfield 48 years ago), his scenes don't serve the film particularly well. They have the quality of being tacked on, or extraneous to the plot. Either the baseball scenes should have been made more integral to the story line or dropped altogether.

The film is full of good intentions. Director Paul Sirmons and screenwriter Gary Rogers, both making their feature film debuts, have clearly set out to construct a film that the entire family may enjoy. For that they should be well commended. However, in their execution, they have left room for improvement. The dialogue is stilted at times, character development is often clumsily handled, and plot transitions are a bit heavy handed, resulting in a film that doesn't flow as smoothly as it should.

Still, The First of May does have much about it to recommend. The circus scenes are delightful. The relationship between Carlotta and Cory is both real and touching. And it is refreshing to be able to watch a film completely devoid of evil villains, computer enhanced monsters, and language better suited to prison inmates and salt-encrusted sailors. This is truly a family film in the purest sense of the word.

Our need or desire to be a part of a family is one of the spiritual truths illustrated in the film. We know from His Word that God's will is not for us to go through life alone. We are called to "one body" it says in Colossians. We are to be part of the "household of faith" it says in Galatians.

By 'belonging' to a family or household, we have the means by which we may be strengthened, corrected, nourished, reproved, and loved. As the credits roll at the end of the film, we see the simple plea, "Consider Adoption." Perhaps if more people recognized the importance of the family unit, more would be willing to provide it for those children to whom it has been denied. It does make an enormous difference. We should know. After all, God adopted us, didn't He?

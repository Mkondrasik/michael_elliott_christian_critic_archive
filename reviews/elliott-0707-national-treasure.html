Author-verified transcription

National Treasure may go down as being the film that best describes the "Jerry Bruckheimer experience." It's big; it's bold; it's completely implausible; it's entertaining enough to be popular among the masses; and it's bad enough to make critics cringe and once again bemoan the fallen state of the art they love.

Benjamin Franklin Gates (Nicolas Cage) is following in the family "business." Unfortunately, it's a business most believe is run by crackpots. A few centuries ago, a secret was entrusted to the Gates family by the sole remaining signer of the Declaration of Independence who happened to also be a member of the society of Freemasons. The secret spoke of an unimaginable treasure hidden throughout time.

Gates, like his father (Jon Voigt) before him and his grandfather (Christopher Plummer) before him, has dedicated his life to searching for the hidden treasure. One obscure clue leads to another and the search has become an obsession - not so much for the financial reward but for the redemption of his family name that has been long been tainted by their foolish quest.

When the latest clue speaks of an invisible map on the reverse side of the Declaration of Independence, Gates knows he must steal the precious document in order to protect it from falling into the hands of those greedy for gain. Unfortunately, that group includes his ex-partner, Ian (Sean Bean) who may not be as smart as Gates but he's three times as ruthless.

Assisting Gates on his historical scavenger hunt are the wise-cracking Riley Poole (Justin Bartha), and Abigail Chase (Diane Kruger) the beautiful and once skeptical curator of the National Archives. These three must stay one step ahead of Ian and his cronies in order to protect the clues to the treasure left by our country's founding fathers.

Director Jon Turtletaub (The Kid) tries to keep the ludicrous action at a rapid pace but never quite finds the right gear for the film. It has the feel of a poor man's Indiana Jones story that might have an extra added appeal for any conspiracy theorists among us.

Cage knows he's in a "popcorn movie" and acts accordingly. He's not straining any thespian muscles here. It's a relaxed and easy-going performance. Bartha is amusing as the film's comic foil although there's no rationale to why his character exists outside the screenwriter's need for comedy release.

Parents will be pleased to note that the filmmakers have tamed this adventure movie to the point of earning a PG rating. The film's explanation of Masonic influence and the part that the Knights Templar played in the mystery may go over the head of the younger members of the family but they should enjoy the action sequences.

The characters in National Treasure are relentless in the way they pursue each clue - diligently examining and reexamining them for their hidden meaning because at the end of all the clues awaits a treasure beyond imagination.

Their intense passion is one we should emulate - not in search of gold or trinkets but in search of a far greater treasure.

Scooby-Doo 2: Monsters Unleased is the kind of film that critics love to hate. I'd like to tell you that I'm either with them or against them but, to tell the truth, it is hard to get fired up over this film. Count me as falling somewhere in the middle.

I can say this. It is a marked improvement over the ill-conceived Scooby-Doo film of 2002. But seeing as that film was an unmitigated disaster, it is hardly a compliment.

The gang from Mystery Inc. are treated as rock stars by the people of their home town, Coolsville. Known for their ability to unmask the many monsters that seem attracted to Coolsville, they draw crowds, groupies, and media attention whenever they make public appearances. The stardom is beginning to take its toll.

Shaggy (Matthew Lillard) and Scooby-Doo (a computer animated Great Dane with a speech impediment) feel unworthy of being called heroes because they're constantly making mistakes. Velma (Linda Cardellini) doesn't think she fits the profile of being a woman of mystery and intrigue. Fred (Freddie Prinze Jr.) is having troubles with the press as one reporter (Alicia Silverstone) keeps quoting him out of context. As for Daphne (Sarah Michelle Gellar)... she just enjoys being a girl.

When someone breaks into the new crime and mystery museum and steals the costumes of the monsters that the gang had unmasked in the past, they must find out who is behind it before he manages to make the monsters really come to life.

Scooby-Doo 2 is designed for the single digit age bracket and at that level the film is moderately successful. The characters are broad, the humor is silly, and the monsters are monstrous without being too scary. All in all, it is as close to being a live action cartoon as we probably would want to see.

Of the cast, Matthew Lillard certainly stands out as the most "cartoonish." Capturing the vocal inflections and mannerisms of his animated character with uncanny accuracy, he's actually interesting to watch. I also liked the work of Linda Cardellini who plays the brainy Velma. The characters of Fred and Daphne are simply too bland to merit much of a comment.

The message of the film is simple enough and communicated well enough that the school age audience should be able to recognize it. On the way home from the theater, parents could ask their young ones why they think Shaggy and Velma tried to pretend to be something other than who they really are.

Shaggy obviously wanted to feel like he belonged and Velma thought she had to live up to the image of being a famous celebrity. Both had to learn that a person can never find his true place in the world if he or she is pretending to be something they're not.

Our value or self-worth is contained in one simple reality -- our uniqueness in being who we are. It is the one thing we have that nobody else can claim. You can never be too young to begin learning to take satisfaction in being you.

The Apostle

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 20, 1998
Rating
2 1/2 stars
Running time
2 hr: 14 min
Source
Published Web Version

Scripture references

James 4:71 Corinthians 14:40Psalms 62:8

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Christian conduct; consequences; prayer; worship; power of God's Word

Author-verified transcription

In Hebrews 6:10, the Bible states:

"For God is not unrighteous to forget your work and labour of love, which ye have showed toward his name." [KJV]

The Apostle is nothing less than a labor of love for its writer, director and star. Academy Award winner Robert Duvall (Tender Mercies) wears all three artistic hats in this personal story about Sonny Dewey, a Pentecostal preacher whose actions are not always directed by the God he serves. Mr. Duvall literally put his money where his heart was by personally investing five million dollars and nursing the project through its years of development. It is not an overstatement to say that this movie would not have been made if it weren't for the vision and believing of Mr. Duvall.

And make no mistake, this is his movie. From beginning to end, we follow every move, thought, and prayer that Sonny makes. A charismatic evangelist who preaches on the dangers of sin, Sonny is ironically no stranger to harsh realities of its clutches. While temptation is not a sin of itself, it is Sonny's lack of resistance to it that has led to the break-up of his marriage, the loss of his ministry and the necessity to flee from his home town in Texas to avoid arrest for physical assault.

Landing in a small Louisiana town, he christens himself "The Apostle E.F." as he wastes no time in building a new church and congregation. His sincerity and his love for God are undeniable. But are they enough to repair the damage he has left behind?

As played by Mr. Duvall, Sonny's conviction that he was called by God to speak His Word is never in doubt. But as is soon evident, there is a great gulf which separates "speaking the Word" from "living the Word". Mr. Duvall's characterization of a man of God who has fallen short of his calling is fully believable.

Farrah Fawcett (Extremities), as Sonny's wife, gives a restrained, and therefore effective performance. Billy Bob Thorton (Sling Blade), in a small role as a combatant visitor to the "One Way To Heaven Temple," helps to provide the movie with perhaps its most powerful scene.

As far as Mr. Duvall's directorial skills go, let's just say that he is a wonderful actor. Perhaps with someone else at the helm, the movie would have found its proper pace and certain scenes might have been better defined or more fully developed. In his hand, the movie just kind of meanders along with nothing but its meager plot compelling it forward.

You would think that The Christian Critic would give any film titled The Apostle an unreserved endorsement. And, to be honest, it should have been very refreshing to have a major motion picture that predominantly features the Bible and people who believe in it. But the truth of the matter is, as a movie, it amounts to little more than a two hour acting exercise. Granted, Mr. Duvall is recognized as being one of the finest actors working in America today and his performance here proves that he is worthy of that reputation.

As a commercial for Christianity, The Apostle fails miserably. Sonny's rhythmic, Bible thumping manner of bringing his congregation to its feet in an emotional frenzy has little to do with God, the holy spirit, or true worship. There are some, I know, who practice this emotion-based faith, but they are not a representation of true Christianity. A real apostle, Paul, when confronting the Corinthians on the goings-on in their church, said,

"Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).

That's a scripture that Sonny must have glossed over because there is nothing decent nor orderly about his services.

That being said, there are, as would be expected, many spiritual truths that can be seen in The Apostle. One of the morals one can take away from the theatre is that there will always be consequences to the actions one takes. Recognizing the demeaning and embarrassing events that have been occurring in Washington DC with our president, that's certainly a lesson that bears repeating. All of the troubles which beset E.F. later in the film can be traced back to actions he could have controlled.

There is one scene, early on in the movie, that some people may feel is odd. Sonny is in his bedroom at night, loudly voicing his confusion and displeasure to God. There's no one else in the room, just Sonny, at the top of his lungs, and His God, who has no dialogue. It might appear strange to those who don't have that kind of personal relationship with their spiritual Father, but there is actually some scriptural documentation that supports Sonny's behavior in that scene. King David would oftentimes pour out his heart to God in just that kind of manner. Psalms 55 is an example of such a bold crying out to God. Many times it takes that kind of release before we can recover the peace needed to make a wise decision in the midst of spiritual pressure.

The most spiritually significant scene comes midway through the movie when E.F.'s new church is threatened with demolition. In a wonderful recognition of the source of true power, what saves the church from destruction is not the emotional fervor that Sonny is able to generate nor is it the physical prowess of the flesh. What saves the church is the simple, underlying, prevailing strength of the written Word of God.

All other flaws aside, and there are many, if only that one message - that within the Word of God lies the power of God - if just that one message could be communicated to those who see this film, The Apostle would live up to its name.

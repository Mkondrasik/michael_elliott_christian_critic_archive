The Art of War

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 25, 2000

1½ stars

Author-verified transcription

"Appearances are everything," one of the characters in THE ART OF WAR explains. The movie then proceeds to proves that statement to be wrong. THE ART OF WAR, though appearing to be a stylish, high energy action thriller is, in reality, an incomprehensible mess of a movie.

Wesley Snipes (U.S. Marshals) stars as Neil Shaw, a mysterious secret agent working for the UN on a level that is so deeply covered that officially, he doesn't even exist. His UN contact, chief of security Eleanor Hooks (Anne Archer, Clear and Present Danger) is working with the UN Secretary General Douglas Thomas (Donald Sutherland, Space Cowboys) on the eve of a delicate trade agreement being fashioned with China.

When the Chinese UN ambassador is assassinated, Shaw becomes implicated. Running from the police, the FBI, and even his own secret team of agents, Shaw must try to figure out what is going on and who is behind it. The audience spends much of its time trying to do the same thing. Believe me, it isn't easy.

After the assassination, Shaw's partners are killed and he inexplicably pairs up with an uncooperative UN translator (Marie Matiko, The Corruptor) who for some reason is also targeted by the bad guys for elimination. Heading up the FBI investigation is the quirky and perhaps clueless Capella (Maury Chaykin, Entrapment).

The muddled plot serves merely as an excuse for Snipes to show off his martial arts moves, his basketball moves, and his Matrix-like dodging bullet moves. For most of the film, we have a hard time following the story but once it is made clear, then we have a hard time believing it.

Canadian director Christian Duguay (The Assignment) attempted to give this project the high-tech, high gloss look of a Mission:Impossible 2. He managed some success but without the hook of a compelling or at least communicable story upon which to hang his artistic vision, the film fits the oft-used expression: All style, no substance.

The actors do little more than go through the motions. This is a film which is geared more towards the talents of the stunt coordinator (Michael Scherer, The Bone Collector), fight choreographer (Jeff Ward, Blade) and cinematographer (Pierre Gill, TV's Joan of Arc).

The violence (with a surprising preponderance of blood) is extreme, the nudity and sexual references unneeded, and the language is noticeably profane.

The title is borrowed from the writings of Sun Tsu, a Chinese philosopher and battle strategist of the 5th century BC who, in his writings, observed that "all warfare is based on deception." He further instructs on how it is possible to destroy your enemy without engaging in a physical war. He reasons that it is far more effective to attack your enemy from within.

Our spiritual enemy knows this lesson well and has used it many times in the past. Scriptures continually caution us about the danger of those who may try to infiltrate the household of God in order to deceive.

Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them. Acts 20:30 (KJV)

We must remember that we are engaged in a spiritual contest against a ruthless opponent who will use any weapon in his arsenal in order to win. In order to protect our hearts against those who come to us feigning love for deceptive purposes, it is vitally important to use the Word of God as the touchstone of truth. Instead of believing in titles, positions, or personalities, we simply believe in the eternal truth of God. With His Word as our guide, we can learn to prove all things so we may hold fast to that which is good.

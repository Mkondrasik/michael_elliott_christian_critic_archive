Author-verified transcription

It is clear from the start that The Man Who Wasn't There is a "mood piece." The mood may be strangely depressing and a bit off-putting, but it washes over us in wave after wave of black and white cinematography and in a style reminiscent of a 1940s crime drama.

Ed Crane (Billy Bob Thornton, Bandits) is a barber. In his own words, he "just cuts the hair." Obviously, he isn't known for his scintillating conversation. As a matter of fact, he hardly has any dialogue. This has the effect of giving Ed the same personality as the broom that is used to sweep up the clippings at the end of his day. When we do hear him speak, it is in a monotone, at a measured or studied pace, and usually in the voice-over narration which runs throughout the film.

His marriage to Doris (Frances McDormand, Almost Famous) is just as boring as he appears to be so we aren't all that surprised to find that she may be having an affair with her boss, department store owner Big Dave (James Gandolfini, The Last Castle). When opportunity knocks for Ed to get in on the ground floor of a revolutionary new industry (dry cleaning), he raises the needed capital by anonymously blackmailing Dave. The rest of the film depicts the consequences of that action as events start spiraling out of his control.

Those events include a murder, the subsequent police investigation and a pair of murder trials.

Although the circumstances of Ed Crane's life do begin to spin wildly out of control, what remains in total control is the performance of Billy Bob Thornton. As Crane, he is the very picture of restraint. His reactions to the events which threaten to engulf him are so minimal that it often appears that he is not reacting at all. Here is a man who does not shape his life to fit his dreams and desires, but rather chooses to sit back and just let life happen to him. The one active decision he makes in an attempt to change his life turns out to be a poor one and it becomes the pivotal moment upon which his very existence hinges.

Tony Shalhoub (Spy Kids) injects some welcome energy as legal huckster Freddy. Michael Badalucco (Summer of Sam) has some fine moments as Ed's jabbering brother-in-law and Jon Polito (The Big Lebowski) is appropriately oily as the dry cleaning entrepreneur.

What lifts The Man Who Wasn't There to become more than the melodramatic potboiler type of film from which it is obviously patterned is the directorial hand of Joel Coen (O Brother Where Art Thou?) and the masterful black and white cinematography of Roger Deakins (Fargo). Amidst the shadows and swirling cigarette smoke which appears in more scenes than not, the blandness of this man... one who is more mellow than dramatic... is contrasted by the sharp starkness of the images we see.

The life we have been given is a gift but it remains our choice as to how well we use it. Because we have a limited amount of time to enjoy our lives here on earth, we should not content ourselves with passively going through the motions of living. Life should be embraced and celebrated... not merely endured.

Whereas ye know not what shall be on the morrow. For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away. James 4:14 (KJV)

God commands us in his Word to be "redeeming the time." The word redeeming can figuratively mean "rescue from loss." There is much we can accomplish in this life; much that we can build and leave for others to use and enjoy if we aggressively pursue our life's choices rather than wait for "life" to choose for us.

As always, our choices must be based upon the truth that God has given us as a guide. As Ed learns, the wrong choices will also change our lives... and not for the better.

Author-verified transcription

The Rugrats meet the Wild Thornberrys in Rugrats Go Wild, a film combining the characters from the two popular animated Nickelodeon shows. The film also comes with a "scratch and sniff" card to add an aromatic element to the experience. Unfortunately, to my nostrils, everything smelled like stale cardboard. Oddly enough, that turns out to be the perfect smell to describe the quality of this particular film.

Those lovable, messy, and sometimes gross kiddies known as "rugrats" find themselves marooned on a deserted tropical island after their hopelessly incompetent parents book themselves on a cruise vacation aboard a rickety barge.

With no hope of rescue and their parents arguing with one another, the rugrats wander off into the jungle in search of Tommy Pickles' hero, Nigel "Strawberry," the intrepid nature channel explorer.

As luck would have it, the Thornberrys, including Nigel, are on the other side of the island trying to get video footage of a rare spotted leopard much to the chagrin of their two daughters who would rather be on a family vacation.

As the movie wears on, the characters from the different TV series start meeting and interacting with each other. One disaster naturally follows another as the groups get deeper and deeper into trouble.

In addition to the regular cast which give voice to their respective Nickelodeon characters, the film has the opportunity to bring in a few guest voices: Bruce Willis (The Kid) verbalizes the thoughts of Spike, the Rugrats' faithful dog who can speak to Eliza Thornberry, the "Dr. Dolittle" of the cartoon world. Chrissie Hynde of the rock group The Pretenders gives her voice to the spotted leopard who stalks the babies until she is distracted by the dumb but heroic Spike.

Frankly, while sitting through this crudely drawn and absurdly exaggerated story, I found myself wistfully thinking back upon the brilliant animation and precise storytelling of last week's Finding Nemo. Rugrats Go Wild is not only at a completely different level, they don't belong in the same genre. Fans of the Nickelodeon TV shows may find something of value here but for me, the Hollywood animation bar has been raised and this film is limboing under it.

The depiction of the adults in Rugrats Go Wild certainly doesn't help matters. Pettiness, incompetence and negligence are not parental qualities to which one should aspire. And yet, with absolutely no exceptions, these are the main features we see in the adult characters in the film.

The depiction of the toddlers' behavior is more understandable. Yes, they are disobedient, inquisitive, and gross. Yes, they will do things like parade naked, hide waffles in their diapers, eat bugs or mud, and manufacture smells that are so foul they defy description. But there's a reason for that. They are children.

Foolishness [is] bound in the heart of a child; [but] the rod of correction shall drive it far from him. Proverbs 22:15 (KJV)

Children are born foolish. It is part of their nature. We should know... we were once children. Kids need their parents to correct them, teach them, guide them on the path that leads to knowledge, wisdom, and right living. Without a parent helping his child to discern between right and wrong, the child will inevitably choose the foolish way.

The "rod of correction" is not a biblical excuse to beat a child. Figuratively speaking, it merely means that we are to correct our children using the standard of truth which can be found in the Word of God. God sets the standard, expects us to follow it and then teach it to our children and our children's children.

Rugrats Go Wild is an appropriate title for this film because without a standard of truth, we find ourselves in the wilderness without a compass, a map, or any hope of rescue.

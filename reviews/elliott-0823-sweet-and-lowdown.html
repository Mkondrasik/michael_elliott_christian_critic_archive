Sweet and Lowdown

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 10, 2000
Rating
3 stars
Running time
1 hr: 35 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Matthew 22:37-40Mark 12:32-33James 1:5-8

Sermon / spiritual topic: Love; Commitment; Wholeheartedness

Author-verified transcription

Emmet Ray was the second greatest guitar player in the world. At least, that is how he often introduced himself in Woody Allen's (Celebrity) comedic pseudo-docudrama about this fictitious musician of the 1930's.

In Sweet and Lowdown, Mr. Allen uses his well-known love of jazz music and knowledge of its history to have actually invented a character which is realistic enough that he could very well have existed. Emmet Ray is like a composite of various musicians known and revered by jazz aficionados.

The film alternates between "interviews" by jazz historians and others well versed in the genre (including Mr. Allen himself) and scenes depicting the make-believe life and mythical exploits of Emmet Ray.

We first see him in a pool hall, "managing" the careers of two prostitutes while an audience and exasperated music hall owner anxiously await his arrival on stage.

As heavenly as his music may have been, Emmet Ray was no angel. Given to overindulgence in alcohol, drugs, gambling, and women, he frequently mistreated people, suffered from kleptomania, was unreliable as a performer, and excused his boorish behavior with the oft-used and argument ending expression, "I'm an artist."

But while waxing semi-poetically about his own artistic abilities, he was quick to add that there was one who was greater than he: A French "gypsy" named Django Reinhardt. Mr. Reinhardt, an historical personage, is allegedly the greatest jazz guitarist ever to have lived. Recordings of Django's music are prominently featured in a number of scenes. Emmet is so awed by the playing of Django Reinhardt, he cries while listening to him play and even faints whenever in the great musician's presence.

Boasting a delightful score appropriate to the era, (Sweet Georgia Brown, I'll See You In My Dreams, I'm Forever Blowing Bubbles) performed by the amazingly nimble fingers of Howard Alden, Sweet and Lowdown offers a pair of Oscar-nominated performances.

The first belongs to Sean Penn (Hurlyburly) who plays Emmet Ray, giving the character a vulnerable sweetness at the same time making him obnoxiously obtuse when it comes to other people. Mr. Penn, who does not play the guitar, managed to mimic the dexterous finger control of a master guitarist to such a degree that he is very believable in the role, even when shot from close angles.

English actress Samantha Morton (Dreaming of Joseph Lees) is cast as Hattie, a mute laundress with whom Emmet, against his skewed logic, falls in love. ("Terrible things happen to people in love. I've seen it," he tries to rationalize.) Ms. Morton, sans dialogue, communicates more vividly and directly with her expressive face and body posturing than most other actresses manage to convey with a script filled with beautifully written words, a fact emphasized by her receiving of a best actress nomination for this performance.

Sweet and Lowdown is an enjoyable film, melancholic and wistful in its 1930's style, but somehow lacks the comedic punch or emotional catharsis to include it among Mr. Allen's "best" works. Like its central character, the film comes close to greatness but there is something that holds it back from achieving the highest ranking.

With Emmet, it is the withholding of himself that keeps him in second place. His reluctance to give of himself emotionally - to commit to another person - is subconsciously reflected in his music, which, while technically superior, is admittedly not as "soulful" as Django's.

As we choose to love God and serve Him, we are to do so with all of our heart, soul, mind, and strength. According to the scriptures, this is the first and greatest commandment. Anything less than all is second best. And as we know of God, He wants for us only the best. To achieve the best requires a fully persuaded heart's response to His call. When we obey with all our heart, soul, mind, and strength, we can be our best.

Notting Hill

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
May 13, 1999
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Fame; priorities; worldly temptation; spiritual focus; finding God first

Author-verified transcription

Romantic comedies, as a genre, are the soufflés of the film industry. When everything goes perfectly, they're delicious. But make the slightest wrong move... and they flatten like a pancake. Notting Hill is a soufflé that refuses to fall. Buy a ticket, take a date, and be prepared to be thoroughly charmed.

Julia Roberts (Stepmom) plays Anna Scott, an American film actress/icon (not unlike herself) who is in London finishing up her latest project. Her face is plastered on every billboard, bus and tabloid newspaper in sight.

Contrast that with the likes of William Thacker (Hugh Grant, Nine Months). He's a likable but amazingly unsuccessful travel book shop owner who couldn't get his face on a milk carton if he disappeared for a month. They live in two different realities... on two different worlds... worlds that are happily about to collide. And what a pleasure it is to see it happen.

Notting Hill takes a look at fame and love (albeit through rose colored lenses) to ask the question, "Can a superstar actress idolized by millions find happiness with a nice 'every man' kind of bloke?" The question is not as easily answered as we might at first assume.

A couple of chance run-ins sparks an interest. Anna finds herself being charmingly disarmed by the boyish fumbling of William. After inviting him to her hotel suite (in a hilarious scene where he is forced to pretend to be a movie reviewer from "Horse and Hound") they begin a "surreal" dating experience.

They go through the obligatory and certainly expected ups and downs of a romantic comedy relationship. Here the problem is one of intimacy. Not that they are unwilling to go there. They just don't know how to do it with hundreds of paparazzi camped out on the doorstep.

While Miss Roberts' smile is still dazzling and she is every bit the superstar that she plays in the film, the success of Notting Hill is primarily due to the winning performance of Hugh Grant. He is, in a word, masterful. Working from his particular strengths: his self-deprecating humor, his spot-on comic timing, and his irrepressible charm, Mr. Grant warms us to his character as easily as the morning sun warms a garden path.

The supporting cast, primarily Thacker's unusually unsuccessful circle of friends, does what a good supporting cast is supposed to do... they enhance the film without distracting from it. In one scene, they compete for the last brownie by sharing "who's got the most pathetic life" stories, which the rich and beautiful Anna tries to enter with her woes of perpetual dieting.

Rhys Ifans (Dancing at Lughnasa) who plays the thoroughly disgusting Spike, Thacker's flatmate, is allowed to go way over the top in how socially offensive one man can be. But his excesses are tempered by the wonderful restraint that Hugh Grant exercises in his reactions to him.

Tim McInnerny (101 Dalmatians) and Gina McKee (Women Talking Dirty) appear as a married couple who continually play host and hostess to their friends. She's in a wheelchair, he's known to be the worst cook in England and their relationship is both sweet, genuine, and heartfelt.

Director Roger Michell (Titanic Town) and screenwriter Richard Curtis (Four Weddings and a Funeral) are to be credited for keeping the movie on its delicate track and for recognizing the effectiveness of Mr. Grant's on screen persona which they utilize brilliantly.

Late in the film, Anna Scott tells William, "This fame... it's not real." In the media-driven society in which we live and which feeds itself on devouring every tidbit of "news" involving celebrities, it is easy to forget that it's not real. With the continual bombardment from the press on every minuscule detail of the "glitter" crowd, it is easy to lose sight of what is real in life and what is temptation designed to draw us away from that reality.

To live for God is to look beyond the fame and fortunes of the physical realm and focus on the spiritual realities of life. After all, today's celebrity is tomorrow's trivia question. God is eternal. To truly find lasting happiness, find God first.

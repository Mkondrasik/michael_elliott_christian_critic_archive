Author-verified transcription

Actor Ben Affleck goes behind the camera to direct his first feature length film, Gone Baby Gone. It is a powerful debut filled with enough twists, turns, and moral dilemmas to stymie even the most experienced filmmaker but Affleck handles the material with such confident maturity, it's easy to forget that this is his first time helming a picture.

Based on a Dennis Lehane novel, Gone Baby Gone deals with a child's kidnapping and its aftermath, asking deep and probing questions of us as we get closer and closer to learning the who, what and why of the case. A young girl vanished from her Boston home while her low class mother was off partying. Although the police vow to relentlessly pursue the case, the family brings in a young private eye team to "enhance" the investigation. Patrick Kenzie (Casey Affleck) is the young, naive Boston native with hometown connections that is hired to find out what he can regarding the whereabouts of the child. His inquiries lead to more questions which, when answered, beg us to consider even more important questions that speak to the core of our sense of right and wrong.

Affleck, who also co-wrote the film, strings the film along in a simple narrative but each time the story seems to end, another thread reveals itself and proves just as dramatically interesting as what preceded. It is an accomplished job of both masterful writing and directing for which Affleck should be notably recognized.

Casey Affleck brings enough of a baby-faced earnestness to his role to make the boy scout elements of his character work. His character is the prism through which we see the story and we need his youthful innocence (tempered with the street smart reality that comes with the job) to give weight to the moral questions that await us.

There is an abundance of onscreen talent in the film that deserves mention: Morgan Freeman is his typically sublime self as a righteous police chief; Ed Harris and John Ashton take top marks as a pair of cops willing to do the wrong thing for a right reason; Michelle Monaghan adds a nice texture as Kenzie's partner (both personal and professional); and Amy Ryan gives an award- worthy performance as the missing daughter's wayward mother.

Gone Baby Gone asks once again the age-old question, "Do the ends justify the means?" It does so by presenting a series of scenarios that don't necessarily offer tidy solutions. While the "right thing" might be immediately obvious to some, it may be equally true to think that the same solution may not be the "best thing." It is rare to have such a profound moral quandary drive a film and Gone Baby Gone is the better because of it.

Doing the right thing is not always the easiest course to take. But if the "right thing" is based upon sound biblical principle, it will always produce the right results.

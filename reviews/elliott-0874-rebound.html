Rebound

Author-verified transcription

Rebound isn't getting much of a bounce from an old and tired concept that, quite frankly, we've seen all too frequently. Comedies (The Bad News Bears, The Mighty Ducks, Kicking and Screaming) and dramas (Hoosiers, Coach Carter) both have long subscribed to this format with varying degrees of success. Rebound, in this most recent visitation to familiar ground, muddles through the setup and delivers next to no payoff. It is the cinematic equivalent of an airball.

Coach Roy McCormick (Martin Lawrence) is widely considered to be the best college basketball coach working in the CNBA. We just can't tell that by looking at him. He seems more interested in his endorsements and promotional deals than in his team or the game that they play.

When a courtside temper tantrum leads to a lifetime ban from coaching, Roy must swallow his A-list pride and accept a job (any job) in order to squeeze back through a loophole in his contract. If he shows "compliance" to the CNBA's standard of ethics and practices, he must be readmitted into the league.

Unfortunately, his reputation precedes him and the only offer that comes his way is a scrawled fax from the hapless players of his junior high school alma mater. How bad are they? Let's just say that they don't call them the Smelters for no reason. When they lose their first game 0-109, Coach Roy discovers that he'll actually have to teach them something about the sport in order to avoid national humiliation. Wouldn't you know it - they have something to teach him as well.

Director Steve Carr (Daddy Day Care) is painting by-the-numbers here while completely ignoring the fact that he's been given a monochrome palette. There's no build up; no development; and definitely no surprises. He shows us nothing of the process of going from worst to first. He simply expects us to buy into it.

His star has so watered down his trademark "edginess" so much (in order to fit the mold of a family picture) that he's managed to strip himself of all personality traits. The kids who call themselves "the Smelters" are instantly recognizable types with instantly forgettable names.

The only two characters that show any life or liveliness are a pair of female junior high sports correspondents who excitedly record their thoughts and observations into their portable recorder. The young actresses playing these parts seem to be genuinely enjoying themselves and their small role in the film. If only their enjoyment were infectious.

Coach Roy's mistake was a common one. He lost his focus. And he lost his meekness. The element that made him one of the greatest coaches in the league was his love and passion for the game which he placed above all else. When motivated by a pure and selfless love, success is sure to follow. We are driven to excel, to be our best and to put forth every ounce of effort. If we ever allow the idea of success to become our primary motivation not only do those successes become shallow and meaningless... they will also be fewer and less frequently experienced.

And whosoever shall exalt himself shall be abased; and he that shall humble himself shall be exalted. Matthew 23:12 (KJV)

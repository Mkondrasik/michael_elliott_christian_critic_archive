Author-verified transcription

There have always been some people who demonstrate more "school spirit" than the norm would suggest appropriate... but Max Fisher takes it to a whole different level. Max is the central character around whom the new movie Rushmore is built.

Appropriately, the movie is titled after the name of the prep school which, for the fifteen-year-old Max, is more than a place of learning... it is the definition of his very existence. President or founder of more school clubs than you can shake an eraser at, Rushmore is Max's life. That is, it was... until he met Miss Rosemary Cross, the new and beautiful teacher who has recently joined the faculty.

Max goes head over penny loafers for Miss Cross and, as he is wont to do with all his extra-curricular activities, pursues her with a sweet but relentless vengeance.

Enter Herman Blume (Bill Murray, Kingpin), a depressed and disillusioned multi-millionaire whose two sons (jerks, the pair of them) also attend Rushmore. Blume takes an immediate liking to the young, energetic, and wildly optimistic Max and they form an unlikely friendship. That unusual friendship will be put to the test as Blume becomes romantically interested in Max's Miss Cross.

Jason Swartzmann, in his film debut, delivers a star-making performance in the difficult role of Max. He manages to capture with pinpoint accuracy that impossibly awkward age of in-between. Not quite a man but no longer a boy, Mr. Swartzmann keeps Max straddling the maturity fence.

Bill Murray gives a subdued, yet multi-layered performance as Herman Blume. The relationship between Max and Blume works largely because of the textured work of Mr. Murray. Here is an emotionally troubled man who in many ways is as immature as his young friend. In Max, he sees a reflection of the man he may have once been. Or perhaps he sees the son he wished he had (instead of the two he so poorly raised.)

Director Wes Anderson (Bottle Rocket) presents a movie that is as "in-between" as its lead character. A sad but funny tale about both adolescence and adulthood... About dreams as well as reality... About belonging to something greater than yourself, while celebrating the uniqueness of the individual.

Max is as unique a character as they come. Highly imaginative, he writes original plays which he then stages elaborately. (For his final masterpiece, a war epic, he equips the audience with earplugs and goggles due to the explosions and flying shrapnel.) Yet he spends so much time engaged in his extra-curricular work, he never seems to get on top of the fundamental curriculum. In other words, he's flunking out.

Surely, all of us have experienced the problems that result when we misuse our time or poorly arrange our priorities. We can all get deceived into filling our days with the extra "stuff" that life brings, leaving no time for what is really important. God reminds us in His Word that we are to plan our ways and order our steps.

See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil. Ephesians 5:15-16 [KJV]

Walking circumspectly means to walk diligently. We must know where it is we want to go and not allow ourselves to be distracted from reaching our destination. And we must be aware that there is only so much time available to us.

It is ironic that attending Rushmore is one of Max's professed priorities, yet he does not take the proper action to ensure that he may continue being a student there.

Each of us have our priorities of life. As Christians, our relationship with God is at the top of the list. Never let the extra-curricular pressures or pleasures of life stop you from taking the necessary action to keep that relationship strong, vibrant, and dynamic.

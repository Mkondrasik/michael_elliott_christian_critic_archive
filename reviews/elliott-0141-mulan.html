Mulan

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 19, 1998
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Ecclesiastes 9:5-61 Thessalonians 4:13-181 Corinthians 15:52

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Death; resurrection; spiritualism; eternal life; return of Christ

Author-verified transcription

The Disney machine certainly knows its recipe for success:

Take one old and entertaining story. Make sure the main ingredient is a young, attractive girl who is willfully independent, yet still has a pure heart. Add a handsome and heroic male for a love interest. As a sidekick, introduce a sarcastically witty talking animal. Season with a villain who is as nasty as he is big. Garnish with a heavy handed dose of spiritualism and "Voila!" Instant Classic!

This recipe has worked yet again... This time with a distinctive Asian flavor as Disney brings to animated life, the ancient Chinese legend of Mulan.

When the Huns invade China, the emperor calls for reinforcements. Every family must send one male member to fight the Huns and protect the empire.

Having sired no sons, Mulan's infirm father is prepared to do his nationalistic duty, even though it will mean certain death. Mulan, to save her father, disguises herself as a man and answers the summons, thus fulfilling her family's obligations.

The story follows Mulan (or Ping, as she is known in her male disguise) through basic training, into her first combat, and culminates in a final showdown with the big bad Hun, Shan-Yu.

She is accompanied by her brave and noble horse, her lucky cricket, and her faithful lizard (dragon!) Mushu, who offers advice and commentary throughout with a dry and sarcastic humor.

Mulan, while fitting into the Disney mold quite effectively, does make a departure or two in certain respects. This girl is not one who must wait on her Prince Charming to come and save her. Quite the contrary, Mulan is more than capable of saving not only the prince, but his entire kingdom as well.

The vocal talent in this movie have been cast particularly well. A standout is the vocal performance of Eddie Murphy (Doctor Dolittle) who provides the voice of Mushu. His delivery is every bit as powerful and funny as Robin Williams' work in Aladdin.

The only thing that mars an otherwise entertaining and artistically rewarding movie is Disney's penchant for including elements of mysticism and spiritualism in their films. Here, borrowing their story line from an ancient Chinese legend, they promote the Asian belief of ancestral spirit protection. Mushu is actually a spiritual guardian, given an opportunity to redeem a past mistake (evidenced humorously by the decapitated spirit of his earlier assignment).

The idea of our dead relatives looking over us, watching us, guiding us, protecting us, is as old as death itself. But it is, nonetheless, a lie. There is no scriptural basis for the widespread belief that the dead are alive and in heaven now. In fact, the scriptures state just the opposite.

For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not anything, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten. Also their love, and their hatred, and their envy, is now perished; neither have they any more a portion for ever in any thing that is done under the sun. Ecclesiastes 9:5-6 [KJV]

Those who have died have no more knowledge of what is happening right now than those who may be in a deep sleep. In fact, God's Word uses sleep as an analogous reference for death, because His promise to us is that one day, all who have died will awaken. First, those who have been born again of His spirit, then eventually, all men who have died will appear before Him to be judged.

But that event will not transpire until the return of our Lord and Savior. Should we die before his return, we die comforted in the knowledge that we shall "sleep" until we are awakened on that great day when Jesus Christ shall appear again. That is God's recipe for success and one in which we can all share.

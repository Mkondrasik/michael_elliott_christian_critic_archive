Author-verified transcription

The Four Feathers boasts a hot and talented young cast; a director skilled in crafting an epic drama; and tells a classic story of fear, courage and friendship. So what went wrong? For all its promising ingredients, The Four Feathers is a jumbled mix of missed opportunities.

Heath Ledger (A Knight's Tale) stars as Harry Feversham, a dashing 19th century British soldier who seems poised for greatness. When news arrives of a Muslim uprising against the British rule in Sudan, Harry's friends and comrades prepare to be sent to North Africa to fight for queen and country. Harry, for reasons not fully explained, resigns his commission.

Branded a coward by three of his closest friends as well as by his fiancee, Ethne (Kate Hudson, Almost Famous), he receives from them four white feathers meant to symbolize his cowardice. Only his best friend Jack (Wes Bentley, American Beauty) refuses to take part in this rite.

Upon arrival in North Africa, his friends and former regiment find an enemy both brutal and relentless in their attacks. Harry, for reasons not seen or explored, decides to go to Sudan on his own, disguised as a Sudanese native in order to protect his friends from getting in harm's way. He meets Abou Fatma (Djimon Hounsou, Gladiator), an African tribesman who becomes a guardian angel of sorts for Harry. With Fatma's help, Harry ultimately makes his way to his friends where he can keep an eye out for their welfare while they remain unaware of his presence.

The film is based upon the classic novel by A.E.W. Mason and is the sixth such adaptation to have been made. While I haven't had the opportunity to see the other versions, of which reportedly the 1939 film with John Clements and Ralph Richardson is the most notable, I would hope that they tell the story more cohesively than this 2002 attempt.

Director Shekhar Kapur (Elizabeth) has put together some wonderfully expressive and grandiose views of England in the Victorian era as well as visually establishing the stark and unforgiving terrain of the North African desert. His intent certainly seems to have been to produce an epic film on the scale of David Lean's Lawrence of Arabia. Along the way, someone must have forgotten that at the center of any movie. even an epic.. a story must be told. The narrative line of The Four Feathers is so disjointed, it is difficult to keep track of something as basic as a sense of time. Because of the quality of what does appear on screen, I have to wonder if this is simply a case of cutting too much footage from the film in an attempt to meet a desired run time. If so, it is a pity. Here is a film that would have benefited from having more time to tell its tale more completely.

What is missing most is an exploration of Harry's motivation and conflict. We can see the action he takes, and parts of it are wonderfully filmed, but we are not given enough clues to determine why he takes the actions he chooses nor what conflicts he may feel while taking them.

At the heart of The Four Feathers, although it may not be altogether clear in this version, is a story about a man and his fears. Having fear is nothing new to man nor is it anything of which to be ashamed. It visits us all. A courageous man is not one who has never known fear but rather a man who continues to function despite the fear so that fear may be overcome.

Be not afraid of sudden fear, neither of the desolation of the wicked, when it cometh. For the LORD shall be thy confidence, and shall keep thy foot from being taken. Proverbs 3:25-26 (KJV)

Harry's courage is evidenced because he continues to act despite the fear he feels. We know this because he tells us. Too bad the filmmakers couldn't have had him show us instead.

Author-verified transcription

Ask a hundred people to pick the best western ever made, odds are that High Noon will sit atop the list. Winner of four Academy Awards, including a Best Actor nod for Gary Cooper (Meet John Doe) as lawman Will Kane, High Noon succeeds not only as a great story of the old West, but also as a timeless morality tale which remains as poignant today as it was when first seen in 1952.

It is Kane's wedding day and in deference to his new Quaker bride (Grace Kelly, Rear Window) he has decided to retire from serving as the town's marshal. No sooner is the ceremony finished that bad news reaches Kane. Frank Miller (Ian MacDonald, Apache), whom he had sent to prison five years earlier, has been paroled and is expected on the noon train. In fact, three members of his old gang are waiting for him at the train depot and when they reunite, everyone in town expects them to come gunning for Kane.

Even though everyone in town expects Kane to flee... indeed, they want him to do so, Kane opts to wear the "tin badge" one more time and stay to face Miller and his gang. He would like to receive some town support in the way of a deputized posse, but he's having some trouble recruiting them. Nobody, it seems, is willing to risk their lives to help the man who has helped to make their town a decent place to live.

Harvey, his official deputy (Lloyd Bridges, SeaWolf) quits; Amy, his new wife, makes plans to leave on the same train that is bringing his would-be killer to town; Kane's Mexican ex-girlfriend (Katy Jurado, Broken Lance) who also happens to be the ex-girlfriend of Frank Miller, Harvey, and possibly a few other townsfolk, is pulling up stakes and leaving town; all of Kane's friends are turning their backs on him and no one in town seems to appreciate the fact that he's staying behind. But stay he does because he is driven by something stronger than the opinions or approval of those around him.

Gary Cooper brings such depth of humanity to the role of Will Kane that it is difficult to see anyone else in the part. Kane is a simple man in a complex situation torn between self-survival and his own sense of morality and duty.

The rest of the cast are uniformly good in their supporting roles but it is the work of Cooper, director Fred Zinneman (From Here To Eternity), editor Elmo Williams (20,000 Leagues Under the Sea), screenwriter Carl Foreman (The Guns of Navarone), and composer Dimitri Tiomkin (Town Without Pity), whose original music was responsible for two of the four Oscars, that catapults High Noon into the rarefied air belonging to films that can truly be considered "classics."

On a side note, it is interesting to view the film from the perspective of its then contemporary audience. High Noon was produced at a time of heightened political tension. HUAC or the House un-American Activities Commission was in full force and many Hollywood personalities were being subpoenaed to testify on suspected Communist activities. Successful artists were pressured to name names or suffer the consequences of being "blackballed" in their own industry. Obvious comparisons were drawn in the context of the High Noon story of a town who refused to take a stand against a perceived evil.

It is made clear in the film that Kane does not want to stay to face Miller and his gang...it is just that he has to stay. Even though he's scared, tired, and has plenty of reasons to leave, his obligation to the ungrateful town and to his duty as a lawman compels him to take the action that he does. He recognizes that there are some principles or causes that are more important than even one's own self-preservation.

No one faced a greater test in this category than our savior, Jesus Christ. He knew the kind of pain and suffering that awaited him. But he also knew of his purpose and thus was able to subjugate his own interests or desires in order to do his Father's will.

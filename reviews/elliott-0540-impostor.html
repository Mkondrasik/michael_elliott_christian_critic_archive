Author-verified transcription

Science fiction often allows us a unique opportunity to examine ponderous subjects in ways that are both innovative and unconventional. In Impostor, the question that is placed before us is how can we prove that we are who we think we are?

It is the year 2079 and Earth is at war with the Centurions, an alien menace. The survivors of their initial attacks live in pockets of civilization beneath energy shields which, when operable, fend off further attacks.

Spencer John Olham (Gary Sinise, Mission to Mars) is a scientist who for the past three years has committed his life to the construction of an "ultimate weapon" for use against Earth's enemies. One day, nearing the completion of his masterwork, he is confronted by global security forces who state that he is not who he says he is. The technologically advanced Centurions have managed to build "replicants"... robots who look and act exactly like their human counterparts. Each replicant carries a powerful bomb which is triggered mere seconds before detonation. The catch is that the replicants are programmed to believe they are the actual person they have replicated and so even though Olham vehemently denies the charge, there's no way to tell if even he knows the truth.

Escaping his captors, Olham takes to the street, dodging the global manhunt that has been marshaled against him until he can think of a way to prove to everyone that he is human.

Impostor is based upon a short story by renowned author Philip K. Dick (Blade Runner, Total Recall) and consequently, the film has the feel of an idea to which much "filler" has been added in order to extend its running time. The concept might be an interesting one but there's not much there beyond that. Director Gary Fleder (Don't Say A Word) seems to mistake poor lighting for "atmosphere" and tries to build tension/excitement through shoddy camerawork.

Olham isn't much of a stretch for Gary Sinise who is merely called upon to scamper up and down darkly lit hallways and alleys. As his wife, Madeleine Stowe (The General's Daughter) is required only to look as confused as the rest of us. Vincent D'Onofrio (The Cell) plays the government agent saddled with the task of capturing these replicants and destroying them. Because his character opts to do so without any kind of a test to determine whether his subjects are human or alien, he comes across as a monster engaged in a witch hunt.

Tony Shalhoub (The Man Who Wasn't There), proving that he may have the best agent of the cast, departs the film relatively early on.

The most interesting element to the film is only touched upon briefly and thereafter discarded, never to be revisited. That is, namely, the question of what it is to be human. How does one prove that one has a soul? But even in asking that question, the filmmakers show their flawed reasoning because a soul is not unique to man. All breathing creatures who have been made by God possess a soul.

And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul (nephesh chay). Genesis 2:7 (KJV)

What makes man unique amongst the different life forms on this earth is not our soul but rather our ability to have spirit. Soul and spirit are not interchangeable terms. They mean distinctly different things. Upon no other member of His creation did God bestow spirit. It is through this gift of spirit that we can prove to ourselves and others who may doubt, that we are exactly who we claim to be... and more importantly, who God says we are.

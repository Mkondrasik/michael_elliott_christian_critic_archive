Author-verified transcription

There is a Catch-22 associated with film criticism of which the general public may not be aware. Whenever critics deliver a "thumbs down," their readers' expectation for that film is naturally lowered. This has the odd effect of increasing the possibility that they might actually enjoy it. Whenever we come expecting the worst, we are pleasantly surprised if the film isn't as bad as we imagined. Conversely, whenever critics praise a film, expectation is then heightened along with the possibility that the audience will be disappointed when the film doesn't measure up to the hype. In light of this phenomenon, all I can say is that Corky Romano is a very bad film which means you'll probably enjoy it (but now, having said that, you should be disappointed.)

The film is basically constructed using that same kind of logic. Mob boss Pops Romano (Peter Falk, Colombo) is standing trial for a myriad of offenses against his fellow man. The evidence against him is said to be rock solid, obtained by an FBI plant somewhere in his organization. Outraged, Pops gives the order to put his own man undercover to steal the evidence back from the FBI. Unfortunately, the only one they can trust that wouldn't be recognized by the feds is his outcast son, Corky (Chris Kattan, Saturday Night Live)

A veterinarian-in-training, this sweet, spastic doofus not only wouldn't hurt a fly. he'd give mouth to mouth resuscitation to heal one. When building his cover alibi, a computer hacker exaggerated Corky's "resume" so the FBI expects him to be a superspy.

The humor, both obvious and crude, comes from Corky's outrageous, non-superspy behavior held in contrast against the gray, button-down seriousness of a typical FBI agent.

Chris Kattan is a rubber-faced, wiry, extremely physical comedian who will go to any lengths to get a laugh. Anyone that determined is bound to get his fair share of them and, giving credit where credit is due, Corky Romano does invite laughter.. some of it is genuine, some of it is derisive. Out of the supporting cast, I have to say that I enjoyed seeing Peter Falk (Colombo) on screen again.

As the bedridden Pops, he doesn't have a lot of screen time but he still commands our attention when he comes into view.

Falk and Kattan are joined by Vinessa Shaw (Eyes Wide Shut), Peter Berg (Cop Land), Chris Penn (Footloose), Richard Roundtree (Shaft) and Matthew Glave (The Wedding Singer). As supporting cast members, their initial purpose is to play it straight, thus emphasizing or bringing out the humor in Corky's strangeness. When the film sticks with this convention, the humor does work to a certain politically incorrect extent. It is when the rest of the cast begin to demonstrate their characters' own quirks and idiosyncrasies that the movie's framework for laughs collapses. And because the film's premise is so silly, I can't imagine that the filmmakers were reaching for poignancy at any moment.

If they were, the point they might have been trying to make is that each of us is unique in some way and that we shouldn't suppress that. It is our uniqueness that makes us necessary to the body of Christ. Each one of us can offer something to glorify God that is uniquely ours alone.

This point is made in the scriptures through the analogy of the human body. If all body parts conformed to look and function like the foot, the body would lose its versatility.

And the eye cannot say unto the hand, I have no need of thee: nor again the head to the feet, I have no need of you. 1 Corinthians 12:21 (KJV)

It takes each unique body part working together for an individual to function at his fullest potential. The same can be said for each unique believer contributing to the body of Christ. So, celebrating what makes us unique may be the message the filmmakers were trying to send. It's hard to tell when the lead character's unique characteristic includes his ability to pull a python out of his pants.

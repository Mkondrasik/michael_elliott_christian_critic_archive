Author-verified transcription

The late Andy Kaufman alienated his audience as often as he entertained them. The new biopic of his life appropriately follows suit. Man on the Moon stars Jim Carrey (Liar, Liar) as the avant-guard and disputed comic genius who excelled at blurring the lines between reality and shtick... much to the delight and frustration of those who watched him.

After a brilliantly executed Kaufmanesque opening, we are treated to a brief glimpse of Andy's early childhood when he preferred doing standup routines facing a wall rather than interacting with people. Time quickly shifts to an adult Andy, giving the same childish routines to a live audience who give him the same response he once got from his wall.

The film traces his career from his standup days to his breakthrough appearance on Saturday Night Live, to his Latka character on Taxi, to his stint as the inter-gender wrestling champion of the world, through his battle with a rare form of lung cancer that would take his life at the young age of 35. And yet, at the end of the film, we are no closer to understanding Mr. Kaufman than we were when he was alive. Andy, we never knew ya.

Jim Carrey does an admirable job mimicking the machinations of the manic Kaufman, but somehow his tribute remains a pale imitation. When Mr. Carrey does the "Mighty Mouse theme" bit, a smile of recognition does cross our lips. But the gales of laughter that accompanied Mr. Kaufman's performance of the same bit just never followed. Likewise with the Elvis imitation. To this day, Andy Kaufman's impersonation is my personal favorite. Mr. Carrey gives it his best put it just isn't the same.

Joining Mr. Carrey is Danny Devito (The Rainmaker) as Kaufman's manager, George Shapiro. Mr. Devito gives an understated performance of a man who recognizes something special in Andy... he just isn't sure what that might be.

Courtney Love (The People vs. Larry Flint) plays Lynne, a woman who hates Andy enough to want to publicly wrestle him and then loves him enough to want to pretend to publicly wrestle him. Paul Giamatti (Private Parts) plays Bob Zmuda, Andy's partner and perhaps the only one who had an inkling as to what was real and what was play-acting.

Director Milos Forman (The People vs. Larry Flint) and screenwriters Scott Alexander and Larry Karaszewski (The People vs. Larry Flint) have pulled no punches for the sake of immortalizing Andy's character. His obnoxious invention of Tony Clifton is in full regalia and is no more palatable in this incarnation than he was originally. Andy's obsession with pushing the envelope of acceptability is demonstrated throughout the film with no more insight as to why than we were given during his lifetime. For Andy, bringing an audience to their feet in ire was as much a success as was bringing them to their feet in appreciation. He relished in leaving an audience confused and confounded.

Being an enigma and leaving people confused or uncertain may arguably have some merit in the world of comedy, but in the real world there isn't much profit to it. Because we represent Christ in the world, our communication must always be clear and precise. Our words should agree with our actions and both must agree with the truth of God's Word.

Let no corrupt (rotten or worthless) communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers. Ephesians 4:29 (KJV)

According to the film, near the end of his life, Andy was desperately trying to surround himself with positive energy in the vain hope to speed his healing process. This was an ironic fact that did not escape the notice of his manager. "You are the KING of negative energy," he reminds his ailing client. Hence we see a simple spiritual truth in application. One will receive the fruit of the seeds one sows. Andy Kaufman was a wonderful talent. But he was also a confused individual who in turn sowed confusion.

Baby Boy

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 22, 2001
Rating
1½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Titus 2:6-8Ephesians 4:11-131 Corinthians 14:20

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Maturity; responsibility; godly manhood; growing up

Author-verified transcription

Baby Boy gets its title from a theory which is postulated at the beginning of the film. According to the voiceover, today's young black men have been conditioned by society to view themselves as babies. As proof, we are reminded that they call their girlfriends "mama," their male friends are known to them as "boys," and they live in a "crib," Frankly, this whole baby theory sounds like a whole lot of pabulum to me.

At the center of this seriocomic coming of age tale is Jody (played by MTV personality Tyrese Gibson), a 20-year-old male who has yet to accept responsibility or find direction in his life. A father of two children by two different women, he still lives at home with his mother, has no job, and apparently little intention of getting one.

His days are spent "keepin' it real" with his "dogs" whenever he's not "doin' the nasty" with any one of a number of willing black women. It isn't long before we see signs that indicate his self-made cocoon which shelters him from the reality of adult life is about to unravel.

His young mother (A.J. Johnson, House Party) hooks up with Marvin (Ving Rhames, The Green Mile), an ex-con with a major attitude. When he moves into the house, Jody reads the writing on the wall. It won't be long before he's kicked out of the house to fend for himself on the dangerous and often fatal streets of South Central LA.

Both of his babies' mamas are tiring of his childish antics. The only job for which he is suited involves "boosting" clothes from delivery vans and selling them on the street. His best friend, Sweet Pea (Omar Gooding, Zoe, Duncan, Jack & Jane) is a street thug for whom prison is more of an inevitability than a possibility. Baby Boy follows the downward spiral that Jody's life has every appearance of being.

But for all the references of "keepin' it real," there seems to be an awful lot of artifice injected into the script. The situations are exaggerated and many of the scenes are played for their comedy rather than their poignancy. As a result, Baby Boy is not that successful as the social commentary that writer/director John Singleton (Boyz N The Hood) may have originally envisioned.

Taraji P. Henson (All Or Nothing) is very effective as a single mom who must choose between her love for a man who is not yet a man and her realization of what life will hold for them if he refuses to grow up.

Many of the supporting cast, particularly the females, resort to the head-popping, finger-snapping, consonant-deficient dialect which is quickly bordering upon cliché. It is a quick posturing of attitude which simply cannot replace the richness and satisfaction that comes from a fully realized and developed character.

Tyrese Gibson does stand out as the central character of Jody. I believe the film's shortcomings are more a result of the script and style of the director than of any other element. Not to say that the Singleton doesn't have a valid point to make... just that the point was diluted too much for it to make a substantial impact.

Our multicultural society would certainly benefit if more young men, of all races, would understand that maturity and the ability to accept responsibility are signs of great strength. They are qualities that not only bring respect, they bring a certain amount of power.

Young men likewise exhort to be sober minded. In all things showing thyself a pattern of good works: in doctrine showing uncorruptness, gravity, sincerity, sound speech, that cannot be condemned; that he that is of the contrary part may be ashamed, having no evil thing to say of you. Titus 2:6-8 (KJV)

As Baby Boy tries to demonstrate, there are a great many excuses why a young man, be he black or otherwise, might want to avoid growing up. The thing is. none of them are good excuses.

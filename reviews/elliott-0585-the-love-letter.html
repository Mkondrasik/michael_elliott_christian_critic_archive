Author-verified transcription

Some letters are better left unopened. The Love Letter starring and produced by Kate Capshaw (Mrs. Steven Spielberg) is a case in point. This romantic comedy wannabe has little romance of which to speak and even less comedic value. Its only “claim to fame” is that it had the courage to open the same weekend as the blockbuster “Star Wars - The Phantom Menace.” Rest assured that Mr. Lucas has no need to be concerned over the box office “competition.”

Ms. Capshaw (The Locusts) plays Helen, a forty-something bookstore owner in the quaint New England town of Loblolly-by-the-Sea. One day she discovers an unsigned, letter which poetically professes the author’s intense and passionate feelings. Of course, she assumes the letter is for her. But who’s it from?

Could it be from Johnny (Tom Everett Scott, One True Thing), a twenty year old college student, helping out in the store for the summer? Or perhaps from George (Tom Selleck, In & Out), an old friend who’s carried a torch for Helen since high school? Or maybe it is from Janet (Ellen DeGeneres, EdTV), a coworker at the store? The bottom line is... Who really cares? Certainly not the audience. These characters are just too vaguely drawn for us to become interested in their lives.

The letter starts getting passed around town, wooing everyone who reads it. This is an effective device that could have be used to some comic effect. But the letter is so badly written that it is hard to imagine anyone’s knees buckling at its reading. (“When I tie my shoe, when I peel an orange, I remain yours??” Oh, please... If this film ever makes it to become in-flight entertainment, keep the airsickness bags at hand.)

Ms. Capshaw never seems to settle on quite how to play Helen. The character is described as a not so very nice person with whom everyone in town is in love, but there is little in her performance that agrees with that descriptive.

Ellen DeGeneres, as a lonely, man-hungry business manager, suffers from her chosen and well-publicized lifestyle. It is difficult to accept her in a heterosexually romantic role.

Tom Everett Scott is attractive enough as the young man whom Helen believes is the author of her love note. But there is not much substance behind his pretty face and toned body.

Tom Selleck gives George, the fireman, a “gee whiz, have a nice day” kind of persona. Not a lot of depth, but his performance does match the shallowness of the script.

The best characterization comes from Juliane Nicholson (One True Thing), a freckle-faced bookstore employee who also becomes infatuated with Johnny. Her freshness is markedly appealing, especially when contrasted against the staleness of the characters which surround her.

Blythe Danner (The X-Files) and Gloria Stuart (Titanic) make all too brief appearances as Helen’s mother and grandmother respectively. Geraldine McEwan (Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves) also has a small but plot-turning role as Loblolly’s longtime resident, Miss Scattergoods.

By the time the real author of the love note is revealed, our interest has been so diminished that it no longer matters.

Director Peter Chan in his US directorial debut is never able to lift the bogged-down script of Maria Gaggenti (Incredibly True Adventures of Two Girls in Love) out of the mire of mediocrity in which it so comfortably sits.

Not all letters are as bland and uninspired as this movie version. The apostle Paul penned, at God’s direction, a series of seven letters or epistles to the believers of his day. Within these epistles is found the foundation of understanding for the spiritual period of time in which we live today.

The letters addressed to the Romans, Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, and Thessalonians contain the doctrine, reproof and correction we need in order to live lives that are pleasing to God. It is these letters that can truly be called “love letters,” for in them is expressed the love God has for us.

Author-verified transcription

The technical wizardry on display in the new computer animated feature based on a video game, Final Fantasy: The Spirits Within, is quite impressive. The filmmakers have managed to replicate lifelike images that look and move startlingly like the real thing. It is just that the shells they've constructed are empty, resulting in flat and lifeless characters. In others words, in what is undoubtedly a stunning but dubious achievement, the makers of Final Fantasy have succeeded in the artificial reproduction of bad acting.

The film is set in the year 2065 and the earth is in pretty bad shape. Under attack by a phantom menace, the surviving humans are having their souls ripped from them with alarming frequency. Only Aki Ross (voice by Ming-Na, Mulan), with the help of Dr. Sid (voice by Donald Sutherland, Space Cowboys), seems to have a possible solution.

Their plan involves collecting eight "spirits" to form some kind of organic wave which will somehow save the planet and all remaining human life. Aki Ross and a handful of soldiers are fighting more than the invading ethereal hordes. They are fighting the clock. Aki is infected with a phantom which threatens to grow within her like a tumor. To make matters worse, a general with "hawkish" tendencies (voice by James Woods, Scary Movie 2) wants to blast the phantoms off the earth once and for all by using a gigantic orbiting space cannon... even though it will probably destroy the planet in the process.

A number of talented vocal artists are attached to this project including Ming-Na as the determined heroine; Alec Baldwin (Cats & Dogs) as the courageous soldier boy who stands by her; and Steve Buscemi (Armageddon) as a wise guy soldier who provides the film's few moments of comic relief.

While I was disappointed with the film's illogical narrative, there is no mistaking the incredible artistry that has gone into this production. The detailed renderings of a world gone to waste are well designed and executed with exacting care. A pity that the script doesn't live up to the visual imagery that today's animators are capable of realizing.

The convoluted story will best be appreciated by those who might embrace the extreme "New Age-y," heavily spiritualistic philosophies which teach that all living organisms are part of the same universal life force energy. The movie places great emphasis on "Gaia," which is also called the "spirit" of the Earth. It is in conflict with the alien "Gaia" to whom the phantoms belong. It is pretty much gobbledygook as well as being a major perversion of the spiritual realities that we read about in the scriptures.

Gaia (or Gaea) is not a name dreamed up for this film. It is a name that comes from Greek antiquity... the representation of the pagan belief of a "mother goddess," the earth as a living entity which nourishes and sustains its "children." Even though this is a concept which is unsupported by scripture (in fact, it is in total disagreement with biblical truths), it nevertheless is an idea that has recently enjoyed a resurgence in popularity.

In the late 70s, NASA sponsored scientist Dr. James Lovelock, along with microbiologist Lynn Margulis, developed the "Gaia Hypothesis," a scientific postulation which presented a theory on how the earth self-regulates making it possible to support and sustain life. Many New Age followers then took and manipulated this theory to help support their own unbiblical belief that there is a "Mother Earth" to whom we owe worship.

Serenity

Original publication date
September 30, 2005

Sermon / spiritual topic: Free Will; Choice; Obedience

Author-verified transcription

If Serenity looks familiar to you, it's understandable. If it doesn't... that's understandable too. The film reunites the cast of Josh Whedon's Firefly, the short-lived sci-fi TV series that garnered a small but loyal following despite its shabby treatment by FOX in 2002.

The world of Serenity is set some 500 years in the future after man has migrated across space to another solar system containing dozens of "terraform planets and hundreds of moons." The ruling authority, The Alliance, seeks only peace and harmony... even if they have to kill or brainwash every man, woman, and child to attain it.

Serenity is captained by Mal Reynolds (Nathan Fillion) who was on the losing side of the interstellar war that left the Alliance in charge. Now a Hans Solo-type mercenary, he travels the planets in his space "boat" - bending laws just enough to keep his belly full and his craft intact.

His crew consists of a fiercely loyal second in command, Zoe (Gina Torres); her husband Wash (Alan Tudyk), a crackerjack pilot; Jayne (Adam Baldwin), a muscle-bound, gun-toting maverick; and Kaylee (Jewel Staite), a much cuter ship's engineer than Scotty ever was.

Along the way they also picked up two more stragglers: Simon (Sean Maher), a doctor who has just rescued his sister River (Summer Glau) from the clutches of the evil Alliance. It seems she can read minds and when being observed by high-ranking members of that group, she learned their darkest secrets. Now they will destroy worlds in order to find her and keep her from exposing them. Of course Mal didn't know this when he brought them aboard his ship.

Serenity is the legacy of Star Wars - (the original film, not the overblown later versions). A mix of sci-fi and westerns, it provides distinct and memorable characters, a simple plot with life or death implications, and a nice mix of humor, drama, pathos, and excitement. All in all, it is the rousing adventure we wanted Lucas to continue making. It just took writer/director Josh Whedon (Buffy the Vampire Slayer) to do it.

The cast makes the transition from small to big screen with little difficulty. The dialogue between crew members is often playful and amusing with just the right amount of B-movie cheesiness.

As others have noted, Whedon does give enough of a back story to allow this movie to stand on its own so people unfamiliar with the series have an opportunity to see and enjoy it. However, to fully appreciate the film it would greatly help if you watched an episode or two of Firefly to bond with the characters and enter the theater with some emotional investment. It sure seemed to help the fan club members I sat with on the film's opening day.

Among the fans of the series are Christian believers. Like the show, the film espouses moral principles and virtues in a palatable and thought-provoking fashion. The overriding theme here is man's right to choose the kind of life he will lead. The Alliance would have men live in peace by forcing them to bend to the governmental will (and by eliminating those who will not.) This differs greatly from how God approaches the same matter. God encourages us to lead righteous and virtuous lives but he leaves the decision to actually do so up to us.

Attempting to suppress free will is a desperate act of an authority which fears its own subjects. Thankfully, our God loves us enough to want us to freely make the decision to follow his ways.

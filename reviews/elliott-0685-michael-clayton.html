Author-verified transcription

A fixer. A janitor. A miracle man. These are the descriptions ascribed to the titular character of Michael Clayton. Clayton (George Clooney) is a problem solver for a big, high-level law firm that is prone to getting itself into big, high-profile messes. Now, as he faces what may be his biggest challenge, Michael's got the additional burden of facing personal and financial problems that he hasn't been able to solve.

After a brief out of sequence scenario that shows Clayton in action, we jump back to the beginning of the story where Clayton is asked to troubleshoot a particularly delicate matter. A chief legislator of the firm (Tom Wilkenson) has apparently gone bonkers and stripped naked while depositioning a witness. The class action suit he was working on has lasted years and has cost millions. This embarrassment could cause a backlash and possibly a mistrial that would ruin the law firm.

Clayton will need a big broom to sweep this mess out of sight and he's not on his best game. Deep in debt and unhappy with where his life has led him, he is stoically facing a mid-life crisis that stems from his growing realization that the decisions he's made in his life may have been expedient but weren't always wise. He's certainly not where he thought he would be nor is he in a place he ever really desired.

The script, by first timer Tony Gilroy (who also directed) is not without its flaws but it is an intelligent treatment of people who rationalize behavior that is neither ethically nor morally right. The script and direction both demand more than is typically expected from modern audiences. But those who stay engaged will be rewarded with a decent payoff.

Clooney gives a controlled, even subdued performance that almost isn't enough to carry the film. But the strength of the story and his consistency draws us in until the final reel which is as finely conceived, acted and directed as a film can get.

Tom Wilkenson plays the confused, over-medicated attorney almost too well but reveals the clarity behind the cloud when needed. Tilda Swinton is given a plum role as a newly promoted department head who learns that the phrase "swimming with the sharks" takes on a whole new perspective when one is called upon to be the shark.

The central question in Michael Clayton is one that we all must ask ourselves at one point or another: Where are we going and how do we want to get there? More importantly, is getting what we want worth losing who we are?

For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? Mark 8:36 (KJV)

It is this aspect that should draw our attention for none of the characters of Michael Clayton would consider themselves to be "bad men" or "bad women." They were people who made certain decisions that allowed them to get their jobs done. The fact that those decisions were based upon flawed logic and questionable moral fiber was immaterial. They examined their options and chose what they believed would work. They were successful, competent, and 100% wrong.

We cannot do the wrong things for the right reasons or the right things for the wrong reasons and be blessed by God. Doing the right thing because it is right is not always easy... but it is ultimately rewarded by the only judge that matters.

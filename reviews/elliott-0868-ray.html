Author-verified transcription

Genius is an overused description that is more often than not misapplied. However, in the case of Ray Charles Robinson there are few who would dare disagree. It just fits. Ray reminds us why the late Ray Charles is universally considered a genius in his field.

Dropping the Robinson early on in his musical career so as not to be confused with the boxer, Ray Charles made a habit of taking music and the music business in new and unexplored directions - always with incredible and often surprising success.

In Ray, his life’s story has been condensed to a mere 2 ½ hours. It could easily have been twice as long just by playing more of his incomparable music which spanned more than five decades. But there was more to the man than his music and not all of it was melodic.

We follow his arc from a poor boy in Florida who was losing his sight; through the lean years as a struggling musician who had to be constantly vigilant that those around him wouldn’t take advantage of his blindness; to the renowned musician, composer, and innovator that he eventually became. Through it all Ray Charles was uniquely his own soul.

The film does not turn a blind eye to Ray’s weaknesses and indiscretions. Though he deeply loved his wife, he wasn’t able (or willing) to resist the availability of other women. Although he vehemently cautioned others against the dangers of using drugs, he was for many years hooked on heroin. Though he was a black man at a time of tremendous segregation, he was a late entry into the crusade for civil rights. (In fairness, when he did make a stand, it had a tremendous effect.) Through it all, he continued to make music which transcended genres and did more to unite the country than a dozen “million men marches” could ever hope to accomplish.

Unfortunately, Taylor Hackford’s treatment of Ray’s life is full of the typical Hollywood treacle. The one element which lifts it and makes it well worth seeing can be summed up in two words: Jamie Foxx. His portrayal of the legendary musician is nothing short of phenomenal. There are times during the film when it becomes all too easy to forget that we’re watching an actor playing the part of Ray Charles. His impersonation is that uncannily accurate.

So amazing is his performance that the work of his supporting cast may be overlooked. Curtis Armstrong and Richard Schiff, as Ray’s partners at Atlantic Records provide solid support.

Ray also contains a number of very strong female roles which are ably handled. Kerry Washington plays Charles’ long suffering wife with a quiet dignity. Regina King sizzles as Margie Hendricks one of Ray’s back up singers who does more than just back him up. And Sharon Warren makes a dynamite debut as Ray’s incredibly astute mother who teaches him to never to let his blindness stop him from becoming the man that was in him to become.

“Don’t let nobody or nothing turn you into no cripple,” she commands the young 7-year-old Ray. By refusing to make allowances for his “disability,” she built in him the self-reliance and strength that would serve him for his entire life.

Our weaknesses, and we all have them, should never stop us from accomplishing any goal we set before ourselves. For even if our bodies are weak, our faith can still be strong and when our believing is strong, that’s when the impossible can and does happen.

If thou faint in the day of adversity, thy strength is small. Proverbs 24:10 (KJV)

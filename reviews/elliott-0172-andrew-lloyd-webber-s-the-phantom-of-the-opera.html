Andrew Lloyd Webber's The Phantom of the Opera

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 22, 2004
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

2 Corinthians 10:102 Corinthians 10:7Mark 9:43

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Inner character; appearances; heart; spiritual strength; influence

Author-verified transcription

The music of the night plays on in Andrew Lloyd Webber's theatrical juggernaut, The Phantom of the Opera. The screen version reminds us of the intense passion to be found in the stage play but it fails to match it.

All of the elements are there - the classic story (from the novel by M. Gaston Leroux); the huge chandelier; the disfigured titular character; and, of course, the haunting score by Andrew Lloyd Webber. It is just that the secret ingredient that made the live production so emotionally overwhelming is missing.

Emmy Rossum makes a beautiful Christine, the young ingénue who is elevated to star status at the Paris Opera House when a petulant diva (Minnie Driver) abruptly leaves a production. Christine has been coached and guided in her singing career by a mysterious "angel." He turns out to be the infamous Phantom of the Opera, a man who lives in the bowels of the opera house and wears a mask to disguise his hideous facial deformity.

The Phantom's motives are transparent to all but Christine. In addition to being her mentor he desires to be her lover but knows that one look at his face will destroy any romantic feelings she might be able to muster for him.

When the Opera's handsome young patron, Raoul (Patrick Wilson), also sets his sights on Christine the Phantom feels betrayed and his emotions turn dark and dangerous.

While the voices are incredibly strong (Rossum's soprano is especially pleasant - crisp and clean without being shrill) they often have been directed to over-emote while singing which results in giving a modern 20th century feel to this 19th century story.

Adding comedic touches are Minnie Driver as a foreign diva with an attitude problem and Simon Callow and Ciaran Hinds as the new owners of the opera house.

Joel Schumacher has directed the film and seems to have a difficult time keeping the camera still. He is constantly panning or sweeping during scenes as if he were overly concerned that we might miss an inch of the detailed set designs and costumes. It is a technique that grows tiring as the movie drags on. And drag on it does for almost two and a half hours.

Of course, for those who love the music the obvious flaws of the film will be minor and forgivable.

The real tragedy of the film's story can be found in how the Phantom allowed a facial disfigurement to affect his entire life and turn him down a path of darkness. As Christine so eloquently observes, his real deformity lay in his heart and not upon his face.

Protecting our heart (which is a figurative reference to our core beliefs) is of the utmost importance. It is available to keep our hearts strong and pure despite outward circumstances or influences. There are many examples of men and women who did just that to be found within the pages of the Bible. We can see from his writings that the apostle Paul was one of them.

For his letters, say they, are weighty and powerful; but his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible. 2 Corinthians 10:10 (KJV)

Paul did not allow his less than impressive appearance stop him from making a positive impact upon the world. It is an impact that we are still feeling some 20 centuries later.

His story is the mirror opposite of the Phantom's. Paul learned that external circumstances need not have a detrimental influence on the power of the inner man.

Around the World in 80 Days

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 16, 2004
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Proverbs 9:9 Proverbs 1:5 Isaiah 5:21

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Learning; wisdom; humility; growth

Author-verified transcription

Question: When is Phineas Fogg not the central character of Around the World in 80 Days? Answer: When Jackie Chan signs on to play Fogg’s French valet Passepartout. But the good news is that, while it is hardly a faithful rendition of Jules Verne’s classic 19th century adventure, this version manages to be an enjoyable and fanciful family film.

Fogg (Steve Coogan) is an eccentric English inventor whose constant push for progress is a bit of an irritant among the traditionally-minded members of the Royal Academy of Science. A wager is struck that if Fogg cannot circumnavigate the globe in eighty days, he will cease from all future inventing. If he succeeds, he will become the new Minister of Science.

Joining him on his journey is Passepartout (Jackie Chan), a Chinese man pretending to be French who has just robbed the Bank of England and needs quick passage to his home in China. By signing on as Fogg’s French valet (on his mother’s side), Passepartout is given a cover that safely gets him out of England but puts him squarely in a madcap race against time.

Their travels are made more difficult by the Asian thugs out to stop Passepartout and the English cop hired by the acting Minister of Science (Jim Broadbent) to stop Fogg at any cost.

We follow their slapstick escapades across the globe as Fogg and his valet travel by boat, balloon, train, rickshaw, and wheeley-shoes. Unlike the 1956 film which starred David Niven and was more or less a glorified travelogue, this version focuses more on the acrobatic martial arts antics of Chan and the shy romance between Fogg and Monique (Cecile de France), a French artist who joins the trek as they pass through Paris.

The grand scope of the story allows the filmmakers to flood the screen with an endless stream of celebrity cameos. Gov. Arnold Schwartzenegger, Kathy Bates, Owen and Luke Wilson, Rob Schneider, John Cleese, and Mark Addy all make brief and instantly forgettable appearances in the film.

Director Frank Coraci is perhaps best known for his collaborations with Adam Sandler, having directed him in both The Wedding Singer and The Waterboy. One would think that his earlier works wouldn’t have prepared him for a project that boasts a much larger budget and stems from a classic piece of literature. Based on the end result it would appear that Coraci was successful in making the transition. The film is well paced and certainly entertaining in a family friendly sort of way.

Fogg is initially goaded into making his wager by the members of the Royal Academy who hold the absurd belief that “everything worth inventing has already been invented.” Such shortsightedness is a sure sign of foolishness and an unbridled ego. Man will always have new horizons for which to reach. It is in our very nature to develop, grow, and learn as we pass through this life. To stop learning is to stop living.

Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be yet wiser: teach a just man, and he will increase in learning. Proverbs 9:9 (KJV)

It is a wise man who understands how little he knows and how much he has yet to learn. Only a fool will think he knows it all.

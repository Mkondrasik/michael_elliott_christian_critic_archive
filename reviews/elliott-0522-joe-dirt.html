Author-verified transcription

Every so often a character will come along which captures the fancy of a movie-going public, catapulting that character and the movie in which he appears into the rarefied air belonging to movie "icons." For every one of those special characters, there are thousands of easily forgettable ones like Joe Dirt.

To be fair, Joe Dirt is actually a better film than one might be led to believe. Not only does it have structural merit, it has a few decent performances and a worthwhile message at its heart. Unfortunately, someone should have told the filmmakers that comedic ka-ka jokes have long overstayed their welcome. There's very little humor left in watching a septic tank spill its contents.

Joe Dirt (David Spade, The Emperor's New Groove), armed with a mullet-cut wig, a 70's hard rock attitude, and a janitor's mop, is invited onto the radio airwaves to relate the story of his pathetic life. At first intended to be an object of ridicule by the acerbic talk show host (Dennis Miller, Saturday Night Live), Joe and his irrepressible optimism strike a chord with the radio audience and their jeers turn to cheers by the end of the film.

Life hasn't been easy for young Joe Dirt. Born without the top part of his skull, a mullet cut wig was placed on his head for aesthetic reasons. Who knew that it would become fused to his bones? His parents "lost" him at the Grand Canyon when he was eight. He's been living off his wits since then and as we might expect... the living's been mighty meager.

Throughout most of the film, people treat Joe like... well, like dirt. Stumbling upon Silvertown, a pretty-as-a-postcard little village, Joe finds the first place that feels like home and the first real friend he's ever had. The "out-of-Joe's-league" Brandy (Brittany Daniel, Dawson's Creek) takes a liking to our anachronistic hero but still he feels "a hole in his heart" so he sets out in search of his parents.

Among the many strangers he meets along the way are an Indian fireworks salesman (Adam Beach, Smoke Signals), a hot and sexy carnival patron who just might be his long lost sister (Jaime Pressly, Tomcats), an ex-mobster enrolled in the Witness Protection Program (Christopher Walken, The Opportunists), and the owner of an alligator farm (Rosanna Arquette, The Whole Nine Yards).

First time director Dennie Gordon, working from a script by David Spade and co-writer Fred Wolf (Dirty Work) relies far too heavily on sexual and scatological humor. In addition to the septic tank scene, Joe finds a meteor which turns out to be frozen waste from a passing airliner; discovers quite accidentally that methane is flammable; and helps a pooch who has his canine cojones frozen to the porch.

The pity of the film is that Spade actually does give a sweet and endearing performance as a white trash loser with a good soul. His continual upbeat attitude in the face of all the negative reactions thrown his way is the very thing that changes the people's reactions to him. His phraseology indicates his relentlessly positive mindset: "Keep on keepin' on" and "You can't have no in your heart" are words from someone who won't let a little adversity get him down. This is a lesson that is a worthy one to learn. It's just a shame that the filmmakers chose for Joe to suffer through such ridiculous and distasteful situations just to make their point.

Continuance in the face of adversity is an exhortation we all receive. Just because God is on our side doesn't mean that we won't face problems in our lives. But God promises us that if we "keep on keepin' on," we will emerge victorious.

Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us. Romans 8:37 (KJV)

One of the key words in that scripture is the word "in." We are more than conquerors in the midst of whatever trials, tribulations, conflicts, pressures, or temptations befall us. There is no reason to quit or give up. God promises us the victory as we continue to move forward with His Word as our guide.

Author-verified transcription

Part of the fun of the “cheesy” old TV shows which my generation used to watch could be found in the very fact that they WERE “cheesy.” Plots were ludicrous, set pieces were obviously made of foam rubber, costumes looked like they were sewn together from old upholstery swatches, and special effects were pretty much non-existent. We saw it... we knew it... and yet, we forgave it. What television couldn’t afford to produce (or didn’t yet have the technology to produce well), our imagination provided. Today’s audiences aren’t required to use their imagination and they are the poorer for it.

Hollywood’s latest attempt to remake a TV classic is Lost In Space based on Irwin Allen’s sci-fi series which ran from 1965 - 1968, starring Guy Williams, June Lockhart, and Jonathan Harris. You know the story of this interstellar version of Swiss Family Robinson. The Robinson family is on a spaceship that, due to sabotage, has gone way off course (much like this movie) and is now wandering lost amidst the cosmos, looking for a way home.

The movie version stars Gary Oldman (Air Force One), William Hurt (Broadcast News), Mimi Rogers (Austin Powers), Matt LeBlanc (TV’s Friends), Heather Graham (Boogie Nights), Lacey Chabert (TV’s Party of Five), and Jack Johnson (The Tie That Binds). If you don’t blink you may even see some of the actors from the original TV series. June Lockhart, Angela Cartwright, Marta Kristen, and Mark Goddard appear in small and insignificant roles.

All the other familiar pieces are in place: The robot (“Danger, Will Robinson!”), the Jupiter II, the mechanical spiders... Okay, so the spiders are new. Still, you’ll see and enjoy the recognizable shell of the story. It is only the filling within the shell that ultimately fails to satisfy.

Director Stephen Hopkins (Blown Away) has a lot more “toys” at his disposal than did the original TV producers. For this reason, he is able to leave nothing to our imagination. Computer animation, robotics, and digital effects can now fill in the gaps that we used to ask our minds to fill. Somehow, our imagination seemed considerably more real.

The TV series was campy, but always true to its own concept (which of course is where much of the campiness was born.) The movie is simply silly and all too ready to abandon its premise to chase a forced joke (like an out-of-place reference to the Waltons, another TV staple of the past) or an ill-fitting characterization.

Mr. Hurt chooses to portray Prof. Robinson as a man with less personality than the robot. As a matter of fact, the Jupiter II displays more personality than does Mr. Hurt. Ms. Rogers, as his wife Maureen, reaches to the same lack of depth. The only standouts in the entire cast are Mr. Oldman who seems to relish in his role as the diabolically clever Dr. Zachary Smith (as did his TV counterpart), and Miss Chabert as the young, wryly funny Penny Robinson. These two actors at least bring a little life to their characters which make them interesting to watch. The others appear to be serving as those foam rubber set pieces mentioned earlier. Perhaps they were expecting for the animation team to enhance their performances electronically.

The Robinson’s initial mission of space exploration was nebulously explained to be necessary due to man’s misuse and abuse of the earth’s natural resources. Set in the year 2058, the earth is apparently two decades away of losing all its capability to support human life.

This may be a message that today’s environmentalists would like to have conveyed, but the truth is, God created this world. How egotistical do we have to be to think that we can destroy it? While we are commanded to steward that which God makes available to us, the gloom and doom prophecies that many profess are simply illogical when studied in the light of God’s truth. As it is stated in Ecclesiastes, “One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever.” Who are we to argue with the Word of God?

One of the trademark features of the TV show was that it always ended every episode with a cliffhanger. The writers would place the family in some great danger which would be resolved in the first few minutes of the following week’s show. The movie’s filmmakers follow that tradition. Let’s just hope that the next danger proves to be too much for the Jupiter II. The last thing this world needs is a sequel of this environmental disaster of a movie.

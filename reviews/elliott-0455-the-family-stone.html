Author-verified transcription

Perhaps every family has a degree of dysfunctionality lurking behind closed doors. In The Family Stone, those doors are not just unlocked - they are flung wide open so that we can take a look at the chaotic lives of those inside.

This Christmas it is Meredith (Sarah Jessica Parker), a repressed and priggish New Yorker, who is the outsider coming to stay in the Stone's New England home. She's the girlfriend of eldest son Everett (Dermot Mulroney) and has little in common with loose and high-spirited Stones. The uncomfortable tension that arrives with her is felt by all.

Family matriarch Sybil (Diane Keaton) rules the crazy roost in a liberal, free expression kind of way with husband Kelly (Craig T. Nelson providing the stability and calming influence that keeps the Stones from rolling too far off course.

After Everett, who is a successful businessman in NYC, comes his brother Ben (Luke Wilson), now a laid back Californian film editor. Amy (Rachel McAdams) is the most outspoken member of the family and the one who takes an immediate and thinly veiled dislike to Meredith. Her sister Suzannah (Elisabeth Reaser) is so busy looking after her young child and wondering when her husband will join them that she makes little impression on us.

Rounding out the family is Thad (Ty Giordano), hearing-disabled and homosexual, who arrives with his black life partner (Brian White) and, this being a liberally-minded film, they give all indications of being the most emotionally mature and physchomogically stable members of the family.

Fearful of being Stoned to death, Meredith calls her sister (Claire Danes) to New England to stand by her side. Surprisingly, she arrives the next day.

Writer/Director Thomas Bezucha tries to paint his picture using a broad, multi-colored palette. He just doesn't make the hues rich or vibrant enough. There's not enough character development built into the story to justify some of the actions taken. There's a few amusing scenes and a few others that start to pull at some dramatic chords but over time they get washed out by the transparency of the script.

The Stones do exhibit love and tolerance for one another. Where they fall short is in extending that love and tolerance outside of their closed family circle. Meredith is left feeling isolated, abused and insulted.

Hospitality is a talent that must be practiced. It starts with adopting a mindset of giving. We look for what the needs of others are so we might take action to meet those needs. We predict expected needs and try to meet those before they come up. The goal is to genuinely put guests at ease so they may be refreshed during their stay with us. A stay with the Stones is anything but refreshing.

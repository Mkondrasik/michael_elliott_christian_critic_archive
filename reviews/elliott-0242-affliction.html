Affliction

Author-verified transcription

Affliction: 1) A condition of pain, suffering or distress; 2) A cause of pain, suffering, or distress; 3) A new movie directed by Paul Schrader and starring Nick Nolte featuring pain, suffering, and distress.

The above definition accurately indicates that Affliction is not a particularly easy movie to sit through, even at its under two hour running time.

Nick Nolte (A Thin Red Line) plays Wade Whitehouse, sheriff of a sleepy little New Hampshire town. The movie unfolds from Wade's somewhat skewed and biased perspective. As his brother Rolfe (Willem Dafoe, Speed 2) observes in his narration, "Wade lived on the edge of his emotions..." The problem with living on the edge of anything is that the one so precariously perched is prone to fall off, which Wade does frequently.

Whether it is examining a seemingly innocent hunting accident for possible murder implications, or dealing with his ex-wife and daughter regarding his visitation rights, Wade looks at each situation with the trained eye of an investigator, but what he sees is processed through an irrational mind resulting in a flawed set of conclusions.

The reason for his irrational mind is, according to the filmmakers, the pain, suffering, and distress visited upon him at an early age by his sadistically abusive father.

Writer/Director Paul Schrader (Taxi Driver) is certainly no stranger to psychological dramas, but working from the novel of Russell Banks and centered around the powerhouse performance given by Mr. Nolte, Affliction reaches for a higher level, even if it does not always attain it.

Set in the bleak and oppressively snowy environs of New Hampshire, there's a heaviness about the production, as if the weight of the snow rests directly on the heart.

Mr. Nolte has already received many accolades (including an Academy Award nomination) for his riveting performance as the deeply troubled, emotionally scarred Wade. They are richly deserved. He lays his character bare before us, showing us an up close and personal insight to a mind gone terribly wrong.

James Coburn (Maverick) is chilling as the mentally twisted patriarch, Glen Whitehouse. This clan, under his authoritative hand, would have to make great improvements just to earn the right to be called dysfunctional. There is nothing in Glen's manner that even closely resembles a successful manifestation of the spiritual responsibility that God commands of fathers. Perhaps the most obviously ignored scripture is this:

And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord. Ephesians 6:4 [KJV]

In a godly environment, a father is one who encourages, supports, and guides his children along a proper path. Glen does none of this. But the cruelty and mean-spiritedness of Glen is not a genetic trait passed down through the generations from father to son. Such qualities are products of the will. It is learned behavior. Therefore, a father has the God-given responsibility to teach his children well, giving them a proper example to follow.

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it. Proverbs 22:6 [KJV]

The problem is, the above scripture works both ways: Train a child properly, he will have the developed habit patterns which may carry him through his entire lifetime. Train him improperly, and we get movies like Affliction and characters like Wade Whitehouse. But Wade is not absolved of responsibility. It was available for him to change his manner, forsaking his father's ways.

Be ye not as your fathers, unto whom the former prophets have cried, saying...Turn ye now from your evil ways, and from your evil doings... Zechariah 1:4 [KJV]

"Be ye not" is a command. The responsibility to obey it was Wade's... and if applicable, ours.

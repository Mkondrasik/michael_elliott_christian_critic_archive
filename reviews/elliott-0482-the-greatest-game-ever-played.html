Author-verified transcription

Sports columnist Grantland Rice once wrote, "Golf is twenty percent mechanics and technique. The other eighty percent is philosophy, humor, tragedy, melodrama, companionship, romance, camaraderie, cussedness, and conversation." Not so surprisingly, all of these qualities are on display in The Greatest Game Ever Played, a film based upon the inspirational real life tale of Francis Ouimet.

Ouimet (Shia LaBeouf) was an unlikely American hero. The son of a laborer (Elias Koteas), he grew up with an appreciation of hard work, a passion for the sport of golf, and the moxie to have a vision beyond the social/economic barriers that would have held him back.

In 1913, golf was considered a "gentleman's game" to be enjoyed by the privileged class. The closest a working man (or boy) could hope for was to do as Ouimet did - caddy for the elite at a neighborhood club. But when a young man lives and works within his dreams, opportunity knocks.

When the 18th US Open comes to Brookline Massachusetts, an unusual invitation is issued to a local kid - Ouimet. He is offered the opportunity to test his skill against the best players on the planet, including his long time idol, 6-time British Open champion Harry Vardon (Stephen Dillane).

Ouimet must overcome the class prejudice of the times as well as the open disapproval of his father who feels that his son is setting himself up for disgrace and heartbreak instead of bringing home an honest day's wage. This plus the pressure of competing in a world class tournament - a David against a sea of Goliaths. LaBeouf, best known for his work on the Disney Channel's Even Stevens, turns in a mature performance as Ouimet. Golf is a mental game and LaBeouf communicates the mental discipline that gave Ouimet the power to compete at a championship level.

Stephen Dillane also does fine work as Vardon, the British professional golfer who, though recognized as the best the island nation had to offer, lacked the necessary pedigree to gain membership status to the clubs whose tournaments he kept winning.

Stealing the movie is Ouimet's pint sized caddy played by Josh Flitter. With no other option, Ouimet agreed to let ten-year-old Eddie Lowery carry his bags. Lowery's unbridled enthusiasm and can-do spirit contributed to Ouimet's success and Flitter's performance does the same with this movie. The on screen chemistry between LaBeouf and Flitter takes the film to another level.

Director Bill Paxton tweaks the game of golf a wee bit by inserting plenty of cinematic tricks, making the sport appear as fast paced as the final two minutes of a tied basketball game. In other words, he holds our attention and keeps the dramatic tension of the film taut.

There are no villains in the film. Even Ouimet's father, who came close to shattering his son's dreams, did so because he cared for his son and wanted what he thought was best for him. He mistakenly believed that his son, by reaching for the stars, would fall and be hurt.

What he failed to take into consideration was that by discouraging his son to reach for those stars, he was holding him back and keeping him from realizing the dreams that made him unique.

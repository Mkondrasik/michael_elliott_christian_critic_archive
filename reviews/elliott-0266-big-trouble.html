Big Trouble

1 hr: 25 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Grief; death; hope; God's promises; restoration

Author-verified transcription

Take a pair of fun-loving teenagers; add their divorced or unhappily married parents; mix in some Russian arms dealers, a couple of cops, some New York hit men, a few goats, a hallucinogenic frog, and a mysterious suitcase; take 80% of the characters and reduce their IQ by 100 points; set the whole thing in Miami, FL; and you've got Big Trouble, a zany new comedy from Touchstone Pictures.

Big Trouble was originally scheduled to open last fall but was shelved after Sept. 11th. That decision was certainly understandable. Much of Big Trouble centers around terrorist activity in an airport and on a plane. Even now, though the film never veers from its comic intentions, its unintentional parallels might inadvertently evoke memories and emotions which are anything but humorous.

With fourteen featured characters, it is difficult to summarize the complex story lines. Eliot (Tim Allen) is a divorced man whose son Matt (Ben Foster) is involved in an after school game called "Killer" which has its squirt gun carrying players performing "hits" on other players. Matt's newest assignment is Jenny Herk (Zooey Deschanel).

Meanwhile, Jenny's father Arthur (Stanley Tucci) has been targeted by real hit men (Dennis Farina and Jack Kehler) who show up the same night. The real hit men are foiled by the sudden appearance of the squirt gun squad. All this is witnessed by Puggy (Jason Lee) a homeless Frito-loving wanderer living in a tree behind the Herk's house.

Things really get complicated when Arthur buys a suitcase from a pair of Russian no-goodniks, and then is held up by a pair of American imbeciles who don't really know what it is they just stole. A chase eventually ensues which brings all the characters together towards an implausible yet riotous conclusion.

Director Barry Sonnenfeld (Get Shorty!) has bitten off quite a bit here but manages to keep the film both understandable and fast moving. With a talented ensemble cast, who for the most part play their parts with deadpan expressions thus heightening the absurdity of the events, he manages to capture the irreverent and often hilarious writing style of Dave Barry. This is appropriate as it is Barry's novel which serves as the source material for the film.

There are any number of performances worthy of note. The ensemble cast was selected with intricate care. Working well together, no one star shines any brighter than another. Together, they light up the screen with comedic brilliance... but there is a catch.

There are a number of potentially offensive scenes in the film. Tucci's toe-sucking seduction in particular is one I could have done without. The language is harsh and sexual references are aplenty. But there is yet another, more important issue that merits deep consideration.

The intent of Big Trouble is unquestionably humorous. The degree to which it will prove successful is largely dependent upon the reaction of each individual audience member. Unfortunately, it is impossible to view this film without being reminded of the events of September 11th. Even though this film was completed and edited long before Al-Queda became a household word, we cannot ignore the fact that there are scenes in this film which may trigger painful memories... especially in those who were most personally affected by those events.

Sadly, such is the life we lead. We are subject to our emotions and the remembrances of loss, grief, and pain. This sting of death is still sharply felt. But it will not always be so.

And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. Revelation 21:4 (KJV).

God promises that one day, all will be set right. All injustices will be corrected. All tears will be wiped away. All pain will be forgotten. It is this very real and tangible hope that strengthens our resolve in these terrible days that try our souls.

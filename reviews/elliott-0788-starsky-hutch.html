Author-verified transcription

Starsky & Hutch, another TV show from the 70s, is being remembered by Hollywood although someone's memory must be failing. The only thing faithful to the original series is the familiar red and white Ford Grand Torino. The rest is a weak blend of lame jokes and foolish situations.

David Starsky (Ben Stiller) is a by-the-book, enthusiastic cop whose zeal often gets him in trouble. Ken Hutchinson (Owen Wilson) is a laid back, bend-the-rules kind of cop whose personal self-interests often take priority over his police work. Their captain, wearied of them both, figures they deserve each other so he partners them together.

Neither is happy about the situation but when a dead body washes ashore, they stumble onto the biggest drug deal of the decade. Fortunately for them, the dead body has the killer's business card in his jacket so there's not a lot of police work that has to be done. Unfortunately for us, this leaves the filmmakers with a lot of time to fill. They do so with campy silliness, needless scenes which are supposed to be funny, and a ho-hum cameo appearance by the original TV stars.

We see the two stumble over themselves as they question a buxom cheerleader, act out a convict's sexual fantasies in exchange for information, pretend to be mimes at a children's party, and generally act like buffoons for ninety minutes. In the most gratuitous scene in the film, Starsky accidentally takes cocaine and ends up competing in a ridiculous disco dance-off.

Ben Stiller plays Starsky as an overly earnest, straight-arrow, gung-ho kind of guy. It's the kind of character we are accustomed to seeing him play and this characterization is beginning to wear itself thin. Owen Wilson makes no attempt to play a different kind of character. Laid back, California surfer-dude with a lazy drawl is what he does best and he brings it to every role. Hutch is no different.

Snoop Dogg plays their prime informant Huggy Bear but fails to capture the 70s swagger of Antonio Fargas. Vince Vaughn makes for a decent enough villain but the script lacks any of the layers needed to flesh out characters beyond the stereotypes they were written to be.

Director Todd Phillips (Road Trip, Old School) seems less interested in revisiting the dynamics of the TV show than he is in stringing together a number of cheesy jokes and sight gags. He seems to think that the Grand Torino is the best "character" in the series as it is the only one to whom he pays homage.

Admittedly, there is not much to this film that lends itself to a spiritual exploration of truth. This is due to the lack of detail given to the characters and the story line. But in the dynamic of the partnership between Starsky and Hutch, we can see how working in unison builds strength. Starsky gets himself into trouble because he acts before he thinks. Hutch gets himself into trouble because he doesn't want to act at all.

Together, they become stronger and more effective detectives because their weaknesses are strengthened by the attributes of their partner. Starsky is kept in check by Hutch's hesitancy and Hutch is spurred to action by Starsky's zeal.

Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend. Proverbs 27:17 (KJV)

Each of us have individual weaknesses but as we stand in the company of others, we can be strengthened by their believing actions and encouragement.

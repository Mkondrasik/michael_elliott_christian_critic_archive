Elektra

Author-verified transcription

2004 gave us the worst superhero film ever with Catwoman. Here it is just a few weeks into 2005 and already we have a candidate for the usurper to the throne: Elektra.

Elektra (Jennifer Garner), as movie fans will remember, was a supporting character in last year’s easily forgotten Daredevil. In her self-titled feature film, the gaps to her history are filled. The filmmakers need not have bothered. It’s a convoluted and often incomprehensible tale.

We’re told Elektra was a “motherless child” who once died and was brought back to life by Stick (Terence Stamp), a blind martial arts master who takes her under his wing only to cast her off unexpectedly, forcing her to find a place for herself in a cruel and unforgiving world. Her training has given her the ability to see short glimpses into the future - a skill known as “kimagure.” She’s going to need it.

So armed, she becomes a murderous mercenary with her own agent (Colin Cunningham). But when the next gig (i.e. victim) that he lines up turns out to be a nice looking guy (Goran Visnjic) and his young teenage daughter (Kirsten Prout), Elektra decides to become their protector instead of their assassin. Unfortunately for her, the ones who want to see them dead are pure evil with incredible powers. They soon come after them in force.

The evil ones are uniquely empowered: Kirigi (Will Yun Lee) defies the laws of physics in his speed and movements; Tattoo (Chris Ackerman) has body art that will supernaturally come to life; Typhoid (Natassia Malthe) literally breathes death to anyone in her proximity; Stone (Bob Sapp) is as hard as a rock and as dumb as a brick. I’m not sure what Kinko’s (Edson T. Ribeiro) superpower is… perhaps making lethal copies and overcharging for them?

There’s not much of a roster for the good guys. Besides Elektra, there’s the dad, who is no match for faster than light demons; his daughter, who is still learning the ropes of the superhero game; and Elektra’s agent who conveniently disappears for large sections of the script.

Perhaps the overwhelming imbalance between good and evil is part of the reason why the fight scenes are so ludicrous. Whatever the reason, I’m pretty sure laughter was not the audience response director Rob Bowman was going for.

Jennifer Garner, who was delightful in last summer’s 13 Going on 30, is less appealing here. Terence Stamp gets by on reputation and a blank stare. Everyone else is simply forgettable.

To be given a second chance at life, as Elektra has been given, presents us with an interesting question to contemplate: “What do you do with your life after you are brought back from the dead?” The question which Elektra faced is not a new one. Many people before her wrestled with it.

The scriptures tell the stories of a number of people who died and were brought back to life: Lazarus (John 11:1-44), Dorcas (Acts 9:36-41), the daughter of Jairus (Mark 5:35-43), the son of the woman of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:9-24).

It is interesting to note that while the scriptures do provide information regarding the miracle of bringing people back from the dead, little is mentioned regarding what these individuals did with their renewed life after the miracle. Perhaps the reason for this is because God wants our focus to be on his power that is able to transcend death rather than the individuals who benefit from that power.

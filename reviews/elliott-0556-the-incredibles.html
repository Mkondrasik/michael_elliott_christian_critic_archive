Author-verified transcription

How nice to finally have a film that delivers on its title. The Incredibles is simply that – incredible. Writer/director Brad Bird (of the vastly underrated The Iron Giant) has teamed with Pixar Animation which continues to build upon its amazing string of animated hits (Toy Story, Monsters Inc, Finding Nemo, etc.). The end result is an exciting animated adventure that will delight both children and adults.

Mr. Incredible (Craig T. Nelson) is a world saver – the ultimate good guy whose super powers keep the bad guys at bay. But when the world gets too litigious and begins suing do-gooders for doing good, all super heroes are forced into hiding behind their mild-mannered alter egos.

Entering into the Superhero Relocation Program, Mr. Incredible and his wife, Elastigirl (Holly Hunter) become Bob and Helen Parr, just another suburban couple with three children, the super fast Dash (Spencer Fox), the sometimes invisible Violet (Sarah Vowell), and baby Jack Jack who is too young to have developed special powers. Since hanging up his super suit, life has become humdrum and boring. Mr. Incredible has turned into Mr. Average and he is none too happy about it.

When an opportunity comes to flex his super muscles once more, he jumps at it without knowing that it may be a trap. His subsequent disappearance forces his wife and children to adopt their superhero personas and go to his rescue. But will they succeed after so long denying their super powers?

The Incredibles is a fun, action-packed movie that will appeal to anyone old enough (and young at heart enough) to enjoy a good comic book story. Combining the crucial superhero elements (a world crisis; a dastardly villain; internal conflicts which distract the hero) with the real life concerns that beset a typical family, Bird has crafted a fantasy adventure film that touches us where we live.

Just considering the superpowers of the Parr family gives us an idea of the underlying message of the film. Elastigirl can stretch and bend to impossible lengths representing the millions of multi-tasking moms everywhere. Dash, who can run faster than the speed of light, is no different than any other hyperactive young boy. Violet, the budding self-conscious teen with feelings of insecurity is prone to turn invisible. And Dad (Mr. Incredible) is the very image of strength. Take away the exaggerated superpowers and we’re still left with the core elements of many families today. What the Parrs learn is the same lesson most of us do: that the unity of family love is the greatest superpower of them all.

Also interesting is the villain who opposes the heroes. Syndrome (Jason Lee) is a regular guy with delusions of grandeur. With no superpowers of his own, he sets out to manufacture some and become a superhero in his own right – even if he is the one that has to first create terror and havoc in order to have someone to rescue. So full of hero envy is he that he begins to despise that which he first admired in Mr. Incredible.

In this respect, he’s no different than the worst villain this world has ever known. Satan himself started out as one of God’s angels. But he wasn’t satisfied serving God. He wanted to have the worship for himself.

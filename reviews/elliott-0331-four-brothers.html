Four Brothers

Author-verified transcription

The characters that make up the Four Brothers in the title of John Singleton's new film were once considered lost causes. They were given a second chance at life because of a remarkable woman who refused to give up on them. When she is gunned down in a convenience store robbery, the "brothers" return to their Detroit home to pay their respects and wreak their revenge.

Granted, they still aren't what we'd call model citizens but, as one cop points out, they're choir boys compared to what they would have been if it had not been for the loving intercession of Eva Mercer (Fionnula Flanagan).

Bobby (Mark Wahlberg) is the ill-tempered bad seed of the bunch. He played hockey until he was banned for being too violent for the game. Jack (Garrett Hedlund) is the youngest Mercer brother, a rock star wannabe who was sexually abused at a young age.

Jeremiah (Andre Benjamin) has gone the straightest route, becoming a businessman with plans to redevelop Detroit's most rundown areas. Angel (Tyrese Gibson) is good looking enough to be a model; violent enough to be a hoodlum. He completes the family unit and adds to it, bringing his old girlfriend, the fiery Sofi (Sophia Vergara), into the house.

As the four brothers look into the convenience store robbery that claimed the life of the woman who adopted them they begin to suspect that something sinister lies at the heart of the incident. Their "investigation," which consists of threats, violence, and acts of aggression, points them in the direction of mobster Victor Sweet (Chiwetel Ejiofor). The Mercer boys turn Detroit into a war zone as they take on ruthless killers, crooked cops, and an uncooperative community.

Singleton allows his actors to build a nice rapport. They are four troubled individuals from disparate backgrounds who nevertheless share a common bond. They see themselves as family and though they may bicker or disagree, they never lose that bond that ties them together.

The script, by David Elliot and Paul Lovett, is heavily violent and laced with strong profanity. There are numerous holes in the plot that must be overlooked in order to keep credulity alive. Singleton does what he can to draw our attention away from them.

Most of the characterizations are a bit too one dimensional to hold our interest for long. Of course with all the car chases, gunfights and fistfights that populate the screen, there aren't many scenes that depend upon the actors' abilities to plumb the meager depths of their characters. Chiwetel Ejiofor as Victor Sweet is a good example of one such superficial characterization. A bad guy with a mean streak, Victor relishes in humiliating others and ruling through fear and intimidation. His behavior is consistent, and predictable. It also serves as a lesson for why and how the "golden rule" works.

We've been taught the golden rule - "Treat others as you would like to be treated." It's a principle that is lifted off the pages of the Bible.

And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise." Luke 6:31 (KJV)

Victor held himself above this truth, believing that he had the right to hold people, even the people who worked for him, in less esteem than he held himself. It proved to be a fatal error in judgment and the consequences he faced proves another scripture to be true:

Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap. Galatians 6:7 (KJV)

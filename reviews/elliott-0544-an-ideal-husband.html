Author-verified transcription

What with all the current movies featuring either high-tech, computer-generated special effects or low-brow, potty-mouthed humor, it is a rare and distinct pleasure to see a film that is not only character driven but one that contains old-fashioned values as well.

Set in London of 1895, where there is a practiced propriety to every action, Sir Robert Chiltern, an influential and highly respected member of the English Parliament is being sweetly blackmailed by Mrs. Cheveley who seeks his political support of a crooked but potentially lucrative scheme. Due to an indiscretion (amazingly enough to our 20th century sensibilities, it is non-sexual) performed many years ago, Sir Chiltern must decide to vote his conscience by standing against the proposal or else save his political career and relationship with his wife by siding with Mrs. Cheveley and obtaining her silence as regards to his flawed past.

His best friend, Lord Arthur Goring, may be a bit of a gadfly, but he is a man of honor and a man who values friendship above all. He is drawn into his friend's dilemma, and becomes confidante to many of the parties involved. Bound by his word, he must nimbly navigate through his promises and vows, without breaking a one.

Audiences accustomed to the "urine jokes" of Big Daddy or the profane and vulgar humor of South Park will find an unfamiliar intelligence and wit running through this screenplay written by director Oliver Parker [Othello] which was based on the play by Oscar Wilde. Mr. Parker did a marvelous job in this adaptation, not only managing to translate the verbal sparring nature of the English language but also managing to break out of the confining proscenium boundaries of the stage to utilize the less restricted, more fluid medium of film.

Rupert Everett [A Midsummer Night's Dream] has the showiest role as the world-weary bon vivant, Lord Goring. He also has been given the best bons mots of Mr. Wilde: "To love oneself is the beginning of a lifelong romance." "I love talking about nothing. It's the only thing I know anything about." His is a polished, elegant performance that is simply delightful to watch.

The rest of the cast comport themselves just as well. Jeremy Northam [Emma] is Sir Robert Chiltern, a man who enjoys an impeccable reputation but realizes that much of it is due to his morally sound wife Lady Chiltern [Cate Blanchett, Elizabeth]. Their respective reactions to the threat of a revealed scandal bespeaks the level of importance once given to the honor associated with a man's good name.

Minnie Driver [Tarzan], is Mabel Chiltern, the only female in England seemingly capable of matching wits with Lord Goring. Their amusingly droll exchanges are among the numerous bright spots in the film. Julianne Moore [Cookie's Fortune] plays the devious Mrs. Laura Cheveley with a proper English restraint and sophistication... qualities usually not found in a blackmailer.

At the heart of it all is a societal understanding of the importance of one's integrity. In An Ideal Husband, a man's word was not only his bond, it was his most valuable possession. In today's modern world, it would appear that integrity is fast becoming a rare commodity. A man's word, upon inspection, no longer carries the same weight or significance it once did. One need only look at America's political leaders to recognize there is a great difference between what they say and what they do. We, as a people, have grown accustomed to other people lying to us. So accustomed in fact, that we rather expect it from them.

God holds Himself to a higher standard. It is a standard which He offers to us. It is a standard which has its own rewards in the very doing of it. The Word of God has stood for centuries because its integrity cannot be broken. Its strength can be ours as we commit to live according to the standard recorded in its pages.

Till I die I will not remove mine integrity from me. Job 27:5b (KJV)

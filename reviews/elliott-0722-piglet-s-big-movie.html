Author-verified transcription

Walt Disney and the company he founded has always been very protective of maintaining the integrity of his characters. It has certainly paid off with the newest feature film involving those wonderful friends from the Hundred Acre Wood. Piglet's Big Movie reminds us of the reasons why we love Pooh, Piglet, Tigger, Roo and all the rest of A.A. Milne's stuffed animals who come to animated life courtesy of Walt Disney Pictures.

With an inventive concept, the animators take a series of short stories written by Milne in the 1920's and combine them to form a full length feature film. These stories had not yet been produced by Disney even though they have held the rights to them ever since they bought them back in the early 60s. The film opens with Pooh, Rabbit, Eeyore, and Tigger hatching a big and complicated honey-gathering plan that has no part for someone as small as the sweet but diminutive Piglet. While Piglet wanders off wishing that he could be bigger so his friends would need him, Pooh and the others discover Piglet's memory scrapbook. It helps them to realize just how big an impact Piglet has had upon their lives. Using the memory book to find clues as to his whereabouts, they begin to search the Hundred Acre Wood looking for their little pink friend.

Along the way, they recall three adventures of the past where Piglet played an important part. The first, "In Which Kanga and Baby Roo Come to the Forest, And Piglet has a Bath," introduces the maternal Kanga and her rambunctious child Roo to the fear filled residents of the Hundred Acre Wood. Piglet is the first to conquer his fear and recognize them as friends.

The second, "In Which Christopher Robin Leads an Exposition to the North Pole," shows Piglet as being the bravest and most resourceful of them all. The final story, "In Which a House is Built at Pooh Corner for Eeyore," reminds us of the little guy's compassion and the size of his very big heart.

Of course the story which ties these three adventures together might be called: "In Which Pooh, Tigger, and Rabbit Lose Piglet's memories But Manage to Make New Ones."

Along with the wonderful watercolor art direction by Disney animators, and the familiar voice characterizations, the film offers a number of simple, sweet and melodic new songs by Carly Simon including: "If I Wasn't So Small," "A Mother's Intuition," and the catchy march, "Sing Ho For The Life of a Bear."

The entire film resonates with emotion and serves as a reminder that wholesome family entertainment, when done well, is enjoyable for all ages. It's nice to know that it isn't a forgotten art form. Filled with family values and lessons of friendship and love, Piglet's Big Movie even lacks a villain or a bad guy. Every character is likable with a distinctive, recognizable personality. Each of us may have our favorite but everyone in The Hundred Acre Wood is important and contributes to the whole Pooh family. Everyone, regardless of size.

As it is, there are many parts, but one body. The eye cannot say to the hand, "I don't need you!" And the head cannot say to the feet, "I don't need you!" On the contrary, those parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable. 1 Corinthians 12:20-22 (NIV)

Piglet's Big Movie may appear to be a small, insignificant children's film, but the message it delivers and the way in which it is delivered can have a big impact upon our hearts. Does it have a place amid the fast-paced, computer animated, hi-tech entertainment with which our children are tempted daily? I should very much like to think so. In fact, I'd go as far as to say that I think that it is something that is badly needed.

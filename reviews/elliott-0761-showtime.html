Author-verified transcription

Two cops. One is black... the other is white. One goes by the book... the other goes off the wall. One is old... the other is... well, not so old. Before anyone asks, no, I'm not talking about Lethal Weapon V, though with a few minor casting changes, it very well could have been. Actually, it's a description of Showtime, the newest buddy cop comedy from 20th Century Fox which freely borrows from the genre without adding much of anything new.

Mitch Preston (Robert DeNiro, The Score) is a detective so serious that even his community service presentations scare little kids. He certainly has no time for the shenanigans that go on with putting on a prime time reality TV show based upon his reputation as a no nonsense, short fused cop. And yet, that is exactly what he is ordered to do.

What is worse, he is forced to team up with Trey Sellars (Eddie Murphy, Shrek), a street cop and frustrated actor who manages to be equally bad at both his professions. Riding them to television glory is the show's producer, Chase Rensi (Rene Russo, Big Trouble) who smells ratings success from this odd couple pairing.

The paper-thin plot involves Mitch's tracking of a super artillery-grade automatic cannon/gun that is causing havoc in his city. He aims to stop it and the European money-grubbing terrorist (Die Hard anyone?) who wields it. If that means having to put up with the antics of a self-serving camera-hog in order to be given the assignment, then so be it.

Director Tom Dey (Shanghai Noon) and a trio of screenwriters have a workable premise from which to build but they simply don't take it far enough. Their film is riddled with missed opportunities, missing punch lines, improbable scenarios, and stereotypically written characters. While there are a number of laughs in the film, there should be considerably more.

DeNiro and Murphy never quite attain the same movie chemistry that was evident between Gibson and Glover in the Lethal Weapon series. Their roles are neither challenging nor overly original but they manage to perform them well enough. Rene Russo is pretty much wasted in a role that likewise demands little from her.

In the film's most effective and amusing sequence, William Shatner (Star Trek) is cast as himself, brought in to give Mitch and Trey acting (!) tips on how to be a TV cop a la T.J. Hooker. Sharing the secrets of his TV success, from punctuating a line with one's eyebrow to the ways one should jump on a car hood, Shatner proves to be a good sport, parodying himself and his penchant for over-emoting.

Showtime makes the point that there is very little reality dwelling behind the mask of "reality TV." While that comes as no surprise, it does carry a lesson for us to remember. We should always be careful not to immediately believe everything that is presented to us at its face value. What we see is not always what we will get. This is a truth that Jesus himself addressed when he confronted the scribes and Pharisees:

Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness. Even so ye also outwardly appear righteous unto men, but within ye are full of hypocrisy and iniquity. Matthew 23:27-28 (KJV)

There's only one way to avoid being deceived by false appearances. To adopt a standard for truth against which all things may be judged. God has given us His word to be used for such a purpose. It is faithful. It is true. It is life for all who believe it.

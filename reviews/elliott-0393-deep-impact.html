Deep Impact

Author-verified transcription

For every "original" idea that exists in Hollywood, there is a long line of people ready to copy it. Recently, we've had a rash of similarly-themed movies being released almost simultaneously by different studios.

We've seen two "volcano erupting in the United States" movies (Volcano and Dante's Peak), two "Dalhi Lama leaving Tibet" movies, (Kundun and Seven Days in Tibet), two "Gunfight at the OK Corral" movies (Tombstone and Wyatt Earp), and coming soon, two "animated picnic pest" movies (Antz and A Bug's Life).

Now, we have an opportunity to see Deep Impact, the first of two "asteroid destroying the earth" movies (Armageddon is due to be released later this year). To be fair, Deep Impact is not about an asteroid. It's actually a "comet destroying the earth" movie. Who said originality is dead?

Mimi Leder (The Peacemaker) directs this film which, given its subject matter, surprisingly fails to capture our interest. You'd think the end of the world would somehow involve us just a little bit. But no, we just never seem to connect with the events unfolding before us.

Part of the problem is that we never connect with the characters to whom the events occur. Tea Leoni (Flirting With Disaster) plays Jenny Lerner, a TV reporter who bluffs her way into covering the most important story of a lifetime. The attractive Miss Leoni never finds the key that makes her character work. As she struggles to disguise Jenny's own insecurity with a news professional's bravado, she manages to weaken the character to the point that makes her sudden career rise implausible.

The performance of Morgan Freeman (Kiss The Girls), as President Beck, is almost enough to give politicians a good name. It is too bad he's an actor. The United States could use someone as compassionate, thoughtful and genuine as this movie's president.

Robert Duvall (The Apostle) is Spurgeon "Fish" Tanner, an experienced space jockey accompanying a ship full of highly trained but inexperienced astronauts on a mission to deflect the comet. He makes a point of telling his simulator-trained crew mates that flying in space is a lot different than playing with some expensive NASA video game. That might have been an effective argument if it wasn't for the fact that when Fish finally gets around to flying the spacecraft, it looks exactly like one of those video games he so easily dismissed.

The special effects, when all is said and done, were just not that special. I'm sure they had some technical merit, but from an audience perspective, they didn't have the punch of, say, an Independence Day.

The most interesting aspect of the film is the question it poses: "If you knew that the end of the world was coming, how would you spend your remaining time?" There's a question that's got some teeth in it. It's disappointing that after raising the question, the movie provides few insights or answers. Jesus Christ and his disciples certainly had the answers when the question arose in their day.

Jesus Christ understood (and he taught his followers) that no man knows when the final days shall be. It could be tomorrow, it could be a thousand years from now. The when is not the important issue. Neither is the how important. The key question has always been "what?" What will you be doing when Jesus Christ returns? What have you based your life upon? If your understanding of life is limited to your five senses, then the impending destruction of the earth will render that life meaningless. Your remaining hours would be ones of confusion and despair - a life without hope. But if we recognize, as Jesus Christ and his apostles did, that life is spiritual (and therefore eternal), our final days on this earth will be spent as all other days before them (as well as those that will come after them). By loving God, and keeping His commandments.

Deep Impact may have set the question before us but the filmmakers squander an opportunity to examine the implications of that question. Not only do they fail to make a deep impact, they barely make a dent. This is a topic where just scratching the surface does not satisfy.

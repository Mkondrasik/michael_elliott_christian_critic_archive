Author-verified transcription

Most of us are able to think back and remember someone who influenced our lives. Most of us can point to a single individual who helped us develop into the kind of people we were to become. And, for most of us, the individual we remember would be a teacher we had as we were growing up. Freedom Writers is about such a teacher.

Freedom Writers is an inspirational and emotionally charged movie based on the life and experiences of Erin Gruwell (Hilary Swank) and her classes at L.A.'s Woodrow Wilson High School.

Novice and idealistic, Gruwell decides to take action following the L.A. riots of the 1990s. Figuring the best way to stem racial intolerance is through influencing young minds, she takes a job teaching the kids that everyone else is willing to let fall through the system's many cracks. The question remains: will the students let her teach them?

The kids recognize what Gruwell doesn't at first understand. They are embroiled in a daily war that has few survivors and offers very little hope to those on the battlefield. The world these kids live in has been shaped by ethnic hate and their experience has taught them to expect nothing else. Gruwell gets them to look at the world that lies beyond their own experiences.

Swank is wonderful as Gruwell although she plays the early naiveté at an incredulous level. When she errs by asking who in her class belongs to a gang (a violation of trust), she admits her mistake with a sheepish “My badness.” As she grows to know her students, her resolve to not let them fail also grows and we see the backbone behind the smile.

What lifts the film above its formulaic structure is the genuine emotion that director Richard LaGravenese pulls from his cast. Along with Swank is Patrick Dempsey as Gruwell's neglected husband, Scott Glen as her once idealistic father, and Imelda Staunton as her disapproving department head. But the heart of the film comes from the kids, most of whom were unknown and inexperienced at the start of the filming. LaGravenese looked for type and found depth. The relationship formed between Gruwell and her charges is perfectly captured by Swank and company and gives the movie an undeniable emotional resonance.

The connection Gruwell eventually made changed the lives of those students. What she had to do was find a way to reach them. What they had to do was find a way to trust her. By first accepting them for who they were, Gruwell was then able to set a vision for them of who they could become.

By giving them a journal to record their thoughts, and giving them control as to its contents (she would only read them with their permission), she validated their experiences without judging them and empowered their hopes and dreams which they dared not before imagine.

It's what a good teacher does. Caring more about where a student is heading than where the student has been is important in building the trust relationship that is conducive to growth. No one learns by constantly looking backward at what they already know. It takes a teacher to point us in the right direction – into the unknown – and lead us through it.

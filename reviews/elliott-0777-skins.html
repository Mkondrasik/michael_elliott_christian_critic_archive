Author-verified transcription

In 1998, filmmaker Chris Eyre introduced the first film about native Americans made by native Americans. Smoke Signals was a simple and often amusing tale that presented this culture in a never before seen light. For his sophomore effort Eyre takes on a much more sobering subject.

In Skins, based on the novel by Adrian C. Louis, Rudy Yellow Lodge (Eric Schweig) is a cop for the Pine Ridge Police Department. His beat includes the Ogala Sioux Indian Reservation where unemployment is roughly at 75% and death by alcohol is nine times the national average.

Rudy sees the effects of these statistics every day. His older brother Mokie (Graham Greene), a decorated Vietnam vet, is now nothing more than a practicing, barely functional alcoholic. As a cop, Rudy spends much of his time breaking up brawls on the "rez", arresting the drunk and disorderly Indians only to know that as soon as they are released they will fall back into the same pattern of destructive behavior brought on by alcohol abuse.

Frustrated by the helplessness he feels, he turns to vigilantism, anonymously righting wrongs he sees committed against Indians, either with a baseball bat or a gallon of gasoline and a match. His actions not only change nothing, they bring about unexpected consequences with which he will have to come to terms.

Eric Schweig plays the conflicted Rudy with believable intensity and Graham Greene gives Mokie a humanity which is always evident, even through the alcoholic haze in which he lives.

Some of the secondary roles are filled by actors of varying degrees of skill and experience and because of this some of the interplay between the characters are something less than polished.

Skins is not a judgmental or preachy film about the dangers of alcohol abuse although such a lesson will be clearly seen by anyone watching. Eyre's focus is one that is more personal. Mokie may be a drunk and will often bring embarrassment to his family, but this family's love is a bond that cannot be broken and Rudy will not abandon or ignore his brother.

Alcoholism is a serious matter, especially at the levels that the statistics from Pine Ridge suggest. By approaching this sensitive subject with a considerable amount of honesty and understanding, perhaps Eyre has made a film which will drive the "drunken Indian" stereotype out of people's heads so they can examine the nature of the real problem and make suggestions on how to deal with it.

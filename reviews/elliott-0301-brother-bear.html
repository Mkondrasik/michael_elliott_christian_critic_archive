Brother Bear

Author-verified transcription

Watching a Walt Disney animated film has often been something of an anthropomorphic experience. Animals are given human characteristics and expressions in order to tell the story. In Brother Bear, a human is transformed into an animal in order to learn how to become a man.

Set in the Pacific Northwest around 10,000 years ago, Brother Bear features Kenai, a young man awaiting the spirit world to assign him his totem - the symbol of the guiding principle of his life. He's hoping for something that will impress his brothers who have already received theirs - Sitka has the eagle representing guidance and Denahi has the wolf representing wisdom. When it is revealed that his totem will be the bear, a symbol for love, Kenai is sorely disappointed over what he immaturely views as an unmanly characteristic.

When Kenai impulsively tracks down and kills a bear, the spirit world is upset and transforms him into the very creature he despises. As he travels in search of contacting the spirit world to be transformed back, he meets Koda, a small bear cub who has been separated from his mother. They set off together, Koda in search of his mom and Kenai in search of himself.

The animation, which is done in the traditional hand-drawn manner, is at times spectacular. Done especially well are the backgrounds - mountains, glaciers, Northern lights. All very majestic and colorful.

The characters are also well defined and generate sufficient warmth and emotion. There's a nice camaraderie that is displayed first between Kenai and his human brothers and then between the transformed Kenai and his adopted brother bear. Joaquin Phoenix provides the voice for Kenai but the most recognizable voices belong to Rick Moranis and Dave Thomas who reprise their MacKenzie brothers characters from SCTV... this time as a pair of wisecracking Canadian moose. They are responsible for most of the laughs the film generates.

Where I found Brother Bear lacking was in the politically correct message woven into the story itself. The film sought to depict mankind and the animal kingdom as equals, sharing the planet as "brothers." Animal rights activists may applaud the sentiment but that message is not scripturally sound.

God does use animals as examples for our learning. In His Word, He often will refer to a man or a spiritual being as a type of animal in order to emphasize a particular quality or characteristic. For instance:

And he laid hold on the dragon, that old serpent, which is the Devil, and Satan, and bound him a thousand years. Revelation 20:2 (KJV)

This is a figure of speech, employed to convey certain qualities being manifested by the one compared to the animal. The serpent is a creature known for its craftiness as well as for its stealth, qualities certainly shared by the devil.

If Disney had built its film around the totem symbol representation, using the animal kingdom as examples for human learning and growth, that would be beneficial and edifying as well as entertaining. But to present the animal kingdom as somehow being on equal footing with man, spiritually united into one big brotherhood of all living creatures, well. That's just wrong thinking. It violates every truth recorded in the book of Genesis.

And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. Genesis 1:26 (KJV)

God says what He means and means what He says. Dominion does not mean equal partners.

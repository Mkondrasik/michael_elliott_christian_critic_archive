Cradle Will Rock

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 21, 2000
Rating
2 stars

2 hr: 12 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Boldness; courage of convictions; speaking the truth

Author-verified transcription

Art and politics do make strange bedfellows and yet they are not strangers to each other. In the depression era of the 1930's, the American government, thanks to FDR and WPA's Federal Theater Project, became the employer of thousands of out-of-work actors, painters, technicians, and musicians. But even as they gave them money to produce works of great art, they later tried to censor or stifle their freedom of expression when the works exhibited uncomfortable levels of social consciousness or political insurrection.

Such historical material should have resulted in a fascinating and involving tale of courage and integrity as artists literally had to decide whether their art was worth risking their careers, their livelihood, and their freedom. Unfortunately, writer/director Tim Robbins' "mostly true" screenplay was written with such a slanted ideological pen that it smacks of propaganda rather than historical drama.

He has also loaded his screenplay with so many characters and overlapping story lines that our attention is divided and we fail to be emotionally connected to the events taking place on screen.

At the center of the turmoil is an infamous musical production called The Cradle Will Rock being staged by Orson Welles (Angus MacFadyen, Still Breathing) and his Federal Theater group. Written by Marc Blitzstein (Hank Azaria, Godzilla), it is defiantly pro-union and mirrored the labor unrest which was developing in the country at the time.

His production lost its federal support with the US government going so far as to send troops to block the theater's doors, thus preventing the play from being performed.

Meanwhile, Nelson Rockefeller (John Cusak, Being John Malkovich) hired Mexican painter Diego Rivera (Ruben Blades, The Devil's Own) to paint an inspirational mural for the lobby of the newly constructed Rockefeller Plaza. When Rockefeller finds fault with the implied politically oriented messages being painted into the mural, a similar conflict arises addressing the rights of the artist vs. the rights of the arts patron.

Another subplot involves Hazel Huffman (Joan Cusak, Arlington Road), a lowly clerk for the WPA, who teams up with a lonely ventriloquist (Bill Murray, Rushmore) to expose what they view as a communist infiltration in the government sponsored programs. Her testimony before a congressional committee leads to a 20% reduction of funds for the WPA and the closing of many productions, including The Cradle Will Rock, throwing thousands out of work.

For Orson Welles' acting company, this leads to a decision of personal conviction. Do they ignore their own union leaders? Do they risk breaking the law by performing the play anyway? The decision was not an easy one. especially for Olive Stanton (Emily Watson, Angela's Ashes) who was literally living on the streets before being hired by the WPA. Or for Aldo Silvano (John Turturro, Rounders) who had a family to support and desperately needed the income the WPA had provided?

For such an esteemed cast, the acting is a bit uneven. Standouts include Ms. Watson, Mr. Turturro, Mr. Murray, and Cherry Jones (The Horse Whisperer) as Hallie Flannigan, head of the WPA Theater. Less effective is Mr. MacFadyen who chews more scenery as Orson Welles than Mr. Welles did in his entire career. In fact, most of the characters of the Federal Theater are portrayed as loud and obnoxious louts who, being full of passion and unrestrained emotion, are so out of control as to be inexcusably boorish.

Putting the political content aside, there is a biblical principle that can be seen at the heart of the film. Like the strongly opinionated characters (found on either side) in the movie, Christians must also learn to develop the courage of our convictions and the boldness to stand upon the truth of our Lord even at the risk of ridicule or persecution. We are His voice... His ambassadors. His message is heard as long as we continue to speak

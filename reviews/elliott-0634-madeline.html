Author-verified transcription

Madeline is a breath of fresh air from an industry which all too often forgets how to refresh its audiences.

Based on the classic children's books by Ludwig Bemelmans, Madeline tells the story of an irrepressible young redhead growing up in a school for girls. Shot on location in Paris, this movie will make Madeline fans out of even the hardest of hearts.

Director Daisy von Scherler Mayer (Party Girl) remains true to Mr. Bemelman's original tales. Madeline has no "magic" powers. She does not have wisdom beyond her tender age. She's simply the most charming, precious and delightful nine year old that we've seen on screen in quite some time. Londoner Hatty Jones is perfectly cast in the title role. She'll bring a smile to your face before you can say "Bonjour."

Frances McDormand (Fargo) has the central adult role as Miss Clavel, the nun in charge of the school. When Lady Covington, the school's benefactor, dies, Lord Covington (Nigel Hawthorne, Amistad) decides to sell the school to foreign diplomats. What is a red-headed girl to do? In between dealing with the Spanish ambassador's obnoxious young son who lives next door, hiding the stray dog which has adopted her, and battling appendicitis, she also now has to try and save the only home she's ever known from being sold by "Lord Cucuface."

Madeline is a “family film” in the truest sense of the word. And it's a good one to boot. Sit the children down, read them the original 1939 Madeline story and then watch the film together. They will be enthralled.

The movie begins with a visual narrative of the book's opening lines, "In an old house in Paris, that was covered with vines, lived twelve little girls in two straight lines. The smallest one was Madeline." To the biblically minded person, those two straight lines represent discipline, order, and obedience. We can see that, wonder of wonders, living with discipline and order does not emotionally scar children. If anything, it causes them to thrive. Children will discover ways to have fun in even the most dire of circumstances. Giving them boundaries within which they may function allows them the sense of security, love and nurturing they need to grow.

Children cannot raise themselves. And a parent would be insane to let the world (which, after all, is the devil's domain) raise them. It is each parent's responsibility to not only set the boundaries for their child, but to oversee that the child knows how to stay within those boundaries.

If they do not learn at a young age how to obey their parents whom they can see and hear, how will they ever learn to obey their spiritual Father Whom they cannot see? As the scriptures say:

"Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it." Proverbs 22:6

Madeline helps to teach us that lesson again in quite an enjoyable and entertaining way. Judging from the behavior of many of our young people today, it is a lesson which bears oft repeating.

Author-verified transcription

Nothing kills a cinematic controversy more than a film that just doesn't quite work. The Golden Compass, the film version of Philip Pullman's entry to his "Dark Materials" trilogy, was hardly worth the energy that the vocal opposition expended.

Pullman's tale involves a young girl, Lyra Belacqua (Dakota Blue Richards), who lives in a parallel universe where a person's soul exists in animal form outside of the host's body. When Lyra's only relative (Daniel Craig) finds evidence of the existence of "dust," a magical substance that allows passage between universes, he sets off on a journey of discovery.

But this movie is about Lyra and her unique role in the drama that follows. It seems an authoritative organization known as the Magisterium wants to suppress the discovery of dust and has assigned Mrs. Coulter (Nicole Kidman) to stop him. Lyra, who is simply trying to rescue her childhood friends who have been abducted by the "Gobblers," enlists the aid of an ice bear named Iorek Byrnison (voiced by Ian McKellen), an aeronaut named Lee Scoresby (Sam Elliott), and a Gyptian king named John Faa (Jim Carter).

Unfortunately (or perhaps fortunately), the elements which may have made Pullman's story work in his novel are not evident in the film. The movie simply rambles on. not really making much of an impact in any area.

Newcomer Dakota Blue Richards is appealing but can't carry the entire project upon her young shoulders. This is exactly what director Chris Weitz has done and apparently expected. All other characters take what might graciously be termed as cameo roles in this CGI- heavy adventure.

Kidman is fine as the porcelain faced heavy and Elliott adds some western twang to an otherwise proper-English-laced dialogue. But their performances seem laid on top of an existing layer rather then infused to become part of the whole.

There are segments that show promise but are dimmed by the long stretches in between that challenge even the most attentive viewer. All in all, the Christian community need not be concerned about the potential effects of Pullman's atheistic beliefs. For this first installment, those elements have been so watered down as to be negligible and easily addressed. In fact, the film can serve to open up a biblically based discussion regarding the differences between soul and spirit.

In Pullman's world, a person's soul exists outside of him and is housed in an animal form. The reality is that God created man to have both body (the physical form) and soul (the "breath life" which allows the body to function). Body and soul co-exist. A body without soul has no life. A soul cannot exist without a body to give it form.

There is a third and highly important component which is not addressed in The Golden Compass. God also created man to house His spirit. A complete man consists of body, soul, and spirit. All of which is contained in the God- given frame each of us received at birth.

And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. 1 Thessalonians 5:23 (KJV)

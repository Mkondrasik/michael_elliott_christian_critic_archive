Author-verified transcription

"I'd rather be lucky than good" is a phrase blithely bandied about in Woody Allen's latest film, Match Point. While it deals with such matters as marriage, temptation, and infidelity, woven throughout every plot strand is the underlying concept of luck and the part it supposedly plays in our lives.

Chris Wilton (Jonathan Rhys-Myers) came from humble beginnings to become a pro tennis player. Had the ball bounced differently a few times, he might have even beaten a few top seeds during his career. He didn't and has now given up the tour to settle for a new job as a teaching pro at an exclusive London club.

There he meets the affable Tom Hewitt (Matthew Goode), a man born to good looks and family money. Since they both share a love of opera, Chris is invited to join Tom's family at the theater. He and Tom's sister Chloe (Emily Mortimer) hit it off and it soon looks like Chris is being groomed for an upper class life.

But then he meets Tom's fiancée, the alluring Nola (Scarlett Johansson), a struggling actress from America. He is first fascinated by her and then obsessed with her. He pursues her into an adulterous relationship that threatens to destroy all that he has been "fortuitously" given.

Writer/director Woody Allen is revisiting familiar themes here, most notably examined in Crimes and Misdemeanors, and at first seems to be doing so poorly. The film drags a bit in its first act, introducing us to a central character that is almost unbearably bland. But as the story marches forward the film morphs into a kind of suspense thriller that successfully hooks us into wanting to see how it all ends.

Jonathan Rhys-Myers plays Chris as a character easy to dislike. Weak-willed, spineless, and thoroughly selfish, he has no direction or plan for his life. He falls into a union that benefits him financially and then does his best to muck it up.

The supporting characters don't do much to compensate for the despicable character of the lead. Scarlett Johannson's character is a desperate, rather pathetic woman who has foolishly allowed herself to hang onto a fantasy (or two) while pushing the harsh reality of her life far from her mind. Emily Mortimer plays an emotionally needy woman all too willing to keep a blind eye to her husband's obvious ennui. Only Matthew Goode as a "to-the-manor-born" playboy is able to jump start the film with a touch of energy that makes the first third of the movie at all tolerable.

At the heart of Allen's film is the supposition that luck is of vital importance in determining the course of our lives. His premise is that at any major crossroads we might face, how we emerge and continue is as much due to good or bad luck as it is due to any conscious decision we might make.

His film certainly supports his premise but the fallacy isn't hard to spot. Believing in luck is an easy alternative for those who choose not to believe in the universal laws God built into His creation. Life not turning out the way we want? "Darn our bad luck." Someone else got the promotion we wanted? We gripe, "Why do some people have all the luck?"

God designed life with cause and effect consequences. We follow His standards and guidelines, we will reap the rewards He promises. When we venture away from His truths, we reap the consequences. It isn't luck that brings us joy or misery - it is how we approach and function in the life that God designed for us.

The lot is cast into the lap, but the decision is wholly of the Lord [even the events that seem accidental are really ordered by Him]. Proverbs 16:33 (Amplified)

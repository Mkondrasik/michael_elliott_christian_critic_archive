Barbershop

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 28, 2002
Rating
3 stars
Running time
1 hr: 42 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: True riches; people over possessions; community; love and respect

Author-verified transcription

It's all about the attitude. An ensemble cast brings its street black 'tude to the Barbershop, a place where the black man can be himself and offer his opinion on any subject under the sun. It's Mayberry RFD in the 'hood.... 2002 style.

Filling in for Floyd the barber is Calvin (Ice Cube, Three Kings), a dreamer who inherited the shop from his father who had inherited it from his father 40 years earlier. It's been two years since Calvin took over the management of the shop and already he's thinking of a bigger and better life for himself and his pregnant wife.

With debts mounting and pipe dreams fading, Calvin decides to sell the shop to the neighborhood loan shark (Keith David, Novocaine). It is a decision that he regrets almost as soon as he makes it.

What he belatedly comes to realize is that his barbershop is more than just a struggling business in the inner city of Chicago. It's a refuge. A safe haven. The barbershop has become an oasis of hope and promise set in the midst of the sea called urban blight. Not only for the patrons, but primarily for many of the young men and women who have chosen to work there.

They include Jimmy (Sean Patrick Thomas, Save The Last Dance), an erudite black who is working his way through college; Ricky (Michael Ealy, Bad Company), a two-time felon trying to avoid his "third strike"; Dinka (Leonard Howze, The Antwone Fisher Story), a smiling, overweight Nigerian who is in love with Terri (Eve, XXX), the shop's only female barber; Isaac (Troy Garity), the token white guy; and Eddie (Cedric the Entertainer, Serving Sara), an old school barber who provides much of the humor with his outspoken and outrageous opinions on the topics of the day.

From time to time we cut away from the antics in the barbershop to follow a pair of bumbling crooks (Anthony Anderson, Exit Wounds and Lahmard J. Tate, Breakfast of Champions) as they try to break into an ATM machine they stole from a neighborhood convenience store the night before.

First time director Tim Story impresses with his ability to coordinate such a large and talented cast while keeping the movie on track. He's helped immeasurably by an intelligent, culture-savvy script (story by Mark Brown, Two Can Play That Game) that covers such areas as reparations and Rosa Parks' contribution to history from perspectives that may surprise many.

This is no silly, sex-minded comedy to be seen, experienced, and immediately forgotten. It is laugh-out-loud funny to be sure, but with the jokes comes something to stimulate both heart and mind. It's the biggest pleasant surprise I've come across in the theater this year.

Calvin's dreams of prosperity for himself and his family have him chasing the wrong kind of treasures. It's up to Eddie to remind him that while his father may not have had much money, he was still rich because he invested in people. That kind of investment will pay dividends that cannot be recorded in a ledger book.

They that trust in their wealth, and boast themselves in the multitude of their riches; None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him. Psalms 49:6-7 (KJV)

The ills that exist in our society will not be solved by throwing money at them. They can be solved by demonstrating love, respect, and a unity of spirit. Barbershop may sugarcoat some of the harsher realities of life but its heart is in the right place.

Author-verified transcription

We see so many "heroes" depicted in the movies that when a real one comes along, the accolades usually used to describe them seem insufficient. Hotel Rwanda tells the story of Paul Rusesabagina (Don Cheadle), the manager of a five-star hotel in Rwanda, who finds himself in the middle of an unimaginable horror.

It happened in 1994 when the Hutus were given governmental control over Rwanda. Striking with an unmerciful force they went about exterminating what they considered to be the "cockroaches" of their country - those of the Tutsi tribe.

Over a million Tutsis were slaughtered in a little more than three months while the rest of the world turned a blind eye to the modern day genocide taking place. UN peacekeepers impotently stood by and watched, unwilling to intervene with more aggressive action.

It was in the midst of these horrific circumstances that one man found the courage to act. Rusesabagina (himself a Hutu who was married to a Tutsi) turned his hotel into a place of refuge for over 1,000 Tutsis. Using his bargaining/bartering skills and the hotel's resources he managed to keep those in his care alive. As those resources were depleted, his situation became more and more uncertain.

Don Cheadle does an extraordinary job portraying this ordinary man caught in a maelstrom of hate and violence. There are no larger-than-life heroics going on here... just a good man willing to put himself on the line to prevent evil from having free rein. Sophie Okonedo also does some very good work as Paul's distraught wife.

The only other recognizable actors in the cast are less successful. An out-of-place Nick Nolte plays a sympathetic UN colonel hampered by the politically motivated inaction of his organization and Joaquin Phoenix appears briefly as a member of the media sent to document the events for an uncaring world.

"If people see this footage, they'll say, 'Oh my God, that's horrible,' and then go on eating their dinners," one character prophetically observes. It is difficult to imagine that to be the case but, as we now know, the world's nations were shamed by their lack of response to the cries for help coming from Rwanda.

It becomes painfully obvious to us and Paul that he is on his own and his survival, as well as the survival of all who have turned to him for deliverance, rests in his own hands.

There's a moment in the film where Paul is given the opportunity to take his family out of the killing zone. To do so would mean leaving the thousand plus refugees behind to face the oncoming slaughter without him. The decision he makes is the greatest indication of the measure of this man.

The Bible shows us that salvation for a people can often come down to the courage of a single man. Moses had the courage of his convictions to oppose the Pharaoh and lead millions out of bondage and slavery. Daniel, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego believed to stand against King Nebuchadnezzar and prevailed, giving their people some respite during the Babylonian captivity. Joseph, through his believing actions, saved not only his family but all of Egypt from the seven years of famine that was to come upon them.

Throughout history there have been men willing to "stand in the gap" and, by believing, ward off the evil that was poised to destroy the inhabitants of their land. Paul Ruseabagina was one such man. We should be grateful to writer/director Terry George for telling his story so well.

The people of the land have used oppression, and exercised robbery, and have vexed the poor and needy: yea, they have oppressed the stranger wrongfully. And I sought for a man among them that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land. Ezekiel 22:29-30a (KJV)

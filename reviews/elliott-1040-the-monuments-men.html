Author-verified transcription

A little known chapter of history has been revealed, first with the publication of Robert M. Edsel’s fascinating book, The Monuments Men, and then by the release of the movie by the same name. As WWII draws to an end, a small group of art historians are commissioned by the American government to protect Europe’s greatest art treasures and try to track down those items already stolen by Nazi troops.

Those items, it is learned, amount to millions of pieces of unfathomable value – both monetary and cultural. A small core of men led by Frank Stokes (George Clooney) choose to accept the risks associated with war in order to preserve the art they have spent their lives studying and appreciating. It is a daunting task.

The film is not as successful as the story upon which it is based. Most of the criticism levied against the movie has been directed at its tone and structure. While the film’s episodic structure does make for some clumsy moments, the film’s musical score by Alexandre Desplat is the real villain of the movie. It is so poorly conceived and executed that the tone it sets distracts from and weakens whatever else is occurring on screen.

And the “whatever else” is actually pretty good. The cast uniformly does good work, particularly Bill Murray and Bob Balaban as opposite personalities paired together to do important work; Matt Damon and Cate Blanchett work their scenes like the seasoned pros that they are; and George Clooney headlines this ensemble cast with charm and confidence.

Is art worth dying for? It is a question repeatedly asked of the characters that are putting their lives on the line for this very reason. To answer it, we must identify what exactly art does and why it is important to us.

Clooney’s character has a long speech relating art to culture and man’s accomplishments and he makes the point that to destroy art is to eradicate the memory of those who created it. What goes unspoken is why much of the art was created in the first place.

Art communicates on a level that language often cannot. It evokes emotions, inspires thought, and often transcends understanding. This is why since the earliest days, art was used to communicate spiritual concepts to man. A great deal of the art created during the Renaissance was commissioned either by the church or for the church. Now considered artistic masterpieces, they were, at the time of their creation, seen as devotional objects displayed by churches to help bring the masses closer to God.

God will use every means to communicate His heart to those who want to know it. He speaks to us by revelation; He provided His Word in a written form; He arranged the stars to tell a story of redemption; He sent His son to teach us; and He uses the world around us to stir our spirit and remind us of His greater truths. Art is certainly an avenue He does not ignore.

“See, I have called by name Bezalel the son of Uri, son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah, and I have filled him with the Spirit of God, with ability and intelligence, with knowledge and all craftsmanship, to devise artistic designs, to work in gold, silver, and bronze, in cutting stones for setting, and in carving wood, to work in every craft.” Exodus 31:2-5 (NIV)

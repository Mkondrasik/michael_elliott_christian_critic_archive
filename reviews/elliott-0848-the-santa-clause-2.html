The Santa Clause 2

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 1, 2002
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 40 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Acts 20:35Philippians 4:172 Corinthians 9:7

Sermon / spiritual topic: Giving; Generosity; Christmas

Author-verified transcription

Commercially, Christmas seems to arrive earlier and earlier every year. Therefore, it should come as no big surprise to find the first Christmas film of 2002 hitting our theaters the day after Halloween. Better watch out.. The Santa Clause 2 is coming to our towns.

The film is a sequel, arriving some eight years after the original movie became a hit at our cineplexes. In it, Scott Calvin (Tim Allen, Big Trouble), the current and most popular Santa in history, discovers another "clause" in his Santa contract. The "Mrs." clause insists that the divorced Scott remarry or else he will cease to be Santa. In fact, the "de-Santafication" has already begun.. he's losing pounds and his beard is shrinking.

To add to his troubles, his son Charlie (Eric Lloyd, Deconstructing Harry) has managed to show up on this year's "naughty" list. Scott decides that he must return home to suburbia to straighten out his son and, simultaneously, find himself a bride. Leaving a plastic replica of himself in charge (big mistake), Scott leaves the North Pole and thus puts the entire future of Christmas at risk.

Tim Allen reprises his role as the jolly old elf with a twinkle in his eye. His performance is one of the bright moments in a film that is mostly average, though innocuous fare. He also plays the plastic Santa who has some strange ideas on how to handle the naughty and nice list.

Elizabeth Mitchell (Frequency) plays Scott's intended bride and Charlie's high school principal who is asked once again to believe in Santa. David Krumholtz (The Mexican) returns as Bernard, Santa's number one elf. Second in command is the 900-year old Curtis amusingly played by 10 year old Spencer Breslin (The Kid).

The set design, notably the colorful elf village and Santa's workshop, are certainly pleasing to the eye and director Michael Lembeck, making his first feature film, captures them nicely. Not as effective are the scores of children who serve as Santa's elves. Varying degrees of thespian talent make for an uneven production. The animatronic reindeer, however, are crowd pleasers (with the possible exception of one methane-related scene which was both gratuitous and unfunny).

Where the film goes astray is in its implied message. It would appear that to the large posse of credited screenwriters, Christmas is all about the gifts. Receiving them.. not necessarily giving them. In the film, a teachers' holiday party is going poorly until Scott shows up and does his Santa thing - handing out vintage toys that have the adults squealing with glee. Later, the statement is made that Christmas is going to be "ruined" all because the plastic Santa plans to eliminate toys and give all the boys and girls lumps of coal.

The film implicitly gives a selfish message directed to the "me" generation. It may be wrapped in tinsel and glittering lights but when we open this package, it's empty inside.

Christmas (indeed, every day) should be about the joy and pleasure of giving... not about what we'll get. It's an old lesson but obviously it is one which bears repeating.

I have showed you all things, how that so labouring ye ought to support the weak, and to remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he said, It is more blessed to give than to receive. Acts 20:35 (KJV)

Besides, anyone who practices the art of giving (cheerfully and from the heart) knows that the giver always receives the best part of the transaction.

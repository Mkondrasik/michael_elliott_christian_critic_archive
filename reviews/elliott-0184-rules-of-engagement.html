Rules of Engagement

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 7, 2000
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: False witnesses; spiritual warfare; service; loyalty; God's faithfulness

Author-verified transcription

Rules of Engagement takes a look at military justice, although it is a look through a slightly jaundiced eye. The politicians of the United States' government, to whom the military reports, are portrayed as being either weak and cowardly or as evil and manipulating. Either way they would rather allow an innocent man to be ruined than to upset the so-called balance of Middle Eastern peace or weaken the tenuous US presence there.

The innocent man in question is one Col. Terry Childers, USMC (Samuel L. Jackson, Deep Blue Sea), a much decorated war veteran whose last mission involved traveling to Yemen to secure the safety of the ambassador (Ben Kingsley, What Planet Are You From?) and other Americans trapped inside an embassy under siege.

After arranging for an evacuation and under heavy fire resulting in the loss of life, Col. Childers gives the order to return fire which results in the death of 83 Yemen citizens, including many women and children. An outraged National Security Advisor, incensed over the public relations nightmare which Childers' actions have brought, pushes for an immediate court martial.

Childers turns to his long time buddy, Hays Hodges (Tommy Lee Jones, Double Jeopardy) for legal assistance. Hodges served with Childers in Vietnam and owes his life to him. The fact that he is, self-admittedly, a weak lawyer doesn't dissuade Childers. All he knows is that Hodges has been under fire and has seen him in the same position. Hodges, saddled with a debt no one can really repay, takes the case, being given next to no preparation time, and feeling completely out of his depth.

As directed by William Friedkin (The French Connection), Rules of Engagement only partially succeeds in engaging us. The battle scenes are appropriately tense (and bloody) and the relationship between Childers and Hodges emphasizes the close bond which can form between comrades in arms. Less successful are the courtroom scenes and the interplay involving Hodges and prosecuting attorney Maj. Mark Biggs played by Guy Pearce (Ravenous) who adopts such a thick Brooklyn accent that it distracts from anything else he may be trying to do with the character.

The "moral" of the film is also presented "thickly" or with such a heavy hand that instead of triggering our mind with its subtlety, it simply hits us over the head with its obviousness.

Mr. Jones vacillates between being a confident Marine and an uncertain attorney without being totally effective in pulling these two aspects of his character together. His closing argument, an impassioned plea to the Marines sitting as jurors, was nicely done. Mr. Jackson plays the stoic and heroic figure quite well. His character may cut a corner or two in order to get the job done but he is clearly a man of honor, fighting for his honor, against opponents who do not understand what honor is.

That such things may take place should come as no surprise to those who live for God. In the spiritual competition, we stand against an enemy for whom there really are no "rules of engagement." He will use anything in his power to our detriment, including the bringing of false witnesses against us:

False witnesses did rise up; they laid to my charge things that I knew not. They rewarded me evil for good to the spoiling of my soul. Psalms 35:11-12 (KJV)

The difference between our situation and Childers' is that Childers was forced to defend himself against the very one whom he was called to serve, his country. When we serve God, He promises never to fail us nor forsake us. He will not leave us "hanging out to dry" as the movie depicts the US government's actions against Col. Childers. The lesson to be learned: Soldiers who serve in combat at the bequest of their government deserve better treatment than to find themselves later twisting in the wind of political expediency.

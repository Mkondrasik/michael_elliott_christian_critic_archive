Author-verified transcription

We've all complained about the fact that even with 200 channels to choose from, "There's never anything good on TV!" In fact, this has become almost a modern day mantra. But bad TV is exactly what Cynthia Topping, a TV executive for the fictional True TV Network, wants to provide. Her ratings brainstorm is to take an average guy off the street and film his life from morning until night, unscripted, unedited, uncensored. The result - EDtv.

Compared to a car wreck where you don't really want to see anything gruesome but you can't refrain from looking when you pass by, EDtv quickly becomes America's voyeuristic pastime. Everyone seems to have an opinion on his life. The tabloids run polls on who his girlfriend should be (much to the distress of his present girlfriend.) His entire life, as well as the lives of his family and friends, become grist for the public mill. And as many have found, that mill grinds exceedingly fine.

At the center of the public eye is Ed himself. Matthew McConaughey (Contact) infuses Ed with an affable genuineness that is all too easy to like. In the hands of a more caustic actor, we might have dismissed the character as nothing more than a shallow loser being exploited by a corporate greed, but Mr. McConaughey brings to Ed a kindness and an integrity that will connect with the audience.

Jenna Elfman (Krippendorf's Tribe) plays Shari, the girlfriend of whom 71% of America says isn't good enough for Ed. Her growing consternation at being constantly under the microscope is both understandable and amusing.

Ellen DeGeneres (Mr. Wrong), as Cynthia, the self-professed "golden goddess of television", has many very funny moments as she sees her brainchild become as popular as a Beatle (or at least as a Beanie Baby).

Screenwriters Lowell Ganz and Babaloo Mandel (City Slickers) have sprinkled the script with frequent sexual innuendoes and more than a few scenes of overt sexuality. As such, parents should be advised that this PG-13 rating is well-deserved.

Director Ron Howard (Apollo 13) has made a comedy that will undoubtedly draw valid comparisons to The Truman Show, which, while similar, had at least one important distinction. Truman did not know he was the subject of a TV show, as the director Cristof was the guiding force, scripting and manipulating characters to bring about his vision of Truman's life. The focus of that movie was Truman's growing self-awareness and understanding that there was more to life than what he was seeing.

In EDtv, Ed starts out knowing that he's on national television. There is no hidden director in the moon of EDtv. The focus of this movie is not so much on Ed himself, but rather, on fame -- our fascination with it and what it does to us.

Fame is something that our society covets greatly. Our media capitalizes upon it and our people can't get enough of it. To paraphrase one of the supporting characters, "It used to be that you became famous because you were good. Nowadays, you are thought to be good because you are famous."

It takes a meek man to be able to accept fame without losing one's integrity. Jesus Christ had to deal with fame during his earthly ministry. Since the scriptures say that "he was in all points tempted like as we are" (Hebrews 4:15), it would benefit us to see how he handled himself:

But so much the more went there a fame abroad of him: and great multitudes came together to hear, and to be healed by him of their infirmities. And he withdrew himself into the wilderness, and prayed. Luke 5:15-16 /KJV]

Jesus Christ never lost sight of the fact that he was on earth to do His Father's will. All that he did. All the miracles he brought into manifestation... All the people that he healed.. He did it all for the glory of God, not for self-aggrandizement. Anyone who finds himself in the public eye, cannot choose a better example to follow.

K-Pax

Author-verified transcription

Seventeen years ago, Jeff Bridges (The Contender) won an Oscar nomination for playing an alien in Starman. In K-Pax, he stays earthbound, turning the alien reins over to Oscar winner Kevin Spacey (American Beauty)... or does he?

Bridges plays the very human Dr. Mark Powell, an overworked headshrinker at the Psychiatric Institute of Manhattan who doesn’t much appreciate the fact that Bellevue keeps sending him the cases they can’t handle. Such a case is Prot (rhymes with goat and played by Spacey) who believes that he’s from the faraway world of K-Pax. An unconventional traveler (he arrives on a sunbeam), he’s here temporarily to finish a report on the BA-3 planet otherwise known as Earth. Not only does he believe this completely... he has a strange way of making everyone around him believe it as well.

Is Prot an alien or is he, as Powell puts it, “the most convincing delusional I’ve ever met?” That’s the question which drives the movie. As so often happens in films such as K-Pax, just trying to answer that question forces us, as well as the characters in the film, to examine that most curious thing known as “the human condition.”

Most of the action takes place within the walls of a mental institute a la One Flew Over The Cuckoo’s Nest. As with Nicholson’s character of R.P. McMurphy (who, come to think of it, could have been an alien himself), Prot’s presence has a great influence on the other patients in the ward. Soon, inmates and interns alike are vying for invitations to visit K-Pax... even if it means putting aside their own fears and neuroses for a time.

Kevin Spacey’s familiar air of sardonic superiority works well for Prot, who claims to be a member of an advanced culture. He spouts his words of wisdom with a tried patience, as if what he’s saying is common knowledge to everybody in the universe except for the remedial species known as earthlings. While Spacey does a fine job in a showcase role, Prot is really more of a device than anything else. He’s a catalyst, evoking change within the other characters who populate the film... most notably Dr. Powell.

Jeff Bridges’ journey into Prot’s psyche turns into his own journey of self-awareness, challenging him to evaluate his growing distant relationship with his second wife (Mary McCormack, Private Parts) and the children from both his marriages. Bridges plays the character arc very nicely.

Director Iain Softley (The Wings of the Dove) is more interested in character study than sci-fi elements and thus keeps the special effects at a minimum. His use of light is imaginative and quite appropriate given Prot’s unusual sensitivity to ultraviolet rays and his claims that he uses light as a mode of transport.

Based on the novel by Gene Brewer, K-Pax can be taken as an allegorical tale of deliverance and salvation. Indeed, there are Christ-like attributes to be found in the character of Prot. The allegory doesn’t hold up throughout the film but it does provide viewers with plenty of material with which to start an interesting discussion. Bear in mind, this is not a Starman kind of film. There are scenes which are quite disturbing in nature having to do with the reasons why some characters have found themselves in a hospital for the mentally ill.

While I did not find the ending to be ambiguous, judging from some of the comments I heard from patrons filing out of the theater, my view is hardly universal. There is clearly room for differing interpretations as to what really happened.

That Prot’s life had a positive impact upon those who come in contact with him is indisputable. Where hope was once a forgotten dream, it became a vital reality. Shouldn’t our lives deliver the same impact? His one attribute that really set him apart from those around him was his genuine interest in the people he met and the moment he was in. It is an attribute that we can easily emulate... even though we hail from that remedial planet known throughout the universe as Earth.

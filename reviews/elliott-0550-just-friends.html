Author-verified transcription

For comedies to work, they should really try to include some semblance of reality. A good comedy will connect with its audience by providing familiar points of reference to which we can relate. Bad comedies don't. Just Friends belongs to the latter group.

Chris (Ryan Reynolds) was a high school overweight loser who happened to be best friends with Jamie (Amy Smart), the hottest girl in town. Naturally he wanted to move the relationship to the next level but everyone except him knew that the likelihood of that happening was between nil and zero.

After a totally humiliating event, Chris left his New Jersey hometown in a huff and, ten years later, one hundred pounds lighter, 150% sexier, and 200% more embittered by the "friends only" treatment he received from Jamie, he settles in L.A. as an successful music producer.

When circumstance intervenes and places him in his hometown once again, he realizes that with his new svelte figure and big city "game" he might now have a shot at getting out of the dreaded "friend zone."

What he failed to calculate was the old adage: "Once a loser, always a loser." As he returns to his old stomping grounds, Chris loses his L.A. confidence and reverts back to his loser ways. And, to make matters worse, he has new competition for Jamie's attention. Another high school chump turned into a modern day hunk (Chris Klein) has arrived on the scene to complete Chris's worst nightmare.

Sorry... but I don't accept much of anything that director Roger Kumbel (Cruel Intentions) and screenwriter Adam 'Tex' Davis (Spring Break Lawyer) have to say about life and relationships. Unable to associate with any of the characters, the humor seemed to me to be both forced and unfunny.

Ryan Reynolds doesn't provide much of a bridge between his fat but sensitive self and his "amoral L.A. player" self. They are supposed to be two extremes of the same personality but Reynolds fails to add the nuances that link the polar opposites. The question is: if we can't buy into his character, how can we buy into the movie's premise?

I don't mean to infer that the problem is with his performance alone. It's just that he was given a character that at least had potential for artistic exploration. His costars would have loved a little of that. Amy Smart's character was written as a paper thin fantasy of a vaporous idea. There wasn't much she could do but to stand in front of a camera. Any time she tried to do more, the flaws of the script were magnified.

Anna Faris's character, (that of an oversexed and under educated pop star who attaches herself to Chris), bears little resemblance to any life form known to exist on this planet. While it gave her the opportunity to play broad comedy, it is a double-edged sword upon which she often impaled herself.

Chris Klein's character is fine up until the requisite (and last minute) plot twist which forces motivational jumps that no actor could pull off believably. Chris Marquette's character (Chris's brother) is built around a stale one joke gimmick that quickly grows old. Julie Hagerty as Chris's mom isn't even given that one joke.

The moral, though lamely presented, has some merit. Be honest - especially when dealing with others -- doubly so when dealing with yourself. The alternative leads to unhappiness.

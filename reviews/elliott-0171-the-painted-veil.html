The Painted Veil

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 29, 2006
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Marriage

Author-verified transcription

Period pieces based on classic novels generally fall into two categories: total bores or totally captivating. The Painted Veil falls neatly between the extremes.

Based on Somerset Maugham's novel of the same name, the story follows Kitty Fane (Naomi Watts), a woman of means who, due to the convention of the times, marries a man she didn't love only to find herself in the awkward position of having to follow him into the cholera-infested Chinese backcountry to avoid the scandal of a public divorce.

Her husband, Walter Fane (Edward Norton) is a bacteriologist who, after discovering his wife's infidelity, decides to accept the call for medical help from an area where volunteers have been dropping like tsetse flies.

Of course, the arduous journey and an elongated stay within an unfamiliar land gives both individuals the opportunity to do what they never did before. They were able to observe each other and learn about each other, growing to respect and admire various traits they never before knew existed.

Norton gives Walter a kind of social awkwardness hidden behind the starched collared propriety of the times. But he also adds a layer of cruelty born of a deep hurt brought on by betrayal. It is a well thought through and executed performance.

Watts takes Kitty from a spoiled and selfish woman of means to a woman whose riches aren't tallied in a bank account. Her marriage may have started out as one of convenience, it grew to become her lifeline and connection to the world around her.

Director John Curran directs with a slow but continually moving pace which fits for this story of a slowly evolving relationship. Filmed in China and set in the 1920s, the film achieves a level of authenticity that adds to the scope of the tale itself - a small, intimate story playing out in front of a grand, panoramic set.

The supporting cast includes Liev Schreiber, as the "other man," Toby Jones as a fellow Brit working and living among the Chinese, and Diana Riggs as the mother superior of a local convent. But, in the end, it is the work of Norton and Watts that drive the film forward and make it meaningful.

The meaning, for those with eyes to see, has to do with marriage - why it exists and how to make it successful.

God, recognizing that "it is not good for man to be alone," chose to make man a "help" suitable for him. By creating woman, God gave man a partner who could support him, encourage him, strengthen him and complete him. But for this union to work as designed, both parties must adopt the proper mindset. Marriage is not to be a "give or take" relationship. It is purely give.

The husband's concern should be for his wife. Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it. Ephesians 5:25 (KJV)

The wife's concern should be for her husband. Therefore as the church is subject unto Christ, so let the wives be to their own husbands in every thing. Ephesians 5:24 (KJV)

If both parties think not of what they can get out of the marriage relationship but rather what they can give to it, no force on this earth can tear it asunder.

Both Kitty and Walter entered into their marriage for the wrong reasons. Both made dreadful mistakes which they later came to deeply regret. These mistakes (and their initial reactions to them) nearly caused them to sever their union before they ever really tried to properly form it.

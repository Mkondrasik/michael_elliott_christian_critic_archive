Author-verified transcription

Size matters greatly, when we are talking about the size of one's heart.

Simon Birch has the distinction of being "the smallest delivery in the history of Gravestown Memorial Hospital." Not expected to live, Simon fooled them all. Because of this, he is convinced that God made him small for a reason. Simon believes that he is "destined" to be a hero. Nothing else makes sense to him. God must have great plans for him to have made him so different. His believing is contagious.

Based on the novel by John Irving, A Prayer for Owen Meany, Simon Birch weaves a bittersweet tale about love and friendship, faith and believing, one's person's search for his past and another person's search for his purpose.

First time director Mark Steven Johnson, best known for writing the screenplay for Grumpy Old Men, has assembled an immediately likable cast. Newcomer Ian Michael Smith, as the physically challenged but spiritually strong Simon Birch, will amaze you with his candid portrayal of this unforgettable character.

Joseph Mazzello (Jurassic Park) plays the young Joe Wenteworth, Simon's best friend. Joe and Simon gravitated towards each other because they were both social outcasts. Simon because of his size, Joe because his unmarried mother gave birth to him and, to this day, will not divulge the identity of his father. Mr. Mazzello is outstanding as the young teenager whose search for his father takes on a serious urgency.

The adult characters are also played with great charm. Oliver Platt (Bulworth) is disarmingly affable as Ben, boyfriend to Joe's mother. David Strathairn (L.A. Confidential) is Rev. Russell, who becomes increasingly exasperated with Simon's outspoken criticism of the church's social climate ("If God has made the church bake sale a priority, we're in a lot of trouble," Simon muses aloud during the Reverend's announcements.)

Ashley Judd (Kiss The Girls) is absolutely luminescent as Joe's mother, Rebecca. More of a mother to Simon than his own, she demonstrates the pureness of heart and bigness of soul that we would all do well to emulate. It is our loss that her character disappears from the screen as early as she did. Her presence was sorely missed.

There are many amusing moments in the film (the Christmas pageant is ruined because Simon, as baby Jesus wrapped in swaddling clothes ("I look like a burn victim," he complains), cannot suppress his raging male hormones when it comes to the young girl playing Mary.)

Spiritually, there is much that can be seen in the movie... But we must tread carefully, making sure the spiritual message is not based on error. For instance, Simon's unwavering assertion that God made him a dwarf for a reason - that it was all part of God's overall plan, could lead people down a path that attributes to God qualities He does not have or actions in which He took no part.

There is no scriptural documentation that God "makes" people with "defects," whether they be born with physical abnormalities or mental deficiencies. Attributing such things to God is unjustified and improper. Scriptures say that we are "fearfully [awesomely] and wonderfully made" [Psalms 139:14] and that God is "no respecter of persons" [Acts 10:34].

That being said, in the body of Christ there are many members. Each member in particular, regardless of his or her appearance or limitations, can and should contribute to the functioning of that body.

Nay, much more those members of the body, which seem to be more feeble, are necessary: And those members of the body, which we think to be less honourable, upon these we bestow more abundant honour; and our uncomely parts have more abundant comeliness. 1 Corinthians 12:22-23 (KJV)

Simon Birch may be considered "uncomely" to the eye, but he is well deserving of our esteem, respect and yes, our love.

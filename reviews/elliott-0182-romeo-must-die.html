Romeo Must Die

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 22, 2000
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Romans 12:19,21Ezekiel 25:12-17Psalms 9:3-10

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Vengeance; justice; forgiveness; overcoming evil with good; restraint

Author-verified transcription

Romeo Must Die provides an updated answer to Shakespeare's literary question: "Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" With martial artist Jet Li (Lethal Weapon IV) as the star, the reply might as well have been written, "To kicketh the vile hordes where the sun doth not shine."

Actually, let's not blame the Bard for the clumsy plot of this cinematic "version." Romeo Must Die has as much to do with Romeo and Juliet as Hamlet has to do with ham and eggs.

The setting is the much desired waterfront of Oakland, California - the turf shared by Asian and African gangs in a mutual distrust. The "gang war" escalates when the son of Asian kingpin Po (Henry O, Snow Falling on Cedars) is found dead. News of this reaches Po's other son, a policeman serving time in prison for helping his crooked family flee the Asian law.

When Han (which must be Chinese for Romeo?), played by Jet Li, learns of his brother's demise, he escapes his jail cell (even though he was manacled and suspended upside down at the time) and travels to Oakland to solve and avenge the murder. He soon finds that he'll have more than one murder to investigate.

He meets Trish (Aaliyah, in her film debut), the daughter of the African warlord, Isaak O'Day (Delroy Lindo, The Cider House Rules) and together they try and figure out who is killing whom.

First time director Andrzej Bartkowiak, who served as the cinematographer for Lethal Weapon IV, obviously knows much about the visual aspects of film. The look of Romeo Must Die is high gloss... sleek and sexy. There just isn't much beyond that appearance. The script is awful, starting with the main "hidden" motivation of the feuding families and deteriorating from there.

Jet Li does not yet have the cinematic charisma of a Jackie Chan or the linguistic ability to play the various nuances of American dialogue. Musical artist Aaliyah presents herself in a better light but is hampered by the weak script and story idea. Of the supporting cast, Anthony Anderson (Liberty Heights) as the clownishly comic Maurice is a standout, although his character's actions are among the most implausible and contrived in the entire film.

Obviously the main attraction to a film like Romeo Must Die is the opportunity to see incredible fight sequences by martial arts experts. There are eight different choreographed fight scenes in the film, each one with a unique feature added to it. But even here, Mr. Bartkowiak doesn't entirely trust his star and stunt men to effectively "wow" the audience with their natural skills. Instead we are subjected to computer assisted feats which idiotically defy the laws of physics, and simulated x-ray photography to show us the effect that certain blows have on the fragile bones of the human body.

Han's main motivation is one of avenging the death of his brother. This is something that the Word of God addresses directly:

Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord. Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good. Romans 12:19,21 (KJV)

It takes a great deal of courage, strength and emotional maturity to refuse responding to a wrong perpetrated against us with an equally wrong action. Christians need not be the "doormats" of the world, passively allowing ourselves to be abused and mistreated by those who choose not to love God or His Word, but neither should we aggressively play the devil's game by answering in kind.

In a world where inner-city gangs do exist and young men are killed over the most trivial of matters, it would be beneficial to remember that our God is a God of judgment and justice and will hold all accountable for the actions they take. Evil is evil regardless of the rationalization or justification one makes for its existence. Such a belief may make for a boring kung-fu movie, but it allows us to live with peace.

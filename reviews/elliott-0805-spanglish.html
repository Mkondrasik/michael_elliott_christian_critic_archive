Author-verified transcription

Spanglish, the latest film to come from Academy Award winning writer/director James L. Brooks, offers, as the title suggests, a clash of cultures. Into the lives of an affluent though dysfunctional LA family comes a beautiful illegal immigrant and her equally beautiful 12 year old daughter who serves as her mother's interpreter and the film's narrator.

Flor (Paz Vega) has come to America in search of a better life for her daughter (Shelby Bruce) and finds that, when offered, it comes with a price. Deborah Clasky (Tea Leoni), a recently downsized executive, hires Flor as her housekeeper and all but adopts Flor's daughter Christine as her pet project, even managing to get the girl a scholarship at an elite private school. It is an arrangement that Flor finds troubling.

Perhaps it is because she sees that Deborah has made a complete mess of her own life and family. She is dismissive to her husband (Adam Sandler), a renowned chef whose new restaurant recently received its "4 star rating" and downright hurtful to her daughter (Sarah Steele) who is struggling with self-image problems.

On the sidelines is Deborah's alcoholic mother (Cloris Leachman) who remains cognizant enough to offer sage words of advice at appropriate times.

The characters are very well defined and the relationships formed between them are strong and interesting but for some reason Brooks doesn't explore them very deeply. The differing relationships between the two mothers and their daughters should rightly be the focal point of the film but is too often abandoned for the "love triangle" that begins to develop among the adults.

Tea Leoni shines in a tarnished role. Deborah is a thoroughly despicable woman - selfish, thoughtless, unfaithful to her husband and incompassionate to her daughter. It is impossible to make her a likable character but Leoni at least makes her tolerable to watch.

Adam Sandler is in his Punch Drunk Love mode here. His Saturday Night Live goofiness has been shelved and in its place is a restrained, emotionally conservative, and somewhat bland character. Sandler, as Clasky, does have a wonderful moment with Flor as the two characters recognize the sexual tension between them but play against it (and against film convention) by not submitting to it.

Paz Vega is quite good in a role that requires her not to communicate in English for the first three-quarters of the movie. We have absolutely no trouble understanding what she is thinking or expressing at any time during the course of the film.

Flor's desire to keep Deborah's good intentions from "Americanizing" her daughter is certainly understandable. It is evident that the Clasky's affluence and all the trappings that go with it is a temptation that can easily turn the head of an impressionable twelve year old girl.

And yet, though the opportunities that the Claskys offer are tempting, Flor knows that there are other considerations that are more important.

God agrees. He has always warned his people not to get too intermingled with those whose values and beliefs are in conflict with his.

Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness? 2 Corinthians 6:14 (KJV)

Keeping our spiritual integrity intact as we live in this melting pot we call a world can be difficult. But we can take comfort in knowing that it is not impossible.

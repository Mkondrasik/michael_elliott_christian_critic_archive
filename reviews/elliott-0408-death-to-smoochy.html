Death to Smoochy

Michael Elliott

2 stars

1 hr: 43 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Integrity; persecution; worldliness; Christian witness

Author-verified transcription

When Barney was at the height of popularity with his kiddie audience, there grew an opposing faction that actually despised the syrupy sweet manner of the purple dinosaur. It was not unusual to come across websites that plastered the phrase "Barney Must Die" across one's monitor. In Death to Smoochy, a new black comedy from Danny Devito (Throw Mama From the Train), this sentiment is exaggerated to ridiculous levels.

Rainbow Randolph Smiley (Robin Williams, Bicentennial Man) is a foul-mouthed, corrupt kiddie entertainer who is not above taking money from dopey parents who want to give their children 15 minutes of fame in front of his cameras. His inevitable fall from grace shakes the industry, necessitating a search for a "squeaky clean" personality to put on the air and rebuild the trust with parents and children everywhere. Enter Smoochy the Rhino, a purple foam-costumed character created by Sheldon Mopes (Edward Norton, Fight Club), who performs mainly in hospitals and drug rehab centers. Sheldon is the real deal, concerned with providing families with wholesome entertainment mixed with moral messages. He's clean, he's honest, he's sincere, and he is soon driving everyone else at the station crazy with his unflagging integrity.

Randolph tries to ruin him so as to get his time slot back. Crooked charity organizer Merv Green (Harvey Fierstein, Torch Song Trilogy) wants to get rid of him because he's cutting into his ill-gotten profits. Producers Frank Stokes (Jon Stewart Playing by Heart) and Nora Wells (Catherine Keener, Being John Malkovich) are frustrated by him because he refuses to allow the blatant commercialization of his image and name to sell the advertisers' unhealthy products.

Death to Smoochy is an uneven production at its best. Yes, there are a number of clever moments or elements within the film. These include a crime scene chalk outline of a man in a rhino suit; Smoochy's insipid message songs ("My step dad isn't mean, he's just adjusting."); and a punch drunk prizefighter (Michael Rispoli, The Sopranos) who becomes Smoochy's biggest fan. Unfortunately, these moments are sporadic and simply don't add up to a fulfilling experience.

Robin Williams' shtick is looking more and more forced while being less and less funny. Because we never really accept him as a kiddie show personality, we are never drawn into one of the major conflicts of the story. Edward Norton is more successful at building a believable character, but even he has trouble not looking uncomfortable during his "too good for his own good" Smoochy moments. Catherine Keener has little to do as Smoochy's love interest.

What possible spiritual lesson can be drawn from this failed satirical look at a much maligned form of children's entertainment? In actuality, the lesson is fairly clear. Smoochy was targeted for extinction by evil men because its creator, Mopes, held fast to his integrity. We too, as we hold fast to our integrity as it relates to the truth of God's Word, will have our detractors and enemies. We too shall be targeted for attacks whenever we refuse to succumb to "the world's way" and instead follow the ways of God.

If ye were of the world, the world would love his own: but because ye are not of the world, but I have chosen you out of the world, therefore the world hateth you. John 15:19 (KJV)

Scripture advises us not to marvel if the world hates us. In fact, it is something for which we should be prepared. The hate directed towards us is both temporary and misdirected. As we maintain our integrity in the face of it, we may see the hatred begrudgingly turn into respect and respect evolve into amity.

Ye are of God, little children, and have overcome them: because greater is he that is in you, than he that is in the world. 1 John 4:4 (KJV)

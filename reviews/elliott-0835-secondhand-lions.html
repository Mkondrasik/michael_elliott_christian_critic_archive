Author-verified transcription

How does a boy become a man? This is a far deeper and a much more meaningful question than it might at first appear. It is also the central issue in the story of Secondhand Lions, a sweet, life-affirming tale by the screenwriter of The Iron Giant.

Walter (Haley Joel Osment, A.I.) is a fourteen year old boy who is not entering puberty with much grace. Lacking a father figure and saddled with a selfish, irresponsible and lying mother (Kyra Sedgwick, Personal Velocity), Walter is confused much of the time and unsure of himself all of the time.

When Walter's eccentric great-uncles return to Texas and their family farm after a forty year absence, Walter is taken to them by his inheritance-minded mother in order to find out if there's any truth to the rumors of their wealth. What he learns in the summer he spends with them will prove to be infinitely more valuable than money.

On the surface, Hub (Robert Duvall, Open Range) and Garth (Michael Caine, The Quiet American) give every indication of being nothing more than a pair of crazy old Texas goats. Asserting that they've come back to the farm to die, they spend their days looking for distractions, from firing off shotguns to scare off salesmen to buying broken down zoo animals to use in a private safari. But the longer Walter stays with them, the more he is able to see beneath the surface of their idiosyncratic ways.

When he finds an old photograph of a beautiful young woman (Emmanuelle Vaugier, 40 Days and 40 Nights), his curiosity and imagination are stirred. Asking questions of Garth and Hub, he slowly begins to learn about the kind of life they have lived. In so doing, he also begins to learn the kind of things that a boy on the verge of manhood should know. Things like what to value in life, how to treat people, how to believe and how to stand up for what one believes.

Haley Joel Osment has added a few inches to his frame since we last saw him in A.I. His talent, however, has remained intact. He conveys Walter's transformation with considerable skill and confidence.

Academy Award winning actors Michael Caine and Robert Duvall are simply a pleasure to watch. And thus it's a dual pleasure watching them work together. Playing characters who have been around the block more than a few times, they ease into the relaxed and playful banter which suggests the familiarity and trust that is built over many years.

Writer/director Tim McCanlies (Dancer, Texas Pop. 81) has struck cinematic gold by illustrating the impact that a father figure has upon the development of a young and impressionable teen. We not only see the changes taking place within Walter as a result of his association with his uncles, but Hub has a tendency to launch into his "what every boy needs to know before he becomes a man" speech whenever he spots a lad who it might help.

One of the "lessons" he imparts involves believing, something that Walter has trouble doing because he's been lied to all his life. Hub tells Walter that something doesn't have to be true in order to believe it... If you want to believe something, go ahead and believe it... no one can stop you.

As odd as that sounds, there is some logic to his lesson, especially when applied to spiritual realities. Those who might be looking for proof one way or another before they decide if God and His promises are true will never find what it is they're looking for. The proof of truth is promised to them who believe first.

Jesus saith unto her, Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God? John 11:40 (KJV)

Seeing the glory (manifested power) of God is conditional upon our believing. Believing is the key to receiving anything from God. Perhaps now we can understand why Jesus said the following to doubting Thomas:

Jesus saith unto him, Thomas, because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed: blessed [are] they that have not seen, and [yet] have believed. John 20:29 (KJV)

The Stepford Wives

June 11, 2004

1 hr: 30 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Marriage; Complementarity; Perfection

Author-verified transcription

"Perfect doesn't work," says Nicole Kidman's character in the comedy remake of the 1975 sci-fi thriller The Stepford Wives. She's right - perfection may be impossible to attain. But in the case of this film, I would have settled for competent. It turns out to be neither.

Loosely based upon the book by Ira Levin, The Stepford Wives follows the plight of Joanna Eberhart (Nicole Kidman), the president of a TV network, who suffers an emotional breakdown when her reality TV pilot ends disastrously and she is fired from her high profile position.

Thinking that a more serene lifestyle will comfort her and strengthen their marriage, her husband Walter moves them to the idyllic Connecticut community of Stepford. It doesn't take Joanna long to recognize that there is something not quite "normal" about the women of Stepford. Perhaps it's the robot-like way they have of waiting on their spouses. Perhaps it's the perfectly coiffed hair and pressed, color-coordinated outfits. Then again, it just might be the sparks that shoot out of their necks when they malfunction.

Don't ask Walter. He's too busy bonding with the other husbands over drinks and cigars while playing with grown up toys at the Men's Association Club. He seems to be falling right into step with the Stepford way of life. That spells bad news for Joanna who's not conforming as well or as quickly as he (or the other residents of Stepford) would like.

Director Frank Oz and screenwriter Paul Rudnick misstep badly with this adaptation of the classic women's lib horror film. The original film was chilling in its depiction of women being mechanized into subservience by the husbands who resented gender equality. The remake instead tries to go for the laugh but absolutely fails to satirize or spoof the original.

After starting with a moderately amusing backhanded jab at reality TV (an easy target), the film quickly begins to sputter and stall. Kidman's character is morose, Broderick's character has no spine, and everyone else is so one dimensional as to be instantly forgettable.

Rudnick has also added a homosexual couple to the community of Stepford. Roger Bart plays the "wife" counterpart to his character's gay Republican partner. They are a source of cheap laughs based on clichéd stereotypical behavior but completely unneeded from a narrative standpoint.

This film does not even attempt to comment upon the feminist movement or the woman's role in the world. If anything, this version of The Stepford Wives is about the stupidity of men. The men of Stepford, feeling emasculated by the smart and successful women they married, decided to make the "perfect" mate by eliminating everything that made her successful in the first place. As Joanna says near the end of the film, "Perfect doesn't work."

It doesn't work because it isn't attainable. It isn't even definable. The ideal wife is not one who meets some kind of weird criteria drafted by male fantasies. She is a woman who commits 100% of herself to the marriage relationship and by doing so supports and encourages her husband to be the best man he is capable of becoming. She completes her husband as he completes her. That is how God designed it and that is how it works best.

And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him. Genesis 2:18 (KJV)

The word "meet" means complimentary; appropriate to his needs. Woman was initially designed by God and He is perfect. Man need not try to improve upon what he has already provided.

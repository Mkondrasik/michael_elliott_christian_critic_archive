Author-verified transcription

Life can sometimes throw us curves that we never see coming. How we choose to handle them marks the difference between being a victim and being a victor. In Love Is All You Need, two families come together in Italy to celebrate the pending marriage of Astrid (Molly Blixt Egeland) and Patrick (Sebastian Jessen). Each member is dealing with something that they don’t quite know how to handle.

Astrid’s mom, Ida (Trine Dyrholm), has not only lost her hair to cancer treatments, she has just discovered that she may have lost her husband to a younger woman. On the way to Italy she literally runs into the father of the groom, Philip (Pierce Brosnan). Philip has closed himself off from social interaction, never having gotten over the untimely death of his wife.

Joining the small wedding party is Benedikte (Paprika Steen), Patrick’s aunt, whose lack of self awareness leads to one inappropriate comment after another; Leif (Kim Bodnia), Ida’s cheating husband who has brought his mistress to the family affair; and of course the bride and groom who are struggling with the growing realization that this whole thing might have been a terrible mistake.

Love Is All You Need straddles that fine line between comedy and drama. It succeeds because it stays honest to both. Each character is richly drawn and executed. At the top of the list is a remarkable performance from Danish actress Trine Dyrholm.

The film illustrates an important distinction: When it comes to living our lives, what we do is not as important as why we do it. If we make what appears to be the right choice but we make it for the wrong reasons, we are still likely to be unhappy with the results. In this respect, the filmmakers have chosen an appropriate title for their film because love (the pure, selfless kind) is what is needed to help us reach the important decisions of life.

If Leif acted out of love instead of lust, he would have remained by his wife’s side throughout her treatments. If Philip acted out of love instead of grief or anger, he would not have shut himself off from the world and would have been more involved with the raising of his son.

When love becomes the dominant motivating factor in our decisions, we are making those decisions from a positive, healthy mindset. We are turning towards something, not away from something; we look to the resulting consequences with hope and joy, not with fear and trepidation. We stop being victims of our own lives and poor choices. Love enables us to embrace life and all its wonders.

“My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth.” 1 John 3:18 (KJV)

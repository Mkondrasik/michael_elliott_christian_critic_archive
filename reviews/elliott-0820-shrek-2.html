Author-verified transcription

What happens after the "happily ever after?" That's a question most fairy tales don't address. Thankfully, Shrek is not a typical fairy tale and its makers have decided to return to carry on with this unusual "once upon a time" tale.

Shrek 2 picks up where the original film ended. Shrek (Mike Myers) and Fiona (Cameron Diaz) are in wedded bliss and as happy as two ogres in a swamp... literally speaking. But their marriage has yet to face the hurdle that awaits all newlyweds... the in-laws.

After receiving an invitation to the royal ball being organized by Princess Fiona's parents in their kingdom of Far Far Away, Shrek, Fiona, and their annoying companion Donkey (Eddie Murphy) climb into their onion carriage and travel to the glitz and glamour of Fiona's Hollywood-like homeland. The buzz of anticipation fills the air as the excited subjects of the land await the royal couple to arrive at the red carpet ceremony. Of course, the subjects weren't expecting ogres.

Neither were the king and queen (John Cleese and Julie Andrews). The king had naturally assumed his daughter married Prince Charming (Rupert Everett), the son of the legendary Fairy Godmother (Jennifer Saunders) with whom he had a prearranged agreement. Now, in order to escape the wrath of the woman with the wand, he has to make Shrek disappear. He turns to the only one in the kingdom able to defeat an ogre - the incomparable swashbuckler, Puss in Boots (Antonio Banderas).

Shrek 2, in many ways, is an improvement over the original film. The satire on popular culture seems sharper; the crude humor has been softened; the characters are both familiar and fresh; and the computer generated artwork seems more technologically advanced. Bottom line - the film is a winner for all concerned.

Among the new characters which the sequel introduces, Puss in Boots is likely to be an audience favorite. The jealous reaction of Donkey to the footwear-clad feline is hysterical. As the fairy godmother, Jennifer Saunders is saucy fun and Larry King lends his gravelly voice to the bartending ugly stepsister.

Quick comedic vignettes place our fairy tale characters in recognizable scenes from films such as Lord of the Rings, Flashdance, Spider-man, Mission Impossible, Ghostbusters, and From Here to Eternity among many others. They are well conceived and well executed and are a great deal of fun to pick out during the film.

Shrek 2, as its predecessor, also has a message. It is one driven home through the film's ingenious spoof of Hollywood and the undue importance it places on physical beauty and image-consciousness. In their isolated swamp, Shrek and Fiona had each other and their love was based upon things far removed from the outward appearance they shared. In the shallow world of Far Far Away (i.e. Hollywood), there was the constant reminder that they would always be judged by how they looked.

It's an oft repeated lesson, but most valuable lessons are. It never hurts to be reminded to look beneath the surface and past the façade. If we don't, we'll never discover the inner beauty or ugliness that often lies behind the mask.

Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness. Even so ye also outwardly appear righteous unto men, but within ye are full of hypocrisy and iniquity. Matthew 23:27-28 (KJV)

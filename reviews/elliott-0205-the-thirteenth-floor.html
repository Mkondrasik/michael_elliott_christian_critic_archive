Author-verified transcription

The Thirteenth Floor opens with a quote from Descartes who first opined: "I think therefore I am." This movie, which takes a look at virtual reality carried to the nth degree, turns that quote upside down. The question these characters keep asking is, "I think, but am I real?"

Hammond Fuller (Armin Mueller-Stahl, The X-Files) and his team of very talented computer programmers have developed the technology whereby they can "build" entire virtual cities, peopled by computerized simulations. By keeping the program running continually, the simulations appear to live full and happy lives.

Additionally, they have managed a way to plug themselves into the program. Having designed characters that are compatible with their physical and psychological design, they can "jack" their consciousness into their simulated counterparts and "be" a part of this make believe reality.

Until something goes terribly wrong. Hammond, on one of his trips to the simulated world of the 1930's, discovers something which disturbs him to the point of wanting the system shut down. Upon his return to the "real world," he is killed before he can implement his plan.

His partner, Douglas Hall (Craig Bierko, The Suburbans) is the prime suspect, although he's pretty sure he didn't do it. He finds that Hammond left him a clue in the simulated world of the 1930's, so Douglas "jacks" himself in, on a mission to prove his innocence.

What he finds is that the pretended reality of the virtual world has been made known to some of its simulated characters, forcing the characters to ask themselves: "What is life and what is real?" These are deep questions. Whereas The Matrix used virtual reality as a framework for its action thriller fight between good and evil, The Thirteenth Floor strives to use the theme for a moral probing.

The weakest link to the film is the script by director Josef Rusnak (The Way We Are) and Ravel Centeno-Rodriguez. The key for excellent sci-fi must be the ability to have us believe that such worlds and events could exist. The Thirteenth Floor never gets us to suspend our disbelief. The script also tips its hand way too early, clumsily foreshadowing every plot twist.

On the other hand, the overall look of the film is dazzling. The use of colors and textures for each "reality" are distinct and appropriate, giving an unreal, otherworldly quality to the settings.

Craig Bierko does some fine work as the confused and frustrated partner of Hammond Fuller, as does Gretchen Mol (Celebrity) as Fuller's daughter, Jane. Each of the principal actors are able to flex their thespian muscles by playing multiple roles: their "real" characters as well as their simulated counterparts. With the exception of Mr. Mueller-Stahl, who brings his customary European sophistication to both roles, the actors seem to relish the opportunity. Although we were never quite able warm up to Vincent D'Onofrio's (The Newton Boys) two quirky characterizations.

While Mr. Rusnak keeps the pace moving throughout, it is clearly a case of style over substance. A little less seasoning and a lot more meat would have made The Thirteenth Floor quite a memorable experience.

These virtual reality movies that have been produced in the past year can tend to be confusing as "reality" gets bent and manipulated for dramatic purposes. All the more reason for us to have a standard that defines reality. Things that we see or experience could be real or could be illusion. An unchanging standard for truth helps us to answer the question these characters sought. "What is life?" and "What is real?" are questions that only the Creator of life can truly answer.

By the way, Descartes wasn't totally original with his quote. Long before he arrived on the scene, God had Solomon record these words: "As he thinketh in his heart, so is he." Proverbs 23:7 [KJV]. Not only is thinking indicative of "being," it defines what kind of being we are. All the more reason for us to hold the truth of the Word of God paramount in our hearts as our first and only reality.

Author-verified transcription

The Whole Nine Yards was a moderately amusing fish out of water tale back in the year 2000. But there's a problem with fish. If they are kept out of the water too long, they begin to smell. Nowhere is this more noticeable than in the film's sequel - The Whole Ten Yards. It is proof that, sometimes, going the extra yard can be a big mistake.

Most of the main characters have returned. Oz (Matthew Perry) is now married to Cynthia (Natasha Henstridge) who use to be the wife of Jimmy "The Tulip" Tudeski (Bruce Willis), ex-hit man for the mob. Jimmy is married to Jill (Amanda Peet) who used to work in Oz's dental practice but always wanted to kill people for a living. She just isn't very good at it.

Kevin Pollack, whose character, Janni, was killed in the first film, returns as Janni's mobster father, Lazlo. Lazlo kidnaps Cynthia in an attempt to track down Jimmy and avenge his son's death. As he expected, Oz leads him straight to the now domesticated ex-killer who has been hiding out in Mexico wearing bunny slippers and dreaming, rather emotionally, of becoming a father.

The problem with the film is that director Howard Deutch and screenwriter George Gallo give us no good reason to revisit these characters. Perry's nervous antics and pratfalls seem stale and gratuitous. Willis just goes for cheap laughs and doesn't even pretend to care about character consistency. Pollack is good for a chuckle or two but his characterization is simultaneously broad and one-dimensional so we soon tire of him as well.

The basic problem is that these characters are tossed into a film that has no real story to tell. The story that is being told is being told poorly. The plot twist that comes at the end makes as much sense as paying these actors seven figures to do what they do. All in all, it is an easily forgettable movie.

The first film managed to appeal to me because of Perry's character, Oz. Despite the sinful nature of most everyone else in the film, Oz kept a certain level of purity. He was motivated by love. That's lacking in this sequel. The predominant motivation of this film is one of greed. That applies not only to the characters but apparently also to those involved in the making of the film. Perhaps that is the reason why it isn't that successful.

He that is greedy of gain troubleth his own house; but he that hateth gifts shall live. Proverbs 15:27 (KJV)

Bringing this sequel to the screen without having anything further to say about these characters or their relationships was a foolish idea and it shows on the screen.

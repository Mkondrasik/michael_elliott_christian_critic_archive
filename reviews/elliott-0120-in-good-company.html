In Good Company

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 14, 2005
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Proverbs 23:4Proverbs 28:201 Timothy 6:9

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Work; integrity; money; business ethics; contentment

Author-verified transcription

It can be hard to grow old in a culture that shows little respect for its elders. Nowhere is that more of a truism than in America’s corporate culture. In Good Company takes a lighthearted look at what happens when experience is forced to take a back seat to youth.

Sales manager Dan Foreman (Dennis Quaid) is on the wrong end of 50. With his wife (Marg Helgenberger) unexpectedly expecting another child and his oldest daughter (Scarlett Johannson) entering into NYU, the time couldn’t be worse for him to discover that the job he’s held for nearly a quarter century is being given to a younger guy.

Carter Duryea (Topher Grace) is a 26-year-old corporate flack who receives his promotion when the corporation he’s in buys up the corporation that owns the national sports magazine where Dan works. As Dan tries to adjust to working for a boss half his age with no practical experience to speak of, Carter begins parroting the company’s bottom-line mentality by cutting back on payroll (i.e. people). But as anyone in middle management knows, the company line is not always the best thing for the company or those who work for it.

To add to the comedy/drama, the shy and insecure Carter takes his eye off the ledger sheets long enough to notice that Dan’s daughter (Scarlett Johannson) is a babe. And so begins a quasi-romantic relationship that must be kept hidden from the over-protective Dan who would blow a gasket if he knew his daughter was dating his boss.

Writer/director Paul Weitz (About a Boy) has crafted a mature comedy dealing with interesting themes but lacks the finesse needed to smooth out the rough edges of his script. As a result, In Good Company gives us a number of interesting moments and some worthwhile discussion topics to consider. Unfortunately, the film never quite comes together to live up to its potential.

It isn’t the fault of the actors. Dennis Quaid does some nice work as the amiable Dan whose moral values define him as they are evidenced in every aspect of his life - both at home and at work.

Topher Grace makes some interesting choices as Carter. The role could have easily become either caustic or whiney but Grace makes Carter’s emotional journey just as interesting as Dan’s.

What Carter learns from Dan is that good salesmanship is about more than dazzling a client with snappy patter or flashy charts. Good salesmanship is about building relationships over time. It is about identifying needs and working to address those needs in a way that benefits all parties involved.

A godly business or financial transaction must be a win-win for both sides. The problem with bottom line thinking is that it is too one-dimensional. All the focus is on the company’s profits and while that is an important consideration, profitability should be the fruit of our labors — not the motivation behind them.

There is no lasting satisfaction that can be had in selling someone something that they don’t need simply for the sake of increasing profits. Doing so may enlarge the coffers but it will shrink the soul.

Labour not to be rich: cease from thine own wisdom. Proverbs 23:4 (KJV)

If being rich is our sole reason for working, we are destined to become discouraged, disillusioned and bitterly disappointed. What Carter eventually saw in Dan was how much the older man loved what he did - because he did it the “old school” way - with his integrity intact and his values unblemished.

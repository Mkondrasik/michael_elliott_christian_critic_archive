Author-verified transcription

The message of forgiveness is a dominant theme in Mercy Streets. This is understandable as Christian filmmakers are at the helm of the picture. Mercy Streets represents another step forward in the production of films based on Christian values.

The plot is an updated twist on the Prince and the Pauper story. Orphaned twin brothers are leading a hard life on the streets. At the age of fourteen, they are separated by an act of violence. Jeremiah (David A.R. White, The Moment After), presuming his brother to be dead, grows up to be a man of God and is one month away from being ordained as an Episcopal priest. His brother, John (also played by David A.R. White) survived the act of violence against him, only to fall into a life of crime under the tutelage of con artist Rome (Eric Roberts, Cecil B. DeMented).

Newly released from prison, John desires to go straight, but reluctantly agrees to do one last job for Rome. Losing his nerve in the middle of the con, John double crosses Rome and flees from him with the "seed money," turning inexplicably to the brother he hasn't seen since they were fourteen.

Before they connect, Rome, confusing Jeremiah for John, captures the wrong man and holds him against his will. Discovering his brother is alive, Jeremiah is consumed with guilt and agrees to take his brother's place in the con to save him from Rome and his cronies. Meanwhile, John has taken on Jeremiah's place, giving sermons and dealing with a relationship crisis with his girlfriend, Sam (Cynthia Watrous, TV's The Guiding Light).

David A.R. White is quite effective in the dual role of Jeremiah/John. A bit more secure playing the darker John than the confused and guilt-ridden Jeremiah, he nonetheless presents two distinctly different personalities which helps us follow the sometimes choppy story line.

Eric Roberts lightens his bad-guy character with enough humor to keep his scenes interesting and non-melodramatic. Appearing relaxed and natural, he's in one of the best roles he's had in years and reminds us just how good he can be.

However, the film does have some uneven elements to it. Cynthia Watrous as Jeremiah's girlfriend and Shiek Mahmud Bey (Flawless) as his policeman roommate simply don't demonstrate the same level of acting expertise as the stars of the picture. Lawrence Taylor (Shaft) and Stacy Keach (American History X) each have small roles in the film where they play priests who try to give Jeremiah spiritual counsel but their scenes are not integral to the story and are ultimately wasted.

The script, by director Jon Gunn and co-writer John Mann, does manage to avoid the heavy-handed "preachiness" of some Christian films, but it could have benefited from a strengthening of Jeremiah's character. A weak preacher, (his one practice sermon is painfully dull) we never see his conviction to the call of God. Granted, he was struggling with his past, not accepting that God could forgive him for his perceived wrong, but the film ended up giving the impression that receiving forgiveness from his brother was more important to him than receiving forgiveness from God.

Still, Mercy Streets does offer us much spiritual food for thought. We, as humans, spend far too much time condemning ourselves for past mistakes. God would have us spend our time focused more upon the present and the future. By focusing upon the past, we bind ourselves with the guilt and regret of where we have been and what we have done. God has already forgiven us for our past sins. It is we who must learn to forgive ourselves more readily.

There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. Romans 8:1 (KJV)

As one of the characters infers, "The doors to Hotel Forgiveness are open but there's no room for baggage. Leave it outside."

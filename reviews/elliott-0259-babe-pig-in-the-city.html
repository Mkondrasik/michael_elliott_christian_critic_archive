Babe: Pig In The City

Author-verified transcription

The Babe is back in town. Yes, that delightfully pink porcine star of the 1995 hit, Babe, graces the screen once more in a sequel which is well worthy of the original. Although it doesn't quite reach the same dizzying heights of inventive brilliance as the first movie, it retains much of its charm and heart.

Returning characters, in addition to the pig, include the boss and his wife, Arthur and Esme Hoggett (James Cromwell and Magda Szubanski), Ferdinand the duck, the three singing mice (who have expanded their repertoire to include Elvis and Edith Piaf), and the unseen narrator (the rich and resonant voice of Roscoe Lee Brown) who provides the film with its immediately recognizable storybook flavor.

The story picks up where the original ended. Babe has won the sheep-herding contest and returns home the conquering hero. After an accident temporarily disables the boss, the bank threatens to repossess the farm. The only hope for salvation lies with... Babe. It would seem that a sheep-herding pig is something of a rarity and the big county fairs are more than willing to pay a generous appearance fee for a demonstration.

So Esme and Babe pack their bags, heading for the city to raise money to save the farm. Not surprisingly, they never make it to the fair.

Unexpectedly delayed at the airport, Esme finds that the city is not a particularly hospitable place when you're traveling with a pig. Eventually, she is led to a hotel which secretly specializes in animal tenants, thereby breaking a few dozen zoning laws.

Soon, the screenwriters get the human characters out of the way to focus our attention upon the real stars of the film... the animals.

In the city, there aren't as many varieties or species as are on the farm. What we find instead are various dogs and cats, a troupe of performing primates, and a goldfish. But there is also a different caste distinction that has developed among these city dwellers: Those who have humans and those who do not.

The city critters are just as uniquely original as the barnyard varieties. We meet Flealick, a myopic dog whose arthritic hind legs are strapped to a wheeled cart; Thelonius, a world weary orangutan who strives to maintain his dignity as an animal in a world dominated by humans; Bob and Zootie, a pair of hip-talking chimps; a bull terrier who is, at first, Babe's nemesis only to become his strongest supporter; and a pink poodle who, as a canine Blanche Dubois, has always depended upon the kindness of strangers... only to have been repeatedly disappointed.

Much has been made by those who object to the darker nature of this film which basically follows up what was a light and breezy family film. Certainly, the mood is darker and the setting harsher in this sequel than the first farm-based version. But then cities are darker places, aren't they? (The first city mentioned in the Bible was built by the world's first murderer, Cain.)

What the movie does show us, however, is that even in the midst of the unfair and sometimes cruel events that befall the pig and his new friends, Babe is still able to exhibit the qualities of godliness to which we cannot help but respond appreciatively.

As the narrator says, "A kind and steady heart can heal the world." The gentleness with which Babe speaks to an embarrassed Thelonius, who is struggling to get dressed after city workers removed his clothes, or the bravery he demonstrates to save the bull terrier who had been maliciously chasing him through the canal lined streets of the fictitious city, sends us a message that regardless of what surrounds us externally, it does not have to impact us internally.

We can (and should) keep our hearts pure and our minds sound according to our knowledge of God's Word. As we live life and deal with the sometimes harsh and cruel world in which we live, we faithfully maintain our kind and steady heart. That is what enables us to "shine as lights in this crooked and perverse world."

This is a valuable lesson for us to learn and a wonderful example for us to follow. For that, we should all "thank the pig."

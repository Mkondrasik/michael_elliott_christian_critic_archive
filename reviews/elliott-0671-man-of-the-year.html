Author-verified transcription

Comedian Pat Paulsen used his spot on the Smother Brothers' Comedy Hour in 1968 to humorously announce his candidacy for President of the United States. His campaign generated many laughs but few votes. Barry Levinson has taken the bit one step further by supposing a world where the clown actually becomes king.

In Man of the Year, Tom Dobbs (Robin Williams), the popular comedic host of a politically oriented talk show (a la Jon Stewart), surprises his manager (Christopher Walken) and his joke writer (Lewis Black), not to mention the entire nation, by throwing his hat in the political ring. Running as an independent, Dobbs shocks the world by winning the popular vote and becoming President Elect.

Unbeknownst to Dobbs, a new voting technology, designed to make the process more manageable, had a glitch in it the size of the national debt that skewed the election results Dobbs' way. Whistle-blower Eleanor Green tries to warn her company about the snafu but soon learns that her bosses would rather have her dead than hear that their multi-billion dollar product doesn't work.

Her only hope is that Dobbs, once hearing the truth, will know what he has to do. The only question remaining is... now that he's President, will he want to do it?

For a biting satire like Man of the Year to be successful, it has to have some teeth. For whatever reason, writer/director/producer Barry Levinson tries to gum his way into pointing out the flaws of our current political system. There is no edge to this comedy and, without an edge, the humor often doesn't work and the satire is completely missed.

Robin Williams looks uncharacteristically uncomfortable in this role. It should have been a slam dunk for the outrageously inventive performer but, like Levinson, his Tom Dobbs seems unwilling to go for the jugular. Playing it safe has never been a Williams' trademark and it doesn't suit him here.

Laura Linney has a thankless role of being a victim of a corporate cover-up and a love interest in a loveless relationship. She's incredibly talented but has limited possibilities with the confines written into her role.

Levinson tries to score points with his observations of how dependent the parties are on special interest groups. But the points he makes are hardly fresh or new and the way in which he makes them are not particularly memorable.

The film sputters its way through the campaign and post-campaign, adds an extraneous bit about smoking and emphysema, and blends in an implausible romance that, though important to the plot, never is important to us.

The one interesting characteristic that Dobbs wants to see in his elected officials and that he must summon the courage to manifest himself is one of integrity. Dobbs rants that his opponents don't have the integrity to be honest about their ties to special interests. He finds himself in a position of having the integrity to be honest about the tainted election or keeping his mouth shut and doing the good he thinks he might do as President.

The nice thing about integrity is that there is no grey to it. It's a black and white issue. We either have it or we don't. Having it is preferable. Regardless of the immediate consequences, the long term benefits are a blessing that knows no comparison.

The integrity of the upright shall guide them: but the perverseness of transgressors shall destroy them. Proverbs 11:3 (KJV)

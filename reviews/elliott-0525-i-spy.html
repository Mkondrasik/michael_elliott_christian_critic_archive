Author-verified transcription

The 1960s television show, I Spy, with its too cool for words attitude and hip, rapid-fire banter between its stars, became an immediate sensation in its day. The new movie which bears its name borrows very little else that is recognizable from the original show. Still, the filmmakers captured the essence of what made I Spy so successful... a great chemistry between the two actors who play the leads.

In the modern version, Alexander Scott (Owen Wilson, Behind Enemy Lines) is a bumbling special agent for BNS (Bureau of National Security). A top secret stealth plane, nicknamed Switchblade, has been stolen and is being auctioned off to the highest bidder by the nefarious arms dealer, Gunders (Malcolm McDonald, Star Trek: Generations). It will be up to Alex to stop the auction and get the plane back.

The problem is one of access. Alex needs a cover to break into Gunders' heavily guarded Hungarian enclave where an international bad guy "convention" seems to be occurring. Enter Kelly Robinson (Eddie Murphy, Showtime), the brash, egocentric, and undefeated middleweight champion of the world. He's already been invited by Gunders to defend his title against the Hungarian champion.

All Alex has to do is join Kelly's entourage, slip into Gunders' lair, find an invisible plane, and keep Kelly's ego in check long enough to keep them both alive. It's the latter task that proves to be most difficult.

I Spy has the feel of a summer popcorn movie. Nothing too deep or substantial. Explosions, jokes and sexual innuendoes abound. Director Betty Thomas (Private Parts) has a firm enough handle on the material. She keeps the pace lively and gives her stars the latitude they need to develop the humorous give and take which lies at the heart of their relationship.

Screenwriting by committee can often lead to disastrous results but the four credited writers for I Spy have managed to deliver a script which steers clear of any major potholes which might have sunk the project. The film contains a fair number of laughs, a logical story line, and just enough homage to give tribute to the show from which it was derived.

Even with an adequate script, I Spy has always worked because of the contrasting personalities involved in the closely-knit relationship between Kelly and Alex. The original I Spy team (Robert Culp and Bill Cosby) worked together so well that they made it look easy. It isn't. Owen Wilson and Eddie Murphy may not have achieved the same level of success as did their predecessors, but it is clear that they enjoy feeding off each other. This spells enjoyment for the audience.

They both play to their strengths: Murphy as the fast-talking, confident celebrity and Wilson as the slow-thinking, unsure of himself secret agent. To the mix is added Framke Janssen (Run Lola Run) as an attractive and alluring BNS agent; Gary Cole (Office Space) as the top BNS spy who gets all the best spy equipment; and Malcolm McDowell as the villain, whose appearance amounts to little more than a cameo.

Kelly and Alex are on the same team but their personalities continually clash. In this is a reminder to us that personalities or personal differences carry very little weight in the spiritual world. We are called to function together and to strive for the unity of the faith. This is something that is available to achieve. We simply must be willing to set ego aside and recognize the value and strength that others bring to the "team."

Author-verified transcription

With a title that sounds more apropos for a sports film, The Fighting Temptations uses a standard Hollywood formula mixed with an uplifting soundtrack to tell the story of one man’s redemption and a church’s rebirth.

Darren (Cuba Gooding Jr., Boat Trip) is a big-city advertising executive who hails from a small southern town. In his job, he has become accustomed to lying or at least phrasing things in order to tell people what he thinks they want to hear. Living far beyond his means, he finds himself deep in debt after losing his high-paying job. His possible salvation comes in the form of an inheritance. His aunt died and bequeathed to Darren a handsome sum. But there’s a catch.

In order to collect the inheritance, Darren must take the gospel choir of Auntie’s beloved southern church to a national competition and compete for the big money prize. So Darren travels back to his roots where he discovers the church has been ravaged from within by what can only be described as unchristian behavior on the part of its parishioners. The choir, as it is, is every bit as bad as the one Whoopi Goldberg started out with in Sister Act.

Darren’s first mission is to recruit decent singers for the choir and true to his form, he begins promising everything from record deals to a cut of the prize money. After finding little talent among the church membership, he expands his search, ultimately selecting from the best voices he can find from the nearby prison.

Despite the lying, backbiting, power struggles and petty arguing that takes place, the choir starts sounding pretty good. Church membership begins to pick up and the gospel choir competition doesn’t seem such a bad idea. But what will happen once the choir members find out that everything Darren has told them is a lie?

Cuba Gooding Jr. is fine as the small town boy who grows up in the big city and loses his moral way only to discover it again. His choral “direction” gets a bit too frenetic for my tastes but he’s a likable lead actor.

Offering support is pop singer Beyoncé Knowles (Austin Powers III: Goldmember) as a church outcast with a killer voice. LaTanya Richardson (Lone Star) plays Paulina, an outspoken, opinionated harpy who has managed to shape the church and its choir in her rather unpleasant image. Steve Harvey (The Steve Harvey Show) has some nice moments as the town’s radio commentator.

What I find most unfortunate is that despite the fact that most of the film is set in a church and deals with a man’s redemption, the film just isn’t that spiritually edifying. The Christians depicted on screen are not “walking the talk.” It is not Christ or God which gets them in line... it is the music. And that in and of itself can send a wrong message.

Don’t get me wrong. Music can be a beautiful expression of love and worship. The music in The Fighting Temptations is glorious. It sets the spirit singing and the soul soaring to heights yet unexplored. Gospel music, when done well (as it is in this film) is able to reach down into and touch an emotional core that few art forms can emulate.

Yet, we should never so focus upon the tool that we forget the reason we are using it. Spiritual music is intended to glorify God, not the singers.

I will praise the name of God with a song, and will magnify him with thanksgiving. Psalms 69:30 (KJV)

The Fighting Temptations, for all its enthusiasm or zeal, doesn’t seem to aspire to magnify God above its own musical abilities.

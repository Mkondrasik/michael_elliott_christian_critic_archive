The Last Samurai

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 1, 2003
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Witness; example; discipline; spiritual influence; living faith

Author-verified transcription

The period of Japanese history known as the Meiji Restoration is a fascinating time of merging cultures and philosophies. In the late 1800s, after an extensive period of isolationism, Japan finally opened the door to Western influences and began to make steps towards "modernization." As The Last Samurai makes clear, the "modern" way is not necessarily always the "best" way.

Tom Cruise stars as Capt. Nathan Algren, a Civil War hero whose experiences in the Indian campaigns have left him scarred with doubt, nightmares, and disillusionment. It is in this state that he is hired to train the new Japanese army in the use and handling of American weaponry which the US hope to sell to them.

He soon learns that the first test of the emperor's new army will be against the emperor's old army, the warrior class known as samurai. They are led by the highly principled Katsumoto (Ken Watanabe).

The first clash between old and new ends in a rout as the skill and spirit of the samurai prove too powerful for the guns and cannons of the modern force. Algren is captured but his life is spared so Katsumoto might learn from this new and unfamiliar enemy.

As Algren lives among the samurai however, it is he who learns from them and the code by which they live - a code called Bushido (the way of the warrior).

In the samurai he rediscovers the qualities he once valued in himself: discipline, honor, loyalty, courage, and a sense of purpose.

Tom Cruise has turned in some fine performances in the past but this film seems to cater more to his movie star aura than to his acting ability. He's certainly serviceable in the role but doesn't seem to bring that many layers to it. Though it is a physical performance, it isn't all that memorable.

A much more interesting performance can be found in the work of Ken Watanabe who plays the samurai lord. Texturing his characterization with humor and moments of silent reflection, he conveys great strength and dignity as the leader of a way about to become obsolete.

Tony Goldwyn is cast as the "villain" of the film, an army officer with whom Algren served and a man who allows his racial prejudices to influence his actions. Japanese actress Koyuki plays the romantic lead, Taka, a woman who is asked to take care of Algren as he heals from his wounds even though she suffered a loss at his hands.

Director Edward Zwick uses a mighty large canvas upon which to paint his picture. However, despite its epic nature, the film is most successful with its intimate moments. I especially appreciated the way in which the relationship between Algren and Taka was allowed to grow naturally and respectfully. The battle scenes, though grand and nicely choreographed, don't deliver much of an emotional impact save the film's final skirmish.

As Algren walks around the samurai compound, he is not only impressed with their discipline, he is aware of a spirituality that emanates from them. He doesn't understand it but he does recognize that it exists.

As we go about our days, disciplined to the truths we've been taught by God, our lives will be a witness to His spirit and the spiritual power we manifest will be evidenced by those around us. They may not know what it is they sense, but they will know that there is something different about us and the way we live life.

Ye are our epistle written in our hearts, known and read of all men: Forasmuch as ye are manifestly declared to be the epistle of Christ ministered by us, written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart. 2 Corinthians 3:2-3 (KJV)

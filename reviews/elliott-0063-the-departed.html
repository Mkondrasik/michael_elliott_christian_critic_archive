The Departed

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
October 6, 2006
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Lies

Author-verified transcription

Martin Scorsese, long known as an “actor’s director,” has been nominated five times for an Oscar without once being called to the podium. His drought may be over with the release of this year’s The Departed.

The Departed is a Hollywood remake of a Chinese B-movie, Infernal Affairs, and tells the story of an undercover cop trying to infiltrate a mob syndicate operating in Boston even as the mob has placed their own secret informer in the midst of the police department.

Leonardo DiCaprio stars as police recruit Billy Costigan who is tapped by Captain Queenan (Martin Sheen) as a candidate to go deep undercover. He is “kicked out” of the academy and sent to prison to establish his cover. Upon his release, he must then must worm his way into the trust of gangster Frank Costello (Jack Nicholson) so he might gain the evidence to bring him to justice.

Unknown to Billy and Queenan, Costello has his own source inside the police station. Colin Sullivan (Matt Damon) is a fast-rising recruit with deep-rooted ties to Costello. As Costigan grows closer to Costello and Sullivan gets promoted to higher positions within the department, the stress of leading secret lives begins to show on both of them.

This is a very violent, often brutal film that blasts an A-list cast at the top of their game working for a director who is at home in this genre. Clocking in at two and a half hours, the time flies by as we are swept up in the story told and by the expertise of those who are telling it.

The biggest surprise in this cast of highly accomplished actors may be that Mark Wahlberg more than holds his own with heavyweights Matt Damon, Leonardo DiCaprio, Jack Nicholson, Alec Baldwin, and Martin Sheen. As the wise-cracking Sgt. Dignam, Wahlberg steals just about every scene he’s in. Not an easy task considering the talent pool in which he’s swimming.

DiCaprio continues to prove that he is the most serious-minded craftsman of his generation. His performance is electric and well deserved of the praise being heaped upon it.

Matt Damon has the second most visible role and, in many respects, it may be more difficult. Playing the other side of the same coin as DiCaprio, much of the time, Damon’s character must internalize his emotions. Playing a man pretending to be a wise guy allows DiCaprio a fair bit of range to emote, but Damon must play a man pretending to walk the straight and narrow. It’s a subtler performance and nonetheless worthy.

Jack Nicholson defies logic. By this time, with all his media exposure and the numerous mimicries he has generated, he should have devolved into a caricature of himself. But he remains the pure and quintessential Jack. As Costello, he plays a Fagin-like character who enlists the reverence of young boys knowing that they may serve him when they grow older.

Both DiCaprio and Damon (as well as other characters) live lives that are based upon lies. Those lies not only place their lives in danger, they manifest themselves in physical effects that are detrimental to the characters health. Costigan suffers from a sleep disorder and anxiety attacks. Sullivan suffers from sexual dysfunction and a general feeling of uneasiness.

Lies do not carry any promise. They may be a quick and dirty solution to an immediate problem but the solution never holds and the aftermath will be worse than owning up to the truth in the first place.

Behold, ye trust in lying words, that cannot profit. Jeremiah 7:8 (KJV)

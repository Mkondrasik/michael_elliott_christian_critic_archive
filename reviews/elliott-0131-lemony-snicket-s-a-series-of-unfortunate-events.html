Lemony Snicket's A Series of Unfortunate Events

3 stars

Sermon / spiritual topic: Adversity; temptation; deliverance; perseverance; trusting God

Author-verified transcription

In Lemony Snicket's A Series of Unfortunate Events, the Baudelaire children (soon to be forever known as the Baudelaire orphans) are having a run of Job-like misfortune. As the narrator (Jude Law) informs us at the beginning of the movie, this is not a story about happy little elves.

Neither is it all doom and gloom. With a dark but highly elaborate production design, a trio of photogenic children, and a manically inspired performance by Jim Carrey, the film is an enjoyable romp through the trials and adversities that the Baudelaires must learn to overcome.

After being informed that their parents perished in a mysterious fire that also destroyed their home, the children (the inventive 14-year-old Violet; voracious reader Klaus; and the toddler Sunny who communicates her snide and sometimes crudely humorous observations via subtitles) are carted off by a well-meaning but inept social worker (Timothy Spall) to live with Count Olaf (Jim Carrey), a distant relative.

Olaf, a ham actor, is far more interested in the inherited fortune that belongs to the children. He figures that, as the newly appointed guardian, he would be entitled to that fortune should anything fatal happen to his charges. The children manage to escape his early attempts to dispatch them and are bounced from foster home to foster home with a disguised Count Olaf never far behind them with a new scheme to separate them from their wealth.

The characters are the invention of David Handler who has written a series of books featuring the Baudelaires and their woeful plight. This film contains elements found in the first three books: The Bad Beginning, The Reptile Room, and The Wide Window and seems to do a decent job in translating for the screen the strange atmosphere established in Handler's clever writing... a combination of hopelessness and dread draped by a dark veil through which can be seen only the tiniest of silver linings.

Jim Carrey's exaggerated performance (at times he seems to be channeling Oliver Twist's Fagin) lightens the story considerably but also shows how much he has grown as an actor. There's a maturity to his work that is clearly evident. Who would have guessed that in scenes with multiple Academy Award winner Meryl Streep, who plays a daffy grammarian, it is Carrey who carries the scene and makes it work?

Billy Connelly also appears as another relative, a snake loving genial man with whom the children come to live for a brief time.

The kids are well cast, especially young Emily Browning as Violet who as the eldest orphan finds herself with adult responsibilities. All the children discover that they must rely only upon themselves in order to extract themselves from the dangers they face. Every adult they meet is either incompetent, cowardly, or evil.

It is the children's never-say-die attitude that is commendable. Even in the face of certain defeat, they look for the one opening that will provide them the escape they need. They have learned that one will always be there.

There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it. 1 Corinthians 10:13 (KJV)

The trick to facing adversity is to stay peaceful when faced with it. God promises us victory and success if we but stay faithful to his Word and claim the deliverance that he tells us is available.

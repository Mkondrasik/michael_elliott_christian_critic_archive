Mike Newell sits in the director's chair for the fourth magical installment in the story of a boy wizard and his friends. Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire ratchets up the danger, adds a bit of teenage angst, and introduces death to the series with the onscreen murder of a character.

In his fourth year at Hogwarts, Harry learns that his school is playing host to the prestigious Tri-wizard Tournament. Each of three institutions teaching witchcraft and wizardry to its young charges will select a student to vie for the eternal glory that comes with being a Tri-wizard champion.

Due to the life-threatening danger associated with the competition, only advanced students over the age of 17 may submit their names to the Goblet of Fire for consideration. It is, therefore, a big surprise to everyone when the goblet spits out 14-year-old's Harry's name as being a fourth entrant. The ruling of the enchanted goblet is absolute and so Harry, who had no desire to compete, is obligated to fulfill the magical contract.

It is just one more thing to add to Harry's concerns. There's also the signs of a returning Lord Voldemort; the fact that his best friend Ron Weasley (Rupert Grint) isn't speaking to him and his other best friend Hermione (Emma Watson) is dating one of the other competitors; and, worse of all, he has to summon up the courage to ask a girl to the Tri-wizard Ball.

The three children, who began as unknown actors in the first film, have grown into their roles nicely and are showing depths and levels that match their maturity. Radcliffe, Grint, and Watson all show that they have the talent that will take them beyond this franchise if the movie-going public can muster the ability to see them in any other light.

The other regulars take a bit of a back seat as guest artists take center stage. Brendan Gleeson, as Mad Eye Moody, makes the most of his scenery-chewing role and commands the most attention. Miranda Richardson makes an abbreviated appearance as gossip columnist Rita Skeeter.

An unrecognizable Ralph Fiennes is appropriately evil as the re-embodied Lord Voldemort and his arrival coincides with the franchise moving from PG to PG-13. I believe it to be a justifiable rating. There are some emotionally intense moments that parents should take into consideration before they decide to subject their child to them.

It is Voldemort that provides the information that leads to a spiritual lesson. He explains to his minions (and to Harry) exactly why the most powerful wizard in the entire world lost his powers to a mere boy. It was because the boy was protected by a defense greater than Voldemort's powers - a defense called love.

The sacrifice of Harry's mother, motivated by love, saved him just as the sacrifice of Jesus Christ, also motivated by love, saved us.

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. John 3:16 (KJV)

Our victory over our spiritual enemy was won through love. Love has always been the most powerful force in this or any world. It is the greatest motivator one could have and the greatest gift one could ever give or receive.

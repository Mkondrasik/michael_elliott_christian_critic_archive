Author-verified transcription

In Martian Child, sci-fi novelist David Gordon discovers an alien world that is much scarier than any that fills the pages of his best selling books. That world is found in the strange and continually surprising mind of a certain six year old boy named Dennis.

After his wife dies, David (John Cusack) entertains an idea that she was keen on pursuing - adopting a child. After much soul-searching and many self-doubts, he takes the plunge. He decides to adopt a boy who spends most of his time inside a cardboard box.

Dennis (Bobby Coleman) thinks he is from Mars. He believes that he was left here to observe and document the human experience which, thus far, consists of all his parental figures disappearing from view.

David, showing a unique kinship to this odd but lovable child, opens his home and heart to Dennis. Distrustful of all humans, the boy is slow to respond in kind. After all, he is quick to explain... he won't be here long. When his mission is over, he'll be called back to the mothership. David, like all parents, must struggle between giving the child what he knows he needs while, at the same time, giving him the space to discover who he is.

Helping David is Harlee (Amanda Peet), a woman whose long term friendship shows the kindling of potential romance. Those who stand in David's way are a social worker named Lefkowitz (Richard Schiff) who isn't quite sure anyone can help Dennis; David's own agent (Oliver Platt), who would rather see David busily writing to meet his rapidly approaching deadline rather than taking the time to raise a troubled child; and David's sister (Joan Cusack) who sees nothing but heartache and headache ahead.

John Cusack may not have quite reached the stratosphere of stardom but he sure presents likable characters. He sells this film on his personality alone and the humor, pathos, drama, and emotional payoff are solely due to the investment he makes in his character.

Bobby Coleman apparently has taken a page out of the child acting handbook studied by Haley Joel Osment and delivers much of his dialogue in a strained whisper that somehow adds credulity to his role. Right or wrong - the technique works. Together Cusack and Coleman build a dynamic relationship that not only holds the film together it makes it immensely enjoyable.

Director Menno Meyjes understands where the strengths of his film are found and he makes sure to give those strengths the room they need to compensate for the film's weaknesses. The result is a sweet, heartwarming tale of two people wanting to love but needing to trust that they can.

If this isn't a universal problem, I don't know one. And in this world, there isn't a guaranteed solution. We can offer love and be rejected. But that doesn't mean we have to close off to the world and escape into a fantasy life.

A fertile imagination, properly harnessed, can be a wonderful asset or trait to possess. But it is never an adequate substitute for reality. Even in this story, the obstacle preventing David and Dennis from connecting and forming a real bond is the fact that Dennis is clinging to a fantasy born of the emotional hurt he has suffered in his young life.

God is not interested in building fantasies. He is truth. He is light. He is love. And He extends these qualities to us so we might embrace them and, so empowered, embrace each other. His love shields us from the disappointment that sometimes comes from human love. His truth protects us from the lies that so often are told. His light continues to shine even through the darkness that this world contains.

In other words, no fantasy will ever bring us what we so deeply desire... love.

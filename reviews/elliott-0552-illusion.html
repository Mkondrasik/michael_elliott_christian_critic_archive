Author-verified transcription

The connection between film and life is one that has long been explored by Movie Parables. So it is with a certain amount of built in appreciation that I approach Illusion, a film by Michael A. Goorjian that is based on the 17th century play L’Illusion Comique by Pierre Corneille.

In this updated version, Donald Baines (Kirk Douglas), a celebrated film director of romantic pictures is dying of cancer after a long and heralded life. He is visited on his deathbed by his old editor Stan (Ron Marasco) who died many decades earlier. Stan transports his old friend (bed and all) to a movie theater where he gives him the opportunity to watch selected reels of a unique film – the life’s footage of Christopher (Michael A. Goorjian), the son that Baines rejected many years ago.

The product of a one night stand between Baines and a film extra, Christopher has never had the parental guidance a young man needs. Baines watches each reel with growing despair as he sees his stranger of a son make mistake after mistake.

Central to each reel is the unrequited love Christopher feels towards Isabel (Karen Tucker). We see him drawn to her as an awkward high school outsider; as a goth publicist for a bizarre performance artist; and as an ex-con looking to get his life back on track.

Every time it looks like there’s a chance that they might get together, circumstances force them apart.

Baines watches it all, regretting the decision he made not to have a relationship with the boy and wondering if it might not be too late to repent. Writer/Director (and costar) Michael A. Goorjian builds upon this interesting premise and delivers half of an intriguing and entertaining movie.

We’re really watching two stories intertwined. First, the story of Donald Baines and his growing awareness of mistakes made; and second, the story of Christopher Baines and how his life has progressed without a father figure.

Goorjian scores big by having film legend Kirk Douglas lend tremendous credibility to the part of the elder Baines. Even when hampered by the physical aftermath of a stroke, Douglas’ performance carries with it a dignity and strength that communicates. Douglas is supported by a sweet and tender portrayal by Ron Marasco as a gentle editor who deftly leads his friend through this other worldly event.

Taken as a whole, the film has a strong central premise, a few strong moments, but too many weakly drawn or executed scenes that are meant to tie things together in a nice neat package. But it delivers its message in an entertaining fashion and holds our interest until the end. It is well worth a look.

As the cliché goes, hindsight is always 20-20. Each decision we make carries with it consequences that may not be realized for years to come. That does not negate the effect of those consequences nor the regrets that accompany them. The joys of fatherhood far outweigh the burdens of its responsibilities. It’s a lesson that Baines learns far too late in life. We need not be on our deathbeds to realize it.

Children’s children are the crown of old men; and the glory of children are their fathers. Proverbs 17:6 (KJV)

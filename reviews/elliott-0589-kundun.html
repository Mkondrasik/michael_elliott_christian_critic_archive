When judged from a purely visual standpoint, Kundun, the new film by Martin Scorsese (Raging Bull), is a fine piece of movie making. Unlike 7 Days In Tibet, which dealt with the same subject matter, Mr. Scorsese allows no familiar faces or Hollywood names to serve as a distraction to this story of the 14th Dalai Lama who was forced to flee his native Tibet in order to escape Communist China and its advancing forces.

The title refers to the Tibetan name given to the first Dalai Lama. He is believed by his people to be the reincarnation of their Lord Buddha. Unfortunately, Dalai Lamas do not live forever. Therefore, whenever the current Dalai Lama dies, another reincarnation is needed. The movie begins in 1933, shortly after the 13th Dalai Lama has died. A search for his reincarnated successor is already underway. It has taken many years but finally the search has come to a successful end in a tiny village near the Chinese border. And so, a 6 year old boy, now recognized as the reincarnated 14th Dalai Lama is taken to “his” palace where he will be trained in the Buddhist traditions and philosophies. As he matures he will eventually assume the responsibility of being the spiritual leader of many within the Buddhist religion.

Time is not on the side of this Dalai Lama. The world is at war and Communist China is encroaching upon Tibetan territory. As this boy enters into his early teen-aged years, he is called on to lead his people through the most treacherous time in their long history.

As stated, Mr. Scorsese did not “go Hollywood” with this film. Using local talent, he was able to manufacture and maintain an air of authenticity and believability which serves the story admirably. The music score by composer Philip Glass does much to capture the sense of the time and place of an exotic culture such as Tibet in the mid 1900’s.

However, while being able to capture the “feel” of a moment in history is certainly an accomplishment, it is simply not enough to carry a full length motion picture. As visually attractive as Kundun may be, it does tend to drag on. Forty minutes into the film, you may well be looking for the exits. After an hour and a half, you may have involuntarily stopped looking... but don’t worry, the management will be sure to wake you up when the movie ends.

Viewing Kundun from a Christian perspective could be seen by some as an exercise in futility. After all, there are no Christian characters nor is there any Christian doctrine being espoused. The movie is about and populated solely by those who practice Buddhism. Still, we must remember that God has given us his Word which allows us to discern all things. We have the ability to strengthen our spiritual walk as we bring everything back to His Word. Our adversary is a parasite. He uses elements of truth in all of his false doctrines. We only need to separate the lies from the truth.

For instance, watching the way that the elders of the council painstakingly teach this child who would one day be their leader can bring to mind the record in Deuteronomy 6:9 that admonishes parents to continually teach their children the truths of God. Proverbs 22:6 [KJV] reads:

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it.

As parents, we have the responsibility to build in our children the habits of mental discipline that they will utilize for their lifetime, just as the Buddhist elders did for the young Dalai Lama.

Secondly, since so much of Buddhism is based upon the concept of reincarnation (a concept which is certainly featured in this film), we, as Christians, may well be prompted to revisit certain areas of the Scriptures which deal with the subject of life after death and the future resurrection of the just and unjust. We know that the devil’s first lie recorded in Genesis was:

Ye shall not surely die. [Genesis 3:4 KJV]

What is reincarnation but a doctrinal reinforcement of that initial devilish falsehood? Scriptures such as can be found in Ecclesiastes 9, 1 Thessalonians 4, and 1 Corinthians 15 will help strengthen our minds on the truth of God regarding these subjects when confronted with the devilish logic of reincarnation.

Near the end of the film, a man asks the young spiritual leader a direct question, “Are you the Lord Buddha?” He responds, “I think I am a reflection... like the moon on water.” Does that response hold any less true for the practicing Christian? Should not our very lives be a reflection of the life of Jesus Christ? As people view us, should they not see Christ? When we walk as he walked, and think with his mind, and speak the words he spoke; as we obey God’s will as Jesus Christ always did, do we not reflect everything that Christ was and is?

The one difference may be this: The reflection of a moon on water is an illusion. It contains no substance. How comforting it is to know that there is no illusion regarding the gift of Christ in us. It is real. It is substantial. And it is full of the potential power of God.

Being John Malkovich

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 7, 1999
Rating
2 stars

1 hr: 53 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Self-worth; identity; individuality; body of Christ

Author-verified transcription

"Never judge a man until you walk a mile in his shoes." So says the old adage. Now, with Being John Malkovich, we have a movie that brings us not just the shoes, but the entire man himself. Within its premise, for fifteen minutes at a time, we can catch a glimpse what it is like to be inside the mind of John Malkovich.

Being John Malkovich is a unique cinematic experience. Fancifully bizarre, sexually confusing, surprisingly droll, and wonderfully imaginative, it is dizzying in the way it departs from what might be classified conventional or mainstream.

John Cusack (Pushing Tin) is puppeteer Craig Schwartz, eking out a living with his street performances and generosity of his working wife, Lotte (Cameron Diaz, A Life Less Ordinary). Desperate for a change, he takes a job as a file clerk at LesterCorp which is oddly located on the 7½ floor of a Manhattan office building. The ceilings are approximately 5 ft high, a fact made painfully clear as we see the employees stooping, crouching, squatting and more as they traverse the halls.

There he meets Maxine (Catherine Keener, 8MM) and after a rebuffed flirtation, develops an all-consuming obsession. She simply won't have anything to do with him. That is, until he discovers a hidden portal which leads to the mind of actor John Malkovich, allowing whoever enters the unusual sensation of seeing the world through the famous thespian's eyes.

Soon, Maxine and Craig are partners, selling 15 minute rides with the pitch, "Have you ever wanted to be someone else?" After 15 minutes, the Malkovich rider is somehow unceremoniously dumped alongside the New Jersey Turnpike.

Of course, "being" Mr. Malkovich, even if just for a short while, has certain unexpected side effects. Once Lotte goes through the portal, she becomes convinced that she's much more comfortable in a man's body than her own and soon is competing with her husband for the affections of Maxine.

The appeal of this movie is its obvious audacity. It is so unconventional, so original in execution, all attempts to second guess are futile so we have no choice but to let the film take us for its unusual ride.

John Malkovich (Con Air) is the ultimate good sport, allowing the filmmakers to use both his name and persona, and then lending his talented abilities to the process. A quirky performer in his own right, Mr. Malkovich was the perfect subject for this bizarre tale.

John Cusack deadpans his way through much of the inexplicable events to great comedic effect. In fact, much of the humor comes from the characters' unquestioning acceptance to circumstances which are more than a little "out there."

To my eyes, the one negative which could not be overlooked was the gender-bending sexual subplot that is woven throughout two-thirds of the film. I found it to be quite intrusive, actually diminishing the entertainment value of the film... at least for this observer.

Director Spike Jonze and screenwriter Charlie Kaufman are both making their feature film debut and by the looks of this film, their future is quite a promising one. Plans are already being made for a second collaboration.

Being John Malkovich paints an unsavory picture regarding the sorry state of most people's self-image. The film leaves us with the impression that the majority of men (and women), if given the opportunity, would prefer to be anybody other than who they are.

We, as Christians, do not share this misguided fantasy. We have the awareness that God created each of us as unique individuals with special qualities and abilities. He has specifically called us to the body of Christ where we have the privilege to function. To deny our own self worth is to deny the God Who created us.

For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works. Ephesians 2:10 (KJV)

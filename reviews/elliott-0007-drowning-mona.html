Drowning Mona

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 3, 2000
Rating
1 star

Author-verified transcription

Everyone in the small town of Verplanck, NY drives a Yugo. Mona Dearly (Bette Midler, Isn't She Great) just drove one of them into the Hudson River and drowned. Thus begins the quirky but unfunny dark comedy, DROWNING MONA.

The townspeople receive the news of Mona's demise with various degrees of indifference, relief, and joy. She was not a well-loved individual, for reasons seen in the many flashbacks which are sprinkled throughout the film. Mona was a hateful, humorless, overbearing and abusive woman. And those were her good points.

When Lucinda, the town mechanic, discovers that Mona's Yugo was "fixed to kill," Police Chief Wyatt Rash (Danny DeVito, Matilda) finds himself with a mystery to be solved and a town filled with suspects.

The finger of guilt points quickly to Bobby Calzone (Casey Affleck, 200 Cigarettes), landscaper and fiancé to Wyatt's daughter Ellen (Neve Campbell, Three to Tango). His landscape company is being run into the ground because of his imbecilic partner, Mona's son, the one- handed Jeff Dearly (Marcus Thomas, Hearts and Bones), whose missing appendage is the subject of gruesome speculation.

Phil Dearly (William Fichtner, Go), the browbeaten and abused husband of Mona, is not mourning her early departure. He's been having an affair with Rona Mace (Jamie Lee Curtis, Halloween H20), the waitress who is a regular at the nearby motel, dividing her time between Phil and his son, Jeff.

As a mystery, DROWNING MONA disappoints on many levels. As a comedy, it disappoints on many more. Chief Rash's investigation seems to consist of driving around town asking people "Did you do it?" Apparently, once he asks the right person, the case is solved.

The filmmakers have assembled a fine and talented cast who have developed fully realized (though intellectually challenged) characters. Mr. Devito serves as the anchor of normality in a sea of trailer trash goofiness. He is the Andy Taylor character in a town populated by demented Barney Fife and Otis clones.

Ms. Midler portrays a woman so vile that the only person who seems to care about her death is Cubby, the local undertaker (Will Ferrell, Superstar). This may sound like a backhanded compliment, but she is very believable as the character.

If there is a weak link in the acting category, it would belong to Mr. Affleck in the pivotal role of Bobby. His character isn't given the same depth or richness as those with whom he shares the screen. As a result, he comes across as rather bland and uninteresting.

The major flaw of the film lies in the script by Peter Steinfeld (Echo). It strives to be an edgy comedy in the manner of There's Something About Mary, but it lacks the sharpness. The Yugo idea is a case in point. It is a joke which falls flat, but nevertheless runs throughout the film.

There is little evidence that these characters have any kind of spiritual awareness, although Ellen, at one point asks Bobby if he heard "that thing that goes off in the back of your head that says, 'Bad (expletive deleted) idea?'"

We who try to live according to God's standards must train ourselves to listen for "that thing."

And he said, Go forth, and stand upon the mount before the LORD. And, behold, the LORD passed by, and a great and strong wind rent the mountains, and brake in pieces the rocks before the LORD; but the LORD was not in the wind: and after the wind an earthquake; but the LORD was not in the earthquake: And after the earthquake a fire; but the LORD was not in the fire: and after the fire a still small voice. 1 Kings 19:11-12 (KJV)

The voice of God is heard not from a loudspeaker or as booming from the heavens above, but rather as a still small voice that speaks personally and directly to us at times when we most need guidance. It's a voice more people should try to hear.

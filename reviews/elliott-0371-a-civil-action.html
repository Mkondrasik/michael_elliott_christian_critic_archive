A Civil Action

Author-verified transcription

For some strange reason, the marketing brains who are "selling" the public on the movie A Civil Action, are painting it as a typical suspense-filled courtroom drama. It is not.

Instead, it a movie with an intelligent script based upon a wonderful book by Jonathan Harr filled with real characters portrayed by consummate actors.

Based upon a true story, A Civil Action is set in Woburn, Massachusetts, a small town whose water supply has been contaminated by industrial pollutants resulting in an unusually high number of unexplained leukemia deaths. Jan Schlichtmann (John Travolta, Primary Colors) and his small three-attorney practice is hired by the eight families whose children have died.

Their task: to find the parties responsible and uncover what happened. When the finger of evidence points to the huge corporate entities of Beatrice Foods and WR Grace, the case suddenly takes on David vs. Goliath proportions. Pitted against opponents with tremendous resources, Schlichtmann and his partners are asked to risk everything in order to bring this case to court. Mr. Travolta continues with his recent run of steady and thoughtful performances as he portrays Schlichtmann as a stereotypical money-hungry personal injury attorney who, incredulously, grows a conscience as he begins listening to his clients. His transition from a "bottom-feeder" to a man driven to do the right thing is on the mark.

We're running out of accolades for Robert Duvall (The Apostle) who plays Jerome Facher, the lead attorney for Beatrice Foods. His work, again, is flawless. Mr. Duvall has an uncanny ability to always find the humor or lightness in even the most humorless characters.

William H. Macy (Pleasantville) will give his career a boost by his amusing performance as James Gordon, the financially-minded partner of Jan Schlichtmann.

Kathleen Quinlan (Breakdown) brings a quiet strength to the character of Anne Anderson, a woman who after losing her son to leukemia, leads the charge for justice. Her controlled and emotionless portrayal only serves to remind us of the tremendous emotions that must lie beneath her cold exterior.

Writer/Director Steven Zaillian (Searching For Bobby Fisher) should be congratulated for presenting the story without resorting to the typical Hollywood courtroom histrionics. This depiction of the law in practice is probably closer to the truth than what we've seen in other movies that have taken the more populist road of emphasizing Perry Mason-like theatrics. The downside is that, like the legal profession itself, the movie can become mired down in the detail of presenting its case. Still, even without the heart-racing excitement and lack of dramatic tension there is much that this movie has to offer.

Mr. Duvall's character has a line which has been predominantly featured in the movie's teasers. In response to Jan Schlichtmann's cry for the truth to be heard, he said, "Truth? I thought we were talking about a court of law." Damning words directed to a litigious society.

Laws were not written for the righteous. They were written for those who continually choose to operate outside of the Truth.

Knowing this, that the law is not made for a righteous man, but for the lawless and disobedient, for the ungodly and for sinners, for unholy and profane, for murderers of fathers and murderers of mothers, for manslayers, For whoremongers, for them that defile themselves with mankind, for menstealers, for liars, for perjured persons, and if there be any other thing that is contrary to sound doctrine; 1 Timothy 1:9-10

God's primary will was not to give His people a series of laws which carried with them legal consequences.

Man lived for centuries before God gave His people a legal standard to follow. But because man was unable to live consistently according to God's Truth, laws were established.

The legal action depicted in this movie could have been avoided completely if all parties involved would simply have adhered to the truth. Perhaps that is too high an ideal for man to achieve. But then again... perhaps it isn't. Wouldn't it be something if more of us would try?

The Three Burials of Melquiades Estrada

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 24, 2006
Rating
1½ stars
Running time
2 hr: 1 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

2 Corinthians 7:9-10Ezekiel 18:27Romans 6:4

Sermon / spiritual topic: Repentance

Author-verified transcription

Tommy Lee Jones splits his time between opposite sides of the camera as he balances both acting and directing responsibilities in the strange modern day western/morality tale, The Three Burials of Melquiades Estrada.

Jones plays Pete Perkins, a laconic rancher living and working north of the Rio Grande where Mexicans stream across the border almost as freely as the water. One of them, the title character (played by Julio Cedillo), is hired by Jones and becomes his best friend. When Melquiades is killed by a trigger-happy border patrolman named Mike Norton (Barry Pepper); and the local sheriff (Dwight Yoakam) takes no action to deliver justice, Pete decides to take the law into his own hands.

Fulfilling a promise he once made, Pete kidnaps the patrolman, exhumes his friend's body and the three of them begin a tortuous trek to Mexico so Mel might be buried in the village where his family still lives. Why Pete forces Mike along is a question that is never fully answered and many have speculated on the significance of the film's closing moments.

Director Jones has said that the theme of alienation runs throughout the piece, alluding that all characters experience it in one fashion or another. I don't know about that but I can say that, as an audience member, I certainly felt alienated. This film simply did not connect with me on any level.

None of the characters were particularly likable. Melquiades came the closest I suppose, but he was dead for most of the film and it was rather hard to watch his decomposing body (excessively shown to us in uncomfortable detail).

Pepper painted his character as a brutal and cowardly man, quick to use his position of authority to demean or physically abuse those weaker than he. January Jones plays his wife, a bored blonde looking for some outlet to spice up a tedious existence. She looks to floozy waitress Rachel (Melissa Leo) as her mentor. Rachel passes her days by having sex with most of the male supporting characters, including the sheriff and Pete.

The script was written by Guillermo Arriaga (21 Grams, Amores Perros) and is planted firmly in the dark side of human existence. There's very little redemptive light shining from any of these characters making for a dismal and ultimately disappointing film. The possibility of redemption is, of course, always present but the ambiguous choices that were made by Arriaga and Jones kept me from embracing the film's possibilities. For me, this was an opportunity lost.

There are some that liken the tale to a Christian parable. Mike killed Mel and, over time (forced to wear his clothes, drink from his cup, and rebuild his house), came to regret what he did and asked for forgiveness. The comparison is a bit of a stretch and gives too much credence to a transformation by Mike that, to me, rang false.

Now I rejoice, not that ye were made sorry, but that ye sorrowed to repentance: for ye were made sorry after a godly manner, that ye might receive damage by us in nothing. For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death. 2 Corinthians 7:9-10 (KJV)

Redemption comes with true repentance. Repentance is often preceded by godly sorrow which is the awareness of the consequences of one's wrongful actions that leads to a willingness to change. It is the willingness to change (repent) that I missed from Mike's character. It can't be forced upon another simply by pointing a gun at him.

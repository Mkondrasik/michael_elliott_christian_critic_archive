Author-verified transcription

“Winning isn’t everything... it’s the only thing” is a well known sports cliché that has been attributed to just about every successful coach who ever drew breath. It is a philosophy at the heart of We Are Marshall which is based upon the true events of a tragic story. Except this time, the phrase takes on a different meaning.

In 1970, a chartered airplane crash took the lives of the Marshall University football team, its coaches and staff, along with many of its boosters and fans. The only ones spared were the few that for one reason or another did not make that tragic road trip.

It was a devastating loss for a small West Virginian town and many of them could not stomach the idea of trying to field another team the following season due to the memories it would stir up. But there were some who could not stomach the idea of letting their deceased teammates down by giving up on the program that meant so much to them.

Coach Jack Lengyel (Matthew McConaughey) arrives to Huntington WV as an outsider to accept the head coaching position of Marshall which, due to the tragedy, has a grand total of 3 returning players. His job was to rebuild a team. He soon discovers that his mission went a lot further than that. He needed to rebuild a town - or at least give it back its heart.

McConaughey gives a brave and valiant effort as the unconventional and idiosyncratic Lengyel. It’s a performance that, at first, may seem a bit off-putting but the actor’s consistency and enthusiasm for the role is infectious.

Matthew Fox as the team’s assistant coach - one who survived the crash by giving up his seat at the last moment - would be the second lead but there simply not enough of his character mapped out to make him as substantial as we might like. So it is with the rest of the cast.

David Strathairn, as the mousey university president who finds a backbone; Ian McShane, as a grieving father of a potential all star who can’t bear to see another season without his son; Kate Mara, as a young woman whose future crashed along with the plane... all these characters have stories to tell but screenwriter Jamie Linden simply didn’t put enough dramatic meat on the bones that history provided.

Director McG isn’t showy with the camera and he is respectful of the real tragedy that forms the basis of his film. As a whole, the film isn’t as successful as it might have been but it is effective in parts and well worth a look.

“Winning is the only thing” is a statement that certainly seems to embody the culture in which we live. And it does have significance in this film. However, the key question remains... what is “winning?” The victory that the Young Thundering Herd of Marshall claimed was not to be found on the scoreboard. It was to be found in the courageous hearts of those who survived the tragedy and yet chose to continue living despite the pain and suffering the tragedy brought upon them.

They were victors not because they won every battle they fought but rather because they continued to fight every battle and by doing so eventually won the war.

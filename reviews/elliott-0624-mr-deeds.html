Author-verified transcription

It takes a great deal of nerve to choose to remake a classic film which has been enjoyed by millions over a number of decades. Actor Adam Sandler (Big Daddy) and his cinematic partners, director Steven Brill (Little Nicky) and screenwriter Tim Herlihy (Happy Gilmore), certainly have plenty of nerve. They just apparently don't have the ability to produce something that matches the quality of the original.

Mr. Deeds is an unapologetic knockoff of Frank Capra’s superb 1941 comedy, Mr. Deeds Goes To Town, which starred Gary Cooper as a small town poet who inherits a fortune. In the 2002 version, Cooper’s simple elegance is replaced by Adam Sandler’s “common man” shtick.

As played by Sandler, Longfellow Deeds of Mandrake Falls, NH is a pizza parlor owner who yearns to write greeting card poetry. Upon finding out that he's the sole heir to a forty billion dollar inheritance, he steadfastly holds to his simple, understated ways even as he is whisked to NY in a private Sikorsky helicopter.

In NY, he seems almost oblivious to the massive public interest in him. Thus, when he meets a young damsel in distress, it never occurs to him that she might be a sleazy tabloid reporter (Winona Ryder, Girl, Interrupted) looking for a scoop.

What with his business partners looking to oust him from the company, the media setting him up to look like a fool, and the girl he loves becoming a great disappointment, it looks like Longfellow might just call it quits, give up the money and go home where life is a whole lot simpler.

Simple is the best descriptive for this disappointing piece of fluff. Even better, it is simply simple-minded. Apparently nothing stands in the way of reaching for a laugh. Certainly not logic, physics, or character development.

One of the reasons that the film doesn’t work, even by Sandler standards, is the casting. Adam Sandler has built his career playing goofy guys with a heavy sarcastic and sardonic sense of humor. As Longfellow Deeds, Sandler does not play to his comedic strengths. He is muzzled and his performance turns bland.

Winona Ryder plays Babe Bennett as a wide-eyed wannabee instead of a hard core tabloid news journalist which weakens her character arc. Peter Gallagher (American Beauty) is appropriately oily as the head corporate executive who is set at odds against Deeds. Allen Covert (The Waterboy), one of Sandler’s long time collaborators, is embarrassing as an oddly cartoonish investigative reporter who works with Babe.

John Turturro (O Brother Where Art Thou?), as Deeds' faithful and sneakily efficient butler Emilio, comes the closest to breathing some life into this tired and ill-conceived project, but even he seems somewhat hampered by the lack of intelligence within the script.

The strength of the story (which is badly told in this version) lies in the character of Longfellow Deeds. Deeds is a man of conviction and high moral character who is able to hold onto his integrity in the midst of a crooked and corrupt society. For Deeds, money is not a goal to be desired or the evidence of a life well lived. It is merely a tool by which one may help others. Having such an attitude is both healthy and biblical.

Command those who are rich in this present world not to be arrogant nor to put their hope in wealth, which is so uncertain, but to put their hope in God, who richly provides us with everything for our enjoyment. Command them to do good, to be rich in good deeds, and to be generous and willing to share. 1 Timothy 6:17-18 (NIV)

This world can use more men like Deeds. What it doesn’t need are more movies like this one.

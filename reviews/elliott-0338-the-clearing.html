The Clearing

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 16, 2004
Rating
3 stars
Running time
1 hr: 31 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Envy; comparison; forgiveness; prosperity

Author-verified transcription

The makers of most suspenseful thrillers are used to gauging their success on how well they can keep the audience on the edge of their seats. With The Clearing, director Pieter Jan Brugge seems more intent on making people think and reflect upon their lives and relationships than in providing the thrills and chills normally associated with the genre.

The single act which drives this film is a kidnapping. But instead of a pulse-throbbing action picture about the crime and rescue, we are given relatively quiet scenes as the three main characters get introspective about life and their respective places in the world.

The victim is Wayne Hayes (Robert Redford), a wealthy businessman who made his money the old fashioned way - by working hard, building a business from scratch, and selling it for a fortune. But his success has come at a price he is still calculating. His relationships with his wife and his children have not had the priority that they should.

The kidnapper is Arnold Mack (Willem Dafoe) whose life could be considered a mirror opposite of Wayne's. Like Wayne, he worked hard and contributed, in his way, to the success of a company. But when the company chose to downsize, Arnold became a workplace casualty. His American dream became a nightmare from which he could not awake.

For the victim's wife, Eileen Hayes (Helen Mirren), the kidnapping is a severe wake up call that exposes just how many loose ends she has allowed to exist in her life. As she welcomes the FBI into her home, they begin delving into the past and the unresolved problems of her marriage.

The film has an odd structure which takes a while to notice. Brugge and screenwriter Justin Haythe cleverly manipulate time to serve the story they wish to tell. Actually, there are two stories really being told. The first involves Eileen and the family who, with the FBI, are trying to cope and make the decisions that could mean life or death for Wayne. At the same time, Wayne engages Arnold in an ongoing conversation as they travel on foot through a heavily wooded area to a predetermined location. Wayne finds that his negotiating and people management skills have never been more important.

The three central characters are managed by a trio of accomplished actors. Mirren is a master of understatement and control. She lets her silences speak for her and they communicate vividly. Redford portrays Wayne as a man who has reached the top and, in looking down, finds that he has shed too much that was important to him during his ascent. Dafoe brings a remarkable multilayered complexity to Arnold that makes him unusually sympathetic.

Each of the three characters is flawed in their own ways. Wayne is painted as a remarkable man but is shown to have a skeleton in his closet that has affected his marriage - in large part because his wife, Eileen, refuses to honestly address it. Because she has simply buried the matter, she doesn't allow the healing and restoration that would come with forgiveness.

Arnold's initial sin is one of envy. He had followed Wayne's career and successes even as he was suffering through his own failures. By comparing his life to Wayne's, he became discontent and despondent. The kidnapping was not an act of desperation. It was a deliberate action that Arnold rationalized out of the envy he felt and the disappointment he experienced.

One of the easiest ways to make yourself miserable is to compare your life to the life of someone else. It is also a way to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory. There is nothing good that comes from making comparisons with a judgmental eye. It will either lead to pride (which, according to Proverbs 16:18, "goes before destruction") or envy (which, according to James 3:16, accompanies "confusion and every evil work.")

A far better use of a man's time would be to keep his focus on his own life. Working hard, living within one's means, and giving with a cheerful heart are the keys to having prosperity. It is how Wayne built his fortune. If only he had applied the biblical principles to other aspects of his life.

Save The Last Dance

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 6, 2001
Rating
2 stars

1 hr: 52 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Equality; Race; Love

Author-verified transcription

Save the Last Dance tries hard to please on a number of levels... as a treatise on the social stigma attached to interracial relationships which continues to exist in our day... as a contrasting study of two different cultures existing side by side... and as an inspirational message which preaches that one should never give up on one's dreams. It is therefore disappointing that its story is so contrived that each level is trivialized and the film ultimately devolves into a rather average tale of love, friendship, and dance.

Sara (Julia Stiles, Ten Things I Hate About You), a white bread Illinois suburbanite, on the day of her audition for admission to the Julliard School of Ballet, is told that her mother was killed in a car wreck as she was rushing to be at Sara's audition. Wracked with grief and guilt, Sara hangs up her toe shoes and moves into a drab apartment in Chicago's South Side with her estranged jazz musician father (Terry Kinney, Oz), who is little more than a stranger to her.

Not only is she now the new kid in school, she is one of the few white kids. Her elite ballet training did little to prepare her for her introduction to the hip hop culture in which she is now immersed. Chenille (Kerry Washington, Our Song) and her brother Derek (Sean Patrick Thomas, The District), begin Sara's instruction in how to assimilate a head-popping, streetwise attitude into her Brady Bunch persona. Not surprisingly, this instruction takes place largely on the dance floor as she learns how to "bust a move" with the same efficacy as she performs a grand jeté.

As Derek teaches her the hip hop moves, he also rekindles the dream of Julliard within her.

When the friendship between Sara and Derek begins to deepen, those around them become increasingly uncomfortable with the time Derek is spending away from the old gang, especially since drive-by shootings have become part of their daily existence. They need every trustworthy, able-bodied brother to back them up in their show of force.

Both Sara and Derek must come to terms with their own wants and desires as well as the pressures and expectations which are pressed upon them by their friends and family.

Julia Stiles portrays her fish out of water character amusingly. Her initial dance moves to the hip hop beat are awkward and laughable, as is the street language which she occasionally tries to adopt. Her Julliard audition piece is well executed and choreographed but doesn't match the quality of the dancing that was seen in last year's Center Stage.

Sean Patrick Thomas does a fine job as Derek. His intensity and focus communicates the character's drive and ambition and he portrays the internal conflicts well.

However, the chemistry between the two simply doesn't work. The relationship just doesn't throw off the sparks as it should and it is this lack of magnetism between the characters which ultimately causes the film to lose its hold on its audience. We also don't see enough opposition to their relationship to justify Sara's comment, "We spend more time defending our relationship than actually having one." Showing more of that conflict may have made this film a more interesting one.

As intelligent a people as we can be, that we still judge, evaluate, and classify people according to color, is pretty stupid of us. The only color that should matter to us is red... the color of blood.

And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation; Acts 17:26 (KJV)

God has made us all and we are all equal in his eyes. The standards by which He will judge us will be far more substantial than the color of our skin or the accent with which we speak. When Jesus was asked which commandment was greatest, he responded with two: Love God and love your neighbor as yourself. There is no provisional loophole that negates this truth if your neighbor happens to be of a different shade.

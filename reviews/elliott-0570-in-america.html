Author-verified transcription

Director Jim Sheridan grafts a piece of his heart and puts it on celluloid in the very personal, very intimate, and quite magnificent In America.

Written by Sheridan and his daughters Naomi and Kirsten, it traces the lives of an Irish immigrant family looking to start their lives anew in the promised land of America. They carry with them some emotional baggage, having lost a son to a brain tumor earlier. It is a reality which they keep trying to push aside but are quite unable to.

Don’t get the wrong idea. This is not a depressing film. Not by a long shot. It is told primarily through the eyes of the two young daughters, Christy and Ariel (brilliantly played by the real life sisters Sarah and Emma Bolger). When young, all of life seems to be an exciting adventure. Seeing the story played through the eyes of the children softens to some degree the emotional turmoil of the story, replacing it with the childlike wonder of discovery.

Johnny (Paddy Considine) is an unemployed actor who is hampered by the fact that he has buried his emotions deep within himself following the accidental death of his son Frankie.

His wife, Sarah (Samantha Morton), has done a better job of disguising her grief for the sake of their remaining children but there are times when the facade breaks down.

It is the children, Christy (Sarah Bolger) and Ariel (Emma Bolger), who continue to inject life into the family. Christy documents all of life into her ever present video camcorder while Ariel simply relies on her outgoing personality. Soon they know everyone in their rundown neighborhood and most importantly, everyone knows them.

An unexpected “addition” to their family comes when Mateo (Djimon Hounsou), a black-skinned neighbor who is known for his screaming fits in the middle of the night, responds to the girls’ openness and offer of friendship. We soon learn the reason for his screaming and why Johnny’s family has touched him so deeply.

The specific incidents that occur in the film seem slight when described: Johnny gets an air conditioner for the apartment; Johnny tries to win an ET doll at a carnival booth; Christy and Ariel go to a Halloween party in homemade costumes… Each of these events, though they may seem uneventful, are full of passion, substance, and dramatic heft… not surprisingly, just like life itself.

While the performances of the cast are uniformly excellent, we must mention again just how wonderful the two Bolger sisters are. As natural as nature can be, they carry the picture upon their tiny shoulders and lift it to us with all the innocence that their tender ages possess. Heartbreaking and heartwarming all at the same time. It is not an easy feat… though they make it look as if it were.

The principal obstacle in the film is Johnny’s and Sarah’s half-buried grief of losing their son (a grief which is shared by Christy and Ariel). The death of a loved one is a devastating event. It doesn’t matter how religious we are, or how much we believe. Death stings. We who remain must deal with the aftermath as best we can. We can gain comfort from the scriptures and edification from other believers. Ultimately however, it must be our own decision to move on with the life remaining in us.

Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus. Philippians 3:13-14 (KJV)

The Ladykillers

Michael Elliott

March 26, 2004
Rating
3 stars

Original Author Copy

Author-verified transcription

Sometimes, the greatest criminal minds don't have a lick of sense between them. The proof of this can be seen on display in the Coen Brothers' new dark and dimwitted comedy, The Ladykillers.

Based on the English film of 1955 that carried the same title, it also carries essentially the same story. A group of criminals, handpicked for their specialized skills by their erudite and eccentric leader (Tom Hanks), meet at the home of an elderly black woman (Irma P. Hall) from whom they are renting space for their music rehearsals.

That is a ruse, of course. With concert music quietly playing from a boom box, the "musicians" are in actuality digging their way to the counting room of a casino riverboat. Once completed, they plan to take the money, cover their tracks, and run with no one being the wiser. But what kind of movie would it be if things went according to plan?

After a few setbacks, they do manage to abscond with the money but before they can make good their escape, the strong-willed, churchgoing lady whose home and hospitality they grace, finds them out and insists they return the money like good boys should.

The crooks have no choice but to eliminate this sweet but not so defenseless old woman. They will soon find that bumping her off will not be an easy task as, one by one, their attempts backfire.

It has been a while since Tom Hanks returned to his broad comedic roots and he's chosen a great role to trumpet his return. Professor Goldthwait Higginson Dorr, PhD, is a flowery talker, a master of dead languages, an appreciative fan of the poetry of Mr. Edgar Allen Poe, and about a tenth as smart as he thinks he is. Hanks throws himself into the part and milks the comedic potential with true expertise.

As his primary foil, Irma P. Hall plays Mrs. Munson as a strongly opinionated, devoutly religious, and deeply principled old woman who doesn't understand much of what Dorr may be saying but sure does love the way that man can talk on. Hall and Hanks play off of one another beautifully.

The rest of Dorr's band of miscreants include Marlon Wayans as their lazy, foul-talking man inside the casino; J.K. Simmons as a demolition expert with irritable bowel syndrome and an uninvited girlfriend who happens to be tagging along; Tzi Ma as a chain-smoking, tunnel-digging General; and Ryan Hurst as Lump, arguably the dumbest among the dumb who is there only for the brute strength he can add to the project. Each character has their moment in the sun which, of course casts them in the worst possible light. The actors seem to relish the opportunity to dumb down because they do it so well.

There are any number of moral lessons that we can glean from this film: Crime does not pay. Actions have consequences. There is no honor among thieves... But perhaps the most obvious lesson for us can be summed up by a quote that is borrowed from a previous Tom Hanks film. As Forrest Gump so eloquently put it, "Stupid is as stupid does."

It is as fitting a description of this film as I've yet to hear. The masterminds of this caper are not deserving of the high-minded description given them. They are fools of the highest sort and, in this particular film, we are rewarded by seeing that they will receive a fool's end.

Yea also, when he that is a fool walketh by the way, his wisdom faileth him, and he saith to every one that he is a fool. Ecclesiastes 10:3 (KJV)

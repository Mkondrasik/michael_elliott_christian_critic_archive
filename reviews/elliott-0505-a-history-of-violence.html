Author-verified transcription

Is it possible for a leopard to change its spots? More importantly, can a man change his nature? It is the central question at the heart of A History of Violence, David Cronenberg's character study of one man wrestling with his past.

By all counts, Tom Stall (Viggo Mortensen) is a good man. Devoted husband and father; hardworking owner of a small town diner; decent citizen and friend to the community of Millbrook Indiana. When a pair of violent drifters enter his diner and threaten to murder all who are there, this mild-mannered man takes action, killing both intruders. His heroism makes national news and unfortunately places him in the gunsights of some undesirable characters.

Carl Fogerty (Ed Harris), a scarred mobster from Philadelphia, arrives in Millbrook to confront Tom with the accusation that he is, in reality, Joey Cusack, hardened killer and brother of a Philly crime boss.

Much of what follows is murky. Are Fogerty's claims true? If not, how was Tom capable of such violent action? How can his family handle the contradictions in the man they thought they knew?

Director David Cronenberg asks us these questions and more but leaves most of the answers for us to consider long after the film departs the screen.

Viggo Mortensen turns in a very subtle performance that keeps the audience guessing up to the grand revelation. Maria Bello plays his wife who is slowly coming to the realization that there's more to the man she married than she either knew or feared.

Ed Harris is menacing as the one-eyed thug looking to settle a long standing score and William Hurt in a few brief scenes establishes his talent once more with an engrossing portrayal of a brotherly mobster.

Cronenberg builds the suspense and doesn't hide the violence - especially the images after the bloodletting is done. The purpose is to unsettle us; to keep us wondering about the nature of man and what makes him capable of such ugly acts. Is such violence within each of us? Locked away but accessible when the situation presents itself? After such violence, is it possible to return to a peaceful or tranquil state?

It is questions such as these that keeps A History of Violence on one's mind long after the film has ended.

Of course, the question as to whether a man can change his nature has long been answered by the authority on such matters. God has made it clear that not only can we change - it is eminently important that we do.

For you have put off the old man, as regards the former behavior, having been corrupted according to the deceitful lusts, and to be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and to put on the new man, which according to God was created in righteousness and true holiness. Ephesians 4:22-24 (KJV)

To answer the initial question posed in this review, it is possible for a leopard to change his spots (figuratively speaking) just so long as the leopard makes the commitment to change its ways and adopt new ones.

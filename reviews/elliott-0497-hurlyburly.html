Author-verified transcription

According to Webster's New College Dictionary, "hurly-burly" means a tumultuous uproar. Welcome to life in Hollywood.

That's the setting of Hurlyburly, an amoral film based on the amoral play by David Rabe who adapted his own work for the screen. As with the play, the movie centers around the perverse lives and actions of two Hollywood producer-types and the people that flow in and out of their universe like so much sewer waste.

Here's the relationship rundown: Eddie loves Darlene, who likes Eddie but finds herself attracted to Mickey. Mickey, though he is Eddie's partner, roommate, and friend, has a fling with Darlene but afterwards, flings her back to Eddie in order to enjoy the underaged company of Donna, a runaway child given to Eddie and Mickey as a "sexual care package."

Meanwhile, Phil, whose friendship with Eddie is based on the fact that they have absolutely nothing in common, is stressing out over his continual marital problems. Eddie fixes him up with Bonnie, a balloon carrying stripper whose main talent has nothing to do with dance. All these characters interact with each other as they ingest obscene amounts of drugs (AKA "Bolivian health food") into their system while trying to figure out the mysteries of life and love and friendship and blah, blah, blah..

That "blah, blah, blah" is actually part of the dialogue. It would seem that in Hollywood, the emphasis is not what you speak. It is that you speak. The quiet man in the corner never lands the movie deal. So, in Hurlyburly, everyone talks quickly without saying much of anything. Substance is but a dim and almost forgotten memory to these shallow characters. Then again, reality is kind of blurry to them as well.

One of the most frustrating occurrences I find in being a reviewer is coming across a film like Hurlyburly. It contains brilliant performances, a script infused with crackling dialogue and wit, assured direction that ties all the disparate elements together resulting in an end product that is a polished and professional piece of artistry. And yet, the story it tells is so reprehensible and spiritually offensive that I cannot in good conscience recommend it.

The movie "belongs" to Sean Penn (The Thin Red Line) who plays Eddie. His tour-de-force portrayal of a downward spiraling, drug-addicted, paranoid and delusional Hollywood scumbag is amazingly detailed. It is a finely crafted introspective study of a thoroughly despicable character.

His performance is almost matched by the wryly amusing work of Kevin Spacey (The Negotiator) who, as the more controlled Mickey, will undoubtedly be overshadowed by Mr. Penn's flashier role. The irony is that the even-tempered and flippant Mickey has probably less of a moral code than Eddie. The reason he can maintain his balance in the midst of such ugliness is quite simple.. He doesn't care.

That kind of unbelievable self-absorption permeates every frame of this movie. These characters' only thoughts are of self-preservation and self-gratification. Such self-centeredness leads to a perception of life that is skewed and ultimately unprofitable. There's only one source for true answers - anything else is an ego-driven deception.

For I would not, brethren, that ye should be ignorant of this mystery, lest ye should be wise in your own conceits; that blindness in part is happened to Israel, until the fulness of the Gentiles be come in. Romans 11:25 (KJV)

Until we learn to adapt our will and desires to the revealed truth which is the Word and will of God, the answers to life's questions will always elude our grasp. As such, life becomes nothing more than a hurly-burly... a tumultuous uproar. As Shakespeare put it, "Full of sound and fury, signifying nothing." Come to think of it, that's an appropriate description for this movie.

Judas

Author-verified transcription

The controversy surrounding Mel Gibson’s The Passion of the Christ has not yet begun to wane and yet another version of the story is about to hit the airwaves.

The main difference is that this TV movie looks at the life and times of Jesus Christ from a different perspective - through the eyes of the one who betrayed him, Judas Iscariot.

As a political “rabble-rouser,” Judas attaches himself to Jesus because he believes that Jesus may be the long awaited Messiah who will become the military leader of God’s chosen people and free them from Roman oppression. Unfortunately, the longer Judas stays and hears the teachings of Jesus, the more disgruntled and disappointed he becomes with his message of love and compassion.

Johnathon Schaech (That Thing You Do!) stars as the title character, portraying Judas as a politically minded individual with a conflicted soul. So anxious is he to “rally the troops” around Jesus that he is completely deaf and blind to the truth that his Messiah came for a different purpose.

Jonathan Scarfe plays Jesus a bit too wimpishly for my tastes. The script has him apologizing for losing his temper when overturning the tables of the moneychangers in the temple. We should remember that Jesus did have great love and compassion, but he also demonstrated righteous anger that needs no apology.

While it is unfair to compare this TV movie to the far superior and more expensive production now in theaters, it is difficult not to. As a result this film comes across as being a well-intentioned but not very polished version of the story of Christ.

Most interesting is how the political environment is depicted with the religious leaders and the Roman authorities trying to outmaneuver each other in how they deal with this Nazarene who is turning their worlds upside down.

It should be said that this version does play loose and fast with the truth, including a great deal of unsubstantiated speculation - particularly in scenes between Judas and Jesus. The film ends with three of Christ’s apostles (James, Peter, and Andrew) going to the body of Judas who had hanged himself in despair over his friend’s crucifixion. They pray for his soul because “it is what Jesus would have wanted.”

This is a great sentiment and in keeping with scriptural truth. Judas was forgiven and it was only his own grief and despair which kept him from receiving the fullness of the holy spirit. What sins do we have that are so heinous that they cannot be forgiven of God if He was willing to grant forgiveness to the man who betrayed His only begotten son?

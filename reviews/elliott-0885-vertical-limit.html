Author-verified transcription

If we could ask a room full of mountain climbers to explain why they do what they do, we would more than likely receive as many different answers as climbers asked. This is because a mountain represents different things to different people. So it is with the characters of Vertical Limit.

To Peter Garrett (Chris O'Donnell, The Bachelor), the mountain is a bringer of death, most notably his father's. To his sister Annie (Robin Tunney, End of Days), it is a reminder of her father's soul and passion for life and a way to remain close to him. To billionaire Elliott Vaughn (Bill Paxton, U-571), it is simply another obstacle which must fall before his indomitable will. To the reclusive mountain man Montgomery Witt (Scott Glenn, The Virgin Suicides), it is an unmarked tomb, containing the remains of his late wife.

After a poorly executed summit assault on K-2, the world's second tallest mountain, Annie, Vaughn and Tom McLaren (Nicholas Lea, TV's Bull) become trapped at 26,000 feet. It is up to Peter to organize a rescue team and engage in a race against time to save his sister and fellow climbers before they succumb to the elements. Joining him are a pair of adventurous Australian brothers (Ben Medelsohn, Sample People and Steve LeMarquand, Two Hands), a courageous Muslim porter (Alexander Siddig, Star Trek: Deep Space Nine), a beautiful French-Canadian medic (Izabella Scorupco, Golden Eye) and Witt, who brings to the mountain his own private agenda. Plus, they carry three vials of unstable and highly explosive nitroglycerine.

Filmed primarily in New Zealand, (on Mt. Cook), the film does not disappoint audiences seeking an adrenaline rush. There are any number of ways a mountain can kill a man and Vertical Limit illustrates many of them.

The downside is that the rather predictable script by Robert King (Red Corner) and Terry Hayes (Payback) is not without its flaws. But director Martin Campbell's (Mask of Zorro) able execution and some solid performances by the lead actors help to minimize those flaws and keep Vertical Limit from being buried under an avalanche of film clichés. The end result is a suspenseful albeit unchallenging adventure tale. In between crises, it also somehow manages to raise a few moral questions regarding survival and sacrifice which might lead to some interesting after movie discussions.

No one in the cast gives what is often called a breakout performance but all provide adequate portrayals of their one-dimensional characters. By doing so, the cast takes an appropriate back-seat to the stunt crews as the action sequences drive this particular film.

The moral question of survival at any price is raised in the film and illustrated by the actions and decisions of Elliott Vaughn. They are contrasted by the more sympathetic character of Annie. We see, rather obviously, that the decisions made by each character are influenced by the way they figuratively view the mountain.

Biblically, mountains are also often used in a figurative way to symbolically represent different things.

In certain verses, they represent the strength and stability of God's household of believers.

They that trust in the LORD shall be as mount Zion, which cannot be removed, but abideth for ever. As the mountains are round about Jerusalem, so the LORD is round about his people from henceforth even for ever. Psalms 125:1-2 (KJV)

Other times it is used to represent the devil spirit realm controlled by the "god of this world." In such instances, we are comforted to remember that through God, our believing can actually move and remove such mountains (Matthew 17:20) With God, there is no such thing as a Vertical Limit. Indeed, there are no limits, for with God all things are possible.

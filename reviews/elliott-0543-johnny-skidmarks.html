Author-verified transcription

"I just take the pictures." That is the mantra of Johnny Scardino AKA Johnny Skidmarks. His nickname is derived from his profession as a crime/accident scene photographer for the police. Most of those who work with him are amazed that he isn't affected by the ugliness and tragedy of the scenes he is called upon to photograph. His secret? He just doesn't take notice of them. Point the camera, frame the scene, shoot and move on. Just take the pictures, don't think about what they mean.

His detached manner is a little eerie, and ultimately dangerous. He feels no moral compunction as he moonlights for a pair of blackmailers, taking pictures of influential men in compromising situations. Just take the pictures - don't think about the men whose lives may be ruined because of those pictures. After all, it's nothing personal.

When his blackmailing buddies start turning up dead, Johnny is forced to consider that one of the unwilling victims of his lens has taken it personally... And he's fighting back.

Peter Gallagher (While You Were Sleeping) plays Johnny and does as well with the part as could be expected. A person who detaches himself from the experiences of life is boring. Not until the experiences begin to force their way into the character's awareness does he begin to show signs of life.

Frances McDormand (Fargo) is Alice, a mysterious woman who appears on the scene just as things start to go wrong for Johnny. While there isn't much for Miss McDormand to do, she does it quite well.

John Lithgow (3rd Rock From the Sun) is on hand adding another off-beat characterization to his resume. This time he's Sgt. Larry Slovik who, taking Johnny under his wing, investigates the seemingly unconnected murders.

Director John Raffo (Big and Mean) clearly has an eye for the unusual. The first five minutes of the film look like a version of "Hairstyles of the Weird and Aimless". Bad comb-overs, pompadours, ill-fitting toupees... These characters are definitely not charter members of The Hair Club for Men.

Mr. Raffo carries that skewed vision into the script and onto the screen. The crime scenes are strangely grisly and bloodless at the same time. The methods of death are gruesome but the visual depiction of those deaths are as emotionless as Johnny's pictures.

Watching the film develop (and the body count rise), we notice that we've become as detached as Johnny Skidmarks. We're just watching the picture. We're not really involved.

That can be a real danger in life (as Johnny discovers). At what point are we to draw the line? What should be the standard that distinguishes between what we will accept in our mind and what we won't accept? All of us need to guard against the "desensitizing" nature of our spiritual enemy. He will continue to pick away at our mind, bombarding us with stimuli, tempting us to ignore or forget the truths of God. The only way to defeat the relentless, daily assault upon our senses is to continually place the Word of God in our mind to act as a filter to all that we see, smell, taste, touch, and hear.

God has given us the ability to discern good from evil. That ability is only as developed as the Word of God that we know. The less we know and are able to practically use, the more susceptible we are to the spiritual blindness in which the devil would have us immersed. God would have us approach and live life with our spiritual eyes wide open and flooded with light. Don't just take pictures. Live each and every moment God has given us to its fullest potential.

Alfie

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 5, 2004
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Ecclesiastes 12:13Proverbs 19:23Galatians 5:16-17

Translation: Amplified
Sermon / spiritual topic: Purpose; meaning of life; fear of God; responsibility; fulfillment

Author-verified transcription

Living a shallow life can be deceiving. A person can be easily fooled into thinking he's having a ball. It's not hard to understand - he's having all the fun and shouldering none of the responsibility. But he's also not seeing the big picture. It is an immature way to approach life and it is in this stage that we find the central character of Alfie.

Based upon the stage play by Bill Naughton and the subsequent 1966 movie starring Michael Caine, Alfie tells the story of a man/boy whose sole purpose in life is to bed as many women as might be humanly possible.

In this, he has lots of experience. Alfie (Jude Law), who addresses the audience directly by speaking into the camera, shamelessly informs us as to his philosophy on sex, life, sex, women, and sex. Needless to say, that philosophy is not very deep.

With his self-serving attitude the limo driving Alfie experiences a series of superficial and ultimately unsatisfying encounters: Dorie (Jane Krakowski) is married and hopelessly committed to the sex she's getting from the charming cad. She barely makes it through the opening credits. Single mom Julie (Marisa Tomei) is his "semi-regular" girl, good for a place to stay and a warm snuggle when in a pinch but comes with too much baggage for Alfie's comfort. Lonette (Nia Long) is the sexy girlfriend of his best friend (Omar Epps) which is a fact that only gives him a moment's pause.

Nikki (Sienna Miller) gives the impression of being the perfect girl up until he gets involved with her. Her manic depressive behavior will eventually sour that relationship. Liz (Susan Sarandon) may be the perfect match for Alfie seeing that she is as superficial as he. She turns out to be even more superficial than he which gives Alfie a bit of a wake-up call.

Jude Law actually does a fine job in a rather thankless task. The character he's playing is a bit of an anachronism. Such a person, despicable as he might be, could be somewhat explained during the sexually experimental 60s. He's less understandable today. Instead of portraying him as a heartless cad, Law instead paints him as a man conflicted by his inability to commit to just one woman. This softens Alfie a bit by showing us that he's on a path of self-discovery thus attempting to increase his appeal for today's audiences.

By softening this character, however, the filmmaker has managed to weaken his movie. If Alfie is indeed questioning himself and his behavior, why doesn't he clue in to the fact that he's on such a dead-end path? Instead of seeing him as a selfish, insensitive, or immoral we simply view him as being rather foolish.

The film closes with a question which echoes the original film - "What's it all about?" By the time the closing credits arrive, Alfie has come to the realization that his life amounts to absolutely nothing. For all the girls that have come in and out of his life (and bed), he's left alone and adrift with an empty and meaningless existence.

"What's it all about?" is a question that every man and woman eventually asks. It is the question that is at the heart of Ecclesiastes. There the writer sums up his discovery in the final verses. Should we live by these words, we are sure to find the peace and satisfaction that all men seek.

"All has been heard: the end of the matter is: Fear God [revere and worship him, knowing that he is] and keep his commandments, for this is the whole of man [the full, original purpose of his creation, the object of God's providence, the root of character, the foundation of all happiness, the adjustment to all inharmonious circumstances and conditions under the sun] and the whole [duty] for every man." Ecclesiastes 12:13 (Amplified)

Author-verified transcription

In The Matador, a chance meeting between two strangers in a Mexican bar leads to an odd couple relationship between a straight-as-an-arrow married businessman and an emotionally damaged contract killer in need of a friend.

Danny Wright (Greg Kinnear) is in Mexico trying to land a business deal that will help establish his company. While he awaits the outcome of his pitch, he strikes up a conversation with a drunken Julian Noble (Pierce Brosnan), a self-described "facilitator of fatalities" who has just finished his latest assignment.

Noble, drawn perhaps to Wright's naïveté or goodness, invites him to a bullfight where he confides in him by telling him what he does for a living. Instead of drawing back in fear or repulsion, Wright's curiosity and interest is peaked. They spend the rest of the day together, with Noble showing an increasingly fascinated Wright some of the tricks and methodology he uses when on a job.

Months later, Noble shows up on Wright's doorstep with a problem. His years as an assassin have taken their toll. He's emotionally burnt out and unable to perform. Those he works for are tired of being disappointed by him and will kill him if he fails one more time. With no one else to turn to, Noble comes to Wright to help him carry out his duties.

This is something of a departure for Pierce Brosnan and shows that he has a range that the James Bond/Remington Steele personas never explored. As Julian Noble, he's a loner lacking in the fine points of social decorum. Rude and/or inappropriate comments spill from him automatically and yet he allows for a certain vulnerability to lurk just beneath the coarse exterior.

Greg Kinnear makes for a perfect counterbalance. As Danny Wright, he's the epitome of the everyman - a decent family guy who is trying to do the right thing but is plagued with self-doubt and financial concerns. Together, the contrasting characters of Brosnan and Kinnear compliment one another and they play them to great effect.

Unfortunately, the film, written and directed by Richard Shepard, seems too infatuated with Noble's seedy life and keeps itself firmly planted there. A wiser choice may have been to stay on the fence separating Noble's life from Wright's and spending equal time on either side so we might see more clearly why these two individuals were drawn to each other.

By favoring the "dark side" Shepard allows his film to become implausible. He crosses a line with his characters that is difficult for the audience to accept because he hasn't laid a sufficient foundation to make the choices those characters make believable.

Noble is a sad and rather pathetic character. Alone in the world and on a downward spiral, he has no one to pull him out of his troubles and no one in whom he can solicit advice which is why he cleaved onto Wright so desperately.

God made man with a need for companionship. As he was creating his good and perfect world in Genesis, it was the only "critical" comment towards his creation that He had recorded.

And the LORD God said, "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him." Genesis 2:18 (KJV)

God corrected what was lacking by giving Adam someone with whom to share his life. None of us need go through this world alone. Spouses, friends and companions will add strength and stability to our days and can help keep us on the right path.

We Were Soldiers

2 hr: 18 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Leadership

Author-verified transcription

As the oft disputed saying goes, “There are no atheists in foxholes.” Watching the intensely real battle scenes of We Were Soldiers, the latest in a string of recent films set against the backdrop of war, we can see why the phrase was coined. This incredibly honest and graphically violent film about a nearly forgotten Vietnam battle brings us as close to hellish conditions as we should ever want to get. Asking God for deliverance from such a place is not only reasonable, it often seemed to be the only option at hand.

Based on the book by Lt. Gen. Hal Moore (Ret.) and war correspondent Joseph L. Galloway, the film tells the story of the first major armed conflagration between American soldiers and the People’s Army of North Vietnam. Perhaps the story smacks of realism because both Moore and Galloway personally survived the battle and were able to record their experiences first hand using their uniquely spiritual perspectives.

Moore (Mel Gibson, The Patriot), devout Roman Catholic, devoted family man, and at the time a Lt. Colonel, was chosen to command a new brand of fighting soldiers, specifically formed to counter the challenging Vietnamese terrain. The “Air Calvary,” as they were dubbed, would use helicopters to drop in and out of “hot zones.” On Sunday, November 14th, 1965, Landing Zone X-Ray in the Ia Drang Valley was “hotter” than anyone expected. Moore and 400 of his men dropped into a zone and were quickly surrounded by approximately 2000 enemy soldiers. What ensued was as savage a battle as America has ever known. This film is a testament to the memory of the soldiers, on both sides, who were there.

In We Were Soldiers, director Randall Wallace (Braveheart) may very well have made the best Vietnam film to date. In addition to the realistic depiction of war, he gives us clearly defined and compassionate characters, a look at the trying emotional times endured by the wives back home, and a hint at the poor decisions being made by American leaders unprepared for the war in which their country was now engaged.

Most importantly, Wallace presents the enemy as something more than faceless, heartless enigmas. He shows them as they were... soldiers, doing a job away from their wives and families just like the Americans. This humanization of the enemy gives the film a resonant depth that many war pictures lack.

Gibson, who again turns in a topnotch performance as Moore, is ably supported by a cast of actors all of whom deserve mention. Madeleine Stowe (Short Cuts) as Moore’s wife, Sam Elliott (The Contender) as a hard as nails First Sergeant, Greg Kinnear (Nurse Betty) as a cocky helicopter pilot, Chris Klein (Rollerball) as a new platoon leader, Keri Russell (Felicity) as his wife, and Barry Pepper (61) as photo journalist Joe Galloway. Kudos to all for their detailed and emotionally rich performances.

Of the many illustrations from which to draw, one of the most poignant for me occurred when high command, assessing that the situation of the Air Calvary forces was hopeless, wanted to airlift Moore from the scene and transport him to a safe area. Moore repeatedly refused these direct orders knowing that to obey them would mean abandoning his men who trusted him to lead them.

The faithfulness, courage, and commitment to his men which Moore displayed is the mark of a true leader. In the church, we too have such a leader who will guide us through the spiritual battlefield. It is someone who has done it all and asks us now to follow him, not out of fear or coercion but rather out of love. We do so because we have the confidence that he will always lead us to victory.

For even hereunto were ye called: because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps. 1 Peter 2:21 (KJV)

A leader will always put those who follow him above his own needs and concerns. Lt. Col. Moore exhibited that Christ-like quality which resulted in many of his men being led to safety.

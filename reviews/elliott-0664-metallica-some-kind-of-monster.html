Author-verified transcription

A behind-the-scenes look at a rock and roll band at the end of their career and in the midst of self-destructing sounds familiar, doesn't it? But unlike This is Spinal Tap, the brilliant and fictitious mockumentary which comically depicted such an event, Metallica: Some Kind of Monster is for real.

The film takes place in the period between 2001 and 2003, and grants us backstage passes to the internal demons, ego clashes and artistic differences that plague the rock group Metallica as they attempt to record an album (St. Anger) for the first time in five years. Directors Joe Berlinger and Bruce Sinofsky get more than they bargained for as the producing of this album turns into the possible dissolution of what is arguably called the greatest heavy metal band of all time.

With relations between members at an all time low, James Hetfield, Lars Ulrich, and Kirk Hammett struggle to hold onto the group dynamic that made them multi-millionaires. It won't be easy, especially after bass player Jason Newsted announces his decision to leave the band.

The remaining three members decide to sequester themselves in a makeshift studio at the California military base called The Presidio. Arriving without a note of music or any lyrics having been written, their plan is to jam their way back into form. They'll worry about finding a bass replacement later. For the time being, they simply get producer Bob Rock to fill in.

To further aid them in their quest, they have hired Phil Towle, a group therapist, whom they hope will help them to get to the root of their personal problems. The film splits its time between the jam sessions and the therapy meetings. It will prove to be a circuitous and rocky road indeed.

The tension between Hetfield and Ulrich is immediately evident and further exasperated by Hetfield's checking himself into a rehab center due to his addictions and substance dependencies. His absence throws the entire project into a tailspin from which the group has doubts it can recover.

When Hetfield eventually returns, he brings with him a long list of conditions, among them - he will only work from 12 noon to 4pm and no one else can work on the music while he's not there. In the most honest exchange that was captured on film, these limitations bring drummer Lars Ulrich to the breaking point. "This is a rock and roll band. I don't want *****ing rules," he candidly blurts out.

While the film does present some interesting perspectives on Metallica's difficulties during this time, most of the film is spent with the group being just a tad too self-conscious of the camera's presence. We get the feeling that they are censoring themselves and framing their responses for the film instead of honestly expressing their thoughts and feelings. Occasionally, as in the example above, there is a moment of genuineness that breaks through but there aren't enough of them to sustain the film.

While I'm not a fan of heavy metal music nor of the lifestyle publicly promoted by those associated with the genre, this film is not really about sex, drugs, and rock and roll. It's more about family, therapy, and business royalties. It is also about the chaos and confusion that ensues when a group loses its direction and two leaders emerge with different ideas and plans as to which direction to take.

Scriptures tell us a double minded man is unstable in all his ways. (James 1:8) The same is true of any group of people who cannot work together to reach a common goal.

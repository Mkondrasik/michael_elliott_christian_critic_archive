Author-verified transcription

Pop culture icon Andy Warhol once "prophesied" that everyone would be famous for fifteen minutes. Without debating the accuracy of his statement, I wonder as to the desirability of it. Fame, after all, is a status that is often overrated.

Even so, fame is at the heart of Fifteen Minutes, though I must say that its heart beats somewhat erratically. The film's much too obvious point that today's media, and thus our entire culture, is too celebrity conscious is beaten into our collective skulls with the subtlety of a jack hammer.

Two Eastern Europeans arrive in America and are passed through immigration. One (Karel Roden, Time of Servants) has a quick temper, high ambitions, and no compunction towards killing other human beings. The other (Oleg Taktarov, Absolute Force) fancies himself a director and, after stealing a digital camcorder, begins making his "movie" which consists of recording the heinous crimes against humanity that his "partner" commits. Together, they figure to claim their twisted version of the "American Dream." By feigning insanity, thus escaping prosecution, they plan to make millions by selling the film and book rights to their story.

Rising up to challenge them is an odd couple of sorts. Celebrity cop Eddie Flemming (Robert De Niro, Meet The Parents) and Jody Warsaw (Edward Burns, The Brothers McMullen), a fire marshal with a boy scout's naiveté, implausibly join forces to track and stop the killers/film producers.

On the sidelines, and oftentimes thrusting himself into the game, is Robert Hawkins (Kelsey Grammer, Frasier), host of a tabloid news show called "Top Story." His morbid news philosophy is "If it bleeds, it leads." He willingly aids the killers in their pursuit for their promised "fifteen minutes" of infamy in exchange for the film footage which he believes will increase his show's ratings.

Robert De Niro is perfectly cast as Eddie Flemming, the high-profile cop who has become the media darling of the force. De Niro plays the grizzled veteran with a soft vulnerability that makes him a very likable character. Czechoslovakian actor Karel Roden and Siberian actor Oleg Taktarov combine to make a believable pair of sociopathic "sickos" whose twisted interpretation of values reflect society's growing tolerance of and fascination with extreme behavior.

The difficulty with the movie lies not in the talented cast, all of whom provide decent performances, but rather in the script which places their characters into implausible situations. Writer/director John Herzfeld is intent on making his point about the media's unparalleled influence upon our culture but lacks the subtlety to draw us into the story while letting his message play out.

The film contains scenes of extreme violence and profanity, which along with occurrences of nudity and sexuality merit the R rating advisory.

A poignant observation comes from the East European visitor who establishes his opinion of American values by watching daytime trash tabloid television. "I love America," he proclaims after a particularly seedy encounter. "No one is responsible for what they do."

That is certainly not the image we wish to convey as a nation, but it is easy to see how the rest of the world might arrive at that conclusion. Let us not be deceived however. It may be possible to pervert justice in this world via the manipulation of unscrupulous men, but such practice will only delay the inevitable. All of us, without exception, will be held accountable for all of our actions when we are called to stand before the highest judge of all.

"So then every one of us shall give account of himself to God." Romans 14:12 (KJV)

At such time, all men shall be viewed equally according to a rock solid and universally applied standard. Celebrity status will become as meaningless as. well, as a celebrity whose fifteen minutes is up.

Author-verified transcription

There's an exclamation point predominantly featured in the title of Saved! It is a misleading punctuation. This film would be more accurately titled by using a question mark because it would have us believe, ironically, that the born again Christian characters of Saved! are the very ones from whom we need salvation. They are presented as being self-righteous, self-delusional hypocrites.

Jena Malone stars as Mary, a senior at American Eagle Christian High School, who claims to have been "born again her whole life." Her daily existence is filled with righteous God-loving causes, righteous God-loving friends, and righteous God-loving phrases which help her to state her faith using convenient soundbite-sized clichés.

Her faith is so strong that when her righteous God-loving boyfriend admits to her that he thinks he might be gay, she believes that Jesus would want her to save him from this "spiritually toxic affliction" by giving up her virginity. When her "sacrifice" doesn't have the desired effect - he's shipped off to a special halfway house for de-gayification and she becomes pregnant - Mary suffers a crisis of faith. To make matters worse, her righteous God-loving friends turn on her and deliver as unloving a judgment as any heathen could muster; forcing her to seek solace from the more understanding and accepting outcasts of the school, the token Jewish atheist (Eva Amurri) and a wheelchair-bound skeptic (Macaulay Culkin) who are offered as the film's most sympathetic characters.

According to the production notes, director and co-writer Brian Dannelly's religious upbringing included going to a Catholic elementary school, a Christian high school, and a Jewish summer camp. He apparently walked away from these experiences with the impression that no one group had a lock on the truth even though they all act like they do.

The script has an obvious axe to grind regarding institutional Christianity and the actors are hamstrung into stereotypical behavior as a result. Mandy Moore plays Hillary Faye, the school's biggest cheerleader for the Lord and someone who seems destined to become the next big-haired televangelist on cable TV. With a holier-than-thou attitude and a mind narrowed by pride and smugness, she represents what happens when love is removed from religion.

The adults don't fare much better. Martin Donovan, as principal Pastor Skip, spends so much time trying to relate to the kids by his hip phrases and rap references ("Let's get our Christ on!" and "It's time to get down with G-O-D!") that he fails to connect with the truth of what these kids are going through. Likewise, Mary-Louise Parker, as Mary's mom, is so infatuated with Pastor Skip that she is blind to her daughter's condition.

I would be hypocritical myself to say that hypocrisy does not exist in the church. It does and it is fair game for satire and sarcasm. But Dannelly paints such a one-sided picture that his points, even if valid, lose their emphasis. There is no Christian character that comes off looking good in this picture unless they disassociate themselves from the tenets of Christianity.

The overall message that he seems to want to leave his audience is that we should be tolerant of all things... except intolerance. Mary ends the film with the observation that no one really knows "what would Jesus do?" and so all we can do is the best we can while we figure it out together.

The problem with that sentiment is that it does not allow for the possibility that there is a known standard of truth available to man; a truth that enables us to discern between right and wrong. Such a standard does exist and can serve as a guide and a help to all who believe it. Those who choose to use it as a weapon to demean or belittle those who don't believe it are wrong and misguided. Our Lord attracted his followers through love and forgiveness... not through threats of ostracism and hatred. But he never once deviated from following the standard of truth given to him by God his father. And neither should we.

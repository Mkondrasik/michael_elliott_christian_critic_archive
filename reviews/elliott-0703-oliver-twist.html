Author-verified transcription

Expecting a twist from a classic story? Don't expect it from Oliver Twist. Director Roman Polanski follows up his Academy Award winning Holocaust movie, The Pianist, with a by-the-book faithful adaptation of Dickens' woeful tale of a mistreated orphan in nineteenth century London.

Parentless Oliver (Barney Clark), after having the audacity to ask for more gruel from the heartless caretakers of the orphanage, is delivered into the hands of a coffin maker to learn a trade. Mistreated there, he runs away to London where he is "befriended" by a gang of under-aged pickpockets led by the nefarious Fagin (Ben Kingsley) who takes the young tyke under his soiled wing.

As Oliver begins to learn this new trade, circumstances intervene and he finds himself experiencing a new reality - mainly that the haves live better than the have nots. Taken in by a gentleman (Edward Hardwicke) who sees the goodness in him, Oliver has the opportunity of a lifetime... if only Fagin and the rest of his indigent "family" would leave him alone.

The film could be an excellent resource for lazy English students looking for a quick way to "get" Dickens' story without actually having to read the book. However, Polanski is so focused on the detailed minutia of art design and production qualities (with the Czech Republic adequately filling in for the London streets of the 1800s) that he fails on a dramatic level. The movie simply plays flat.

A large part of the problem lies with the central character, that is to say the young man who plays the central character. Though he may be photogenic, his on camera skills are simply not sufficient to do more than convey the bare bones of the story narrative.

The rest of the cast offer solid enough support but don't leave us with much of a reason to remember them. Ben Kingsley provides the fullest characterization but in his hands Fagin becomes a pathetic, miserable creature. He can't serve as the film's heart or soul and so the film goes without.

Jamie Foreman's Bill Sykes lacks the menace of Oliver Reed's portrayal and Harry Eden's Artful Dodger lacks the charisma of Jack Wild's rendition. All in all, the 1968 musical version and David Lean's 1948 classic both do a better job of breathing theatrical life into this literary work.

Dickens' 10 year old orphan named Oliver Twist shows remarkable resilience in the face of adversity. With considerable aplomb, he learns to adjust admirably to his surroundings without ever losing his social graces or courteous manners. In other words the elements which defined Oliver Twist did not come from his external conditions. They came from within.

No matter what situation we find ourselves, we have the ability to respond and react as children of God because of the internal presence of our heavenly Father within us. As the Apostle Paul says in his letter to the Philippians:

"I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need." Philippians 4:12 (KJV)

Whether we are enjoying an abundance of blessings or working through various trials and tribulations, the spiritual reality of our lives has not changed. As Paul goes on to say in his epistle:

"I can do all things through Christ who strengtheneth me." Philippians 4:13 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

I'm the first to admit that watching a horror film is not my idea of a good time. Not only do I find blood, gore, and the idea of violent death disturbing, the films in the genre are, for the most part, so mindlessly predictable that they becoming boring to me. Saw demonstrates that there it is possible to find some freshness in a much overworked genre.

The film opens with an unconscious man submersed in a tub located in a dingy bathroom. He awakens and crawls out of the tub to find that he is chained to the wall. Across the room is another man in the same predicament. There is a dead body in the center of the room (and beyond the reach of their chains) along with the gun that was presumably used to kill him.

Neither man knows why he's there or how he came to get there. As they search their surroundings and rack their brains, we come to learn, as they do, that they are the latest pawns in a perverse game played by someone the police call "the Jigsaw Killer."

This sick and twisted individual places people in bizarre, life-threatening situations where the single avenue for escape is only marginally better than death itself. In this instance, with the clock ticking towards a fatal deadline, one of these men is going to have to make an impossibly hard decision.

First time writer/director James Wan and screenwriter Leigh Whannell, who also plays one of the two shackled men, have created a powerful film debut. The tension is strong, the twists are surprising, the tone is consistent, and the ending is a real grabber.

Cary Elwes plays the other shackled man, a doctor whose wife and daughter will be killed unless he can figure out a way to murder the man across the room from him before the deadline is reached. We can sense the desperation of his character as he begins running out of options as well as time.

James Glover and Ken Leung play a pair of police detectives assigned the task of tracking down this sicko who seems able to always stay a step or two ahead of his pursuers. Wan and Whannell keep the suspense strong by telling the story largely from the victims' perspectives. We only know as much as they can figure out and the pieces of this particular puzzle appear slowly.

On a more grisly note, the filmmakers also manage to concoct some truly horrifying situations in which the victims of the Jigsaw killer find themselves. It is argued in the film that he can't really be called a killer because he doesn't really kill anyone. He just sets up a scenario where his victims have to kill or be killed. In fact, the only one of his victims to have survived the nightmare confesses that he helped her to appreciate her life to a greater extent.

This is convoluted logic that some Christians often use, albeit on a lesser scale. While in the midst of hard times it is not unusual to hear someone claim that God is testing them. They believe God sent an illness or some other form of tribulation in order to make them stronger in some way.

That kind of thinking just doesn't agree with the nature of God described in the Scriptures. We know our God to be all light and all love; a god who cannot be tempted with evil, neither will he tempt any man; and a god from whom comes every good and perfect gift. Such a god does not cause evil to visit us because it would violate his very nature.

Evil exists because we have a spiritual adversary whose realm includes the world in which we live. The adversary's only goals are to steal, kill and destroy. Because our God is greater, we can withstand any attack and become stronger as a result of it, but it would be a serious mistake to be grateful to the one who sent the attack in the first place.

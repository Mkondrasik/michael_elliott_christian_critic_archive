Author-verified transcription

Set in the 1980s, A Home at the End of the World takes us inside the lives of a trio of sexually confused people growing up in a world that provides no sense of guidance or direction. It's difficult to know whether the filmmakers intend for us to celebrate the place they carve out for themselves or pity them for what they don't have.

Bobby (Colin Farrell) is a personable young man who has seen more than his share of tragedy. After losing his brother, who introduced him to drugs before he became a teen, he subsequently loses first one parent and then the other. His only remaining emotional connection is with Jonathan, his boyhood chum with whom he experimented sexually during their adolescence.

Jonathan, now openly gay, is living in New York City with his platonic roommate Clare (Robin Wright Penn). Bobby moves in with them and the three become fast friends. Of course both Jonathan and Clare fall in love with Bobby who is more than happy to respond to their romantic advances.

The film carries through the various stages of their deepening bond - Clare's desire to have a baby; Jonathan's sexual promiscuity; Bobby's fear of being abandoned or alone... but we never connect with any of the characters or understand what they are feeling at any given moment.

The script by The Hours scribe Michael Cunningham is contrived and largely aimless. Because he focuses upon Bobby's story, he is unable to provide us with a solid center upon which to hold. Bobby is the human equivalent of a blank expression. He lives passively, with his only motivation being to love everybody and have everybody love him. Not much of a dynamic around which to build a movie.

Colin Farrell is hampered with a terrible wig that he wears throughout half the picture. It is distracting and ineffective. He portrays Bobby as a sweet innocent who has been cast adrift in an unfriendly world. He latches onto the only anchor he can find - his friendship with Jonathan and Clare.

Robin Wright Penn seems to be miscast as the female member of the triad. With hair almost as bad as Farrell's she seems to be playing a variation of Forest Gump's Jenny without having a Forest to play opposite her. Dallas Roberts completes the grouping as a stereotypical gay man who looks for love in all the wrong places.

Sissy Spacek makes a brief appearance as Jonathan's mother, a woman who would have failed Parenting 101 had such a course existed.

What Bobby, Jonathan, and Clare crave is a family in which they belong. It is a natural and understandable desire. But by inventing their own family structure, they have denied themselves of the greatest love of all - the love that Christ demonstrated when he gave his life so that we might be called to become part of the family of God.

God is faithful, by whom ye were called unto the fellowship of his Son Jesus Christ our Lord. 1 Corinthians 1:9 (KJV)

When we respond to God's call, we are able to enter into a fellowship with God, with his son, Jesus Christ, and with all saints who have likewise responded. No matter where we travel in this world, we will never be alone. We are part of a family that will never leave us. The bond we share with them is stronger than life itself.

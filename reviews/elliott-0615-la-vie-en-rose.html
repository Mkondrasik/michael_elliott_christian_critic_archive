The life of Parisian songstress Edith Piaf is brought to full effulgence with a mesmerizing performance by Marion Cotillard in La Vie En Rose.

Piaf, (which means little sparrow) rose from the streets of France to become one of the world’s most prominent and acclaimed singers. She may have been small and frail physically but she was also blessed with an overwhelmingly powerful voice.

Abandoned by her mother, she was left in the care of prostitutes, who formed a close attachment with the young child. As she grew, she learned to provide for herself by literally singing on the streets for her supper. With her friend Mômone (Sylvie Testud) collecting the francs and sous from passers-by, Piaf would sing from the depths of her soul, touching the hearts of all who heard.

Nightclub owner Louis Leplée (Gérard Depardieu) was one such person. He plucked the sparrow off the streets and put her on the stage, beginning her internationally known career.

Piaf lived as she sang - hard and without compromise - and she paid the price for it. Suffering from severe arthritis and drug dependency/addiction, she aged quickly and not at all gracefully. She died at 47.

Central to the telling of Piaf’s story is finding the right actress to play the part. Marion Cotillard is nothing short of brilliant. Every detail, inflection, posture is artfully crafted. Her performance should shine as brightly as any this awards season.

While his narrative is as audacious as his actress, director and cowriter Olivier Dahan plays extremely loose with the timeline, bouncing back and forth freely between the years seemingly without reason. The effect is disconcerting and confusing but the artistry of Cotillard keeps us connected to the story.

The other constant in the film is, of course the artistry of Piaf herself. Cotillard lip-syncs to the unique song styling of the Little Sparrow so we are better able to understand why she received the acclaim that she did. From the title song to the powerful closing (Non je ne regrette rien), Piaf sings with a conviction and emotional connection that is undeniable. Even though we may not understand the literal meaning of the French lyrics, the meaning of the song itself needs no translation.

Piaf considered that closing song, which means “No, I regret nothing” to be her life’s anthem. To truly live without regrets is an admirable goal. Whether or not she actually achieved it is something only she and God knew. But it is possible.

Despite the tragedies in our lives, the adversities we face, the mistakes we make, or the failings we have, we can walk in this world without fear or regret because of the promises that our heavenly father has made to us. There is no tragedy so deep that we cannot recover. There is no adversity that is so large that we cannot climb over. There is no mistake so severe that we cannot be forgiven. There is no failing so great that we cannot be loved. Our God is greater than all of our combined experiences and He loves us regardless and without condition. There is never a need for regrets because, with God, there is always a brighter tomorrow waiting for us.

Brothers and sisters, I do not consider myself yet to have taken hold of it. But one thing I do: Forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead. Philippians 3:13 (NIV)

Last Vegas

Author-verified transcription

“What happens in Vegas, stays in Vegas.” Or so the saying goes. After seeing how relaxed and comfortable the stars of Last Vegas are with one another, we can only hope they stay for a long time to come. This easy-going and enjoyable comedy brings us cinematic heavyweights Michael Douglas, Robert De Niro, Morgan Freeman, and Kevin Kline to portray childhood friends who reunite many decades later for one last bachelor party. These 60-somethings show us that they still know how to kick up their heels… even if their legs don’t go as high as they used to.

Douglas plays the steadfast bachelor of the group, who has finally given in to pop the question to his girl who is, of course, half his age. Upon hearing the news, the rest of the “Flatbush Four” make plans for his big send-off. Morgan Freeman, whose character spends his day playing with his grandchild, has to lie about where he’s going to his over-protective son. Kevin Kline plays a man whose wife, tired of his lack of enthusiasm in the bedroom, sends him packing with a Viagra pill and a condom (and a promise of no questions asked). Robert De Niro completes the quartet as a widower who holds a grudge against Douglas but eventually relents to the pressure from the others to complete the reunion.

And so we head off to Vegas where these so-called geezers take the city by storm. And to add a little female flavor to this 21st century Rat Pack, we’re introduced to Mary Steenburgen who plays a lounge singer who most usually sings to an empty lounge. These five film veterans are as loose and natural as I can remember ever seeing them. And because they seem to be having so much fun in the film, it is hard not to have fun watching them. Director Jon Turteltaub lets his stars command the screen and Dan Fogelman, who penned the script, has crafted a sweet but non-sentimental story of long-lasting friendship.

What the “Flatbush Four” can teach us is the meaning of friendship or, biblically speaking, “brotherly love.” The bond that is forged as children cannot be easily broken. Even the small rift between Douglas’ and De Niro’s characters is inconsequential when compared to the history they share.

In this age of Facebook “friends” and Twitter “followers” it is easy to forget the importance we should place upon those people whose lives have personally touched ours and affected us in ways that have not only made us who we are but have helped us to become even better.

“A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly: and there is a friend that sticketh closer than a brother.” Proverbs 18:24 (KJV)

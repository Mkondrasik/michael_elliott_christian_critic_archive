Author-verified transcription

No castle can have two kings. So states the tag line for The Last Castle, a typical prison film wrapped in the flag and sprinkled generously with patriotic fervor.

King Number One is Colonel Winter (James Gandolfini, The Mexican), commander of a military prison who has never seen combat but takes pride in an extensive collection of war memorabilia. King Number Two is Eugene Irwin (Robert Redford, The Horse Whisperer), a highly decorated three-star general who has been court-martialed for disobeying orders and sentenced to seven years behind bars. These two inevitably clash and eventually will vie for control of the prison.

Because Winter is such a horrible camp commandant, Irwin has no choice but to have him removed from office. Since he no longer has his rank or influence, Irwin must resort to the only recourse open to him. According to the military hand book, a commander will be removed from his position if he loses control of his command. Irwin and his army of inmates must stage a coup to rid themselves of their overbearing warden.

Director Rod Lurie (The Contender), working from a story by first time screenwriter David Scarpa, stacks the deck in Irwin’s favor from the very beginning. Winter may have the weaponry, the authority, and all the power on his side but it is all too clear that he is terribly outmatched by the intellect and righteousness of Irwin. Wanting to liken the conflict as a chess match being played out by two masters, Lurie would have been far more effective with this analogy had both his characters knew how the pieces move.

Still, The Last Castle remains enjoyable even if a bit predictable. It is greatly assisted by strong performances from Redford, Gandolfini and Mark Ruffalo (You Can Count On Me) who plays a prison bookie unmoved by Irwin’s impressive stature.

Redford brings to the film an authoritative presence. Irwin is a larger than life character and Redford plays him with the quiet assurance of a man who has nothing to prove. In a simple but surprisingly effective scene, Irwin teaches a sad sack ex-corporal (Clifton Collins Jr., Traffic) how to properly execute a salute. He does so, not by merely showing him the mechanics but by teaching him the history and meaning of the hand gesture. It is a wonderful scene which helps to establish who Irwin is... an intelligent and wise soldier who commands respect as well as men. He remains a credit to the uniform that he is no longer allowed to wear.

Gandolfini is hampered by the fact that his character was not written to be a worthy opponent to the demigod Irwin. Still, by underplaying the role and emphasizing his respect for his famous captive, Gandolfini manages to provide some interesting screen moments of his own.

The film does not flow as smoothly as it could. There are a number of dead moments that appear throughout the film interrupting the many fine action scenes. Fortunately, the underlying patriotism and love of country demonstrated by these flawed and fallen men more than cover the film’s minor weaknesses.

The biggest difference between Winter and Irwin, other than their battlefield experience, is how each views the inmates. Winter justifies his cruel and unforgiving handling of his prisoners by remembering their crimes. “I see the worst in (them). It makes my job easier,” Winter explains to Irwin. Conversely, Irwin tries to remind the men of who they were... indeed, who they can still be: soldiers of the United States of America. He looks for the good in them which inspires them to look for it in themselves.

Seeing how the men respond to these two opposing approaches is a tremendous lesson for our time. No one of any intelligence will deny that there are things that are wrong with America. But there is also much that is right. Looking for the good and drawing it out is a far worthier endeavor than continually finding fault. The same goes for individuals. As we look for the good in people, we are often rewarded by finding it.

George Washington

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 9, 2001
Rating
1½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Hope; promises of God; endurance; faith

Author-verified transcription

George Washington is a strange hybrid of a movie. Filmed using non-actors in mostly improvised scenes, it has the realistic feel of a documentary. But it isn’t. Neither does it have much of a narrative story for us to follow. It seems content merely to display children growing up in the rural backwaters of North Carolina, capturing their reactions to certain stimuli.

The film is narrated by twelve-year-old Nasia (Candace Evanofski) who, after breaking up with her childish boyfriend Buddy (Curtis Cotton III), turns her attention to an odd, helmet-wearing boy named George (Donald Holden). He wears the helmet because his skull never fused together, requiring him to keep his head dry and protected from sudden blows.

Nevertheless, George dreams of being a hero, president of the United States, or simply of being a central figure who marches in a parade. Nasia firmly believes he will one day fulfill those dreams although she would prefer the parade.

The film takes a macabre turn when an accident results in the death of one of the children. The remaining ones are stunned. Their reactions are so subdued that we realize that these young people have yet to really grasp the concept of the finality of death. Not wanting to be sent to the penitentiary for their part in the accident, the kids decide to hide the body rather than telling their parents.

The film then moves on, occasionally returning to see how the children are handling the guilt of keeping their secret. Vernon (Damian Jewan Lee) is having the hardest time, fearing repercussions and wishing only to be left alone, preferably on his own planet. Sonya (Rachael Handy) who, even at her tender years, describes herself as a “bad person,” is at the opposite spectrum, feeling nothing. With nothing in her life now, and no hope for anything in her future, she appears to be numb to all emotion.

George merely withdraws from his friends, donning a super hero costume consisting of a cape, a basketball jersey, and a hat made out of the carcass of a pet. So clad, he begins patrolling the town looking for good deeds to do.

George Washington has caught the eye of film critics nationwide who have been extolling its cinematic virtues quite appreciatively. While there is a certain value to the cinematography by Tim Orr which captures some arresting film images, the movie itself failed to hold my interest mainly because it went nowhere with the characters it tried to develop.

Writer/director/producer David Gordon Green, in his film debut, has captured some realistic moments on film, such as young black women chatting about wanting babies to love because their boyfriends are so bad at it, but he has interspersed them with scenes that are so extraneous to the plot and meaningless in terms of character development that we’re left scratching our heads and wondering why he’s included them.

The adults of George Washington are little more than afterthoughts. They seem to have next to no influence on the children and, in fact, treat them with the same deference that an extra on a movie set might give the star actors. Even the mother of the dead child is only seen at a distance and is talked about so briefly that we never have to consider her grief or anger.

Perhaps the saddest element, and one which served to distance me from involving myself in the movie, was the absence of any real hope for these characters. They had dreams and fantasies but no real hope. And that just isn’t true of life. Regardless of anything else, there is always hope.

That by two immutable things, in which it was impossible for God to lie, we might have a strong consolation, who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us: Which hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and stedfast, Hebrews 6:18-19a (KJV)

Hope is the steel girder in the framework of our conviction... something which we may call upon to help us build our lives. Our hope, as Christians, is based upon the promises which the God Who cannot lie has made. Because of those promises, we can endure any of life's challenges. Would that the characters in George Washington had been given that hope to cling to in their times of trouble.

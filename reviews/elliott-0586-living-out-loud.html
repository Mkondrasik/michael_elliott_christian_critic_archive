Living Out Loud’s main character, Judith Nelson, is one confused woman. After her husband of sixteen years leaves her for another (pronounced younger) woman, she embarks on an unusual journey of self discovery. What she comes to realize is that she left herself long before he did.

The title appears to refer to the thought processes and mental fantasies in which she indulges herself. We can hear and see them as she plays them out in her mind, but she never acts on them or brings them into physical manifestation. In other words, she isn’t “living out loud.”

But then, an odd romantic encounter involving a stolen kiss from a stranger starts a series of events that begins to reshape her already misshapen life. She forms friendships with a talented jazz singer and a down on his luck elevator operator. She experiments with drugs and alcohol. She appears to flirt with lesbianism and engage in sex with a paid gigolo... excuse me... masseuse. (Reviewer’s note: If this is a journey of self-discovery, remind me to stay at home.)

Holly Hunter (A Life Less Ordinary) makes some interesting acting choices as Judith, a woman whose identity was completely dependent upon being Mrs. Nelson. Once her husband departed, her entire self-image was voided. She changed so many things about herself to please him, there was nothing left of her after he was gone. It is an odd “Stepford wife” like performance which leaves many questions unanswered.

Danny Devito (The Rainmaker) gives his character, Pat Francato, a sincerity as well as a controlled sense of desperation, as he sees in Judith a potential end to his loneliness. He underplays the part nicely, avoiding the melancholy self-pity in which a less accomplished actor might wallow.

Queen Latifah (Hoodlums) also does well with her role of jazz singer Liz Bailey. Her musical renditions are especially memorable. Elias Koteas (The Thin Red Line) has a brief but effective role as the stranger whose passionate but misdirected kiss sends Judith from mental fantasy to physical reality and back again.

Screenwriter Richard LaGravenese (The Bridges of Madison County), in his directorial debut, fails to meld all the movie’s elements together to form a seamless product. Either he had a decent concept which was improperly executed, or a flawed concept made better by his performers. Either way, the end result is a movie which, like Judith’s life, does not satisfy. It begs to be compared to As Good As It Gets, but the only similarity is in the idea of an unlikely pairing (Hunter/Devito in the former, Hunt/Nicholson in the latter).

It is frustrating to watch Miss Hunter as Judith, looking for her lost self by turning to the various forms of worldly pleasures (or deceptions). The world is what gets us so confused to begin with... Why should we turn to it for our answers?

If we want to “find” ourselves, the best place for us to go is to God’s Word. We may be able to forget who we are or where we fit into the grand scheme of life, but God never forgets. And He thinks more highly of us than we oftentimes do of ourselves:

Beloved, now are we the sons of God... 1 John 3:2 [KJV]

Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us. Romans 8:37 [KJV]

That ye may be blameless and harmless, the sons of God, without rebuke, in the midst of a crooked and perverse nation, among whom ye shine as lights in the world. Philippians 2:15 [KJV]

We are His children, shining as lights in a crooked and perverse world, able to be conquerors over all trials and tribulations. There are many such truths contained in the Word of God. If we would believe... really believe... just a fraction of all that God tells us we could be, we would be the greatest people in the history of the world.

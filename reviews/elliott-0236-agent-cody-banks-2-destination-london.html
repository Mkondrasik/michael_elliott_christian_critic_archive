Agent Cody Banks 2: Destination London

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 12, 2004
Rating
2 stars
Source

Sermon / spiritual topic: Mind; thoughts; free will; spiritual battle; renewing the mind

Author-verified transcription

The one bad thing about a halfway successful teen adventure/comedy is the knowledge that a sequel won't be too far behind. Agent Cody Banks 2: Destination London recycles the same premise as last year's original film but uses only half the charm.

Frankie Muniz returns as Cody Banks, junior operative for the CIA. During his summer training, which doubles as an arts and crafts camp, the camp counselor/commander-in-chief narrowly escapes a commando capture thanks to Banks' assistance. Trouble is, the counselor was a bad guy in disguise working on a diabolical plan to rule the world using a mind control device.

Naturally, with so much at stake, the CIA decides to send their 16 year old agent to London in order to save every man, woman and child on the planet. And just to make things as difficult as possible, they assign a bumbling fool (Anthony Anderson) to be his "handler" while Cody goes undercover as a child prodigy in a youth orchestra which will somehow force the bad guys to surface.

Fortunately, the British have their own teenage secret agent (Hannah Spearritt) who is also on the job. Seems to me that the adult agents must have read the script and wisely stayed away from this project.

Once the premise is established, there's really no reason to watch the film. There is nothing about it that smacks of originality, ingenuity or high artistry. It is the equivalent of a paint-by-the-numbers art book. The cast may not embarrass themselves but neither do they distinguish themselves. It is a mild and innocuous family film that may entertain the young ones for about half of its running time. During the other half, I beg parents to please keep your charges out of my row. They will become antsy - that is, if the screening audience (a mix of adults and children) with which I saw the film is any indication.

Frankie Muniz is getting a bit too polished and mature to play the awkward adolescent. His Cody Banks doesn't ring true to the character established in the original movie and instead of allowing the actor to develop his character in a manner befitting his age, the filmmakers simply cut him loose in a sequel apparently designed to do little more than make money at the box office from fans of the first film. There's little attempt at character development or continuity.

English actress Hannah Spearritt assumes the Hilary Duff role as the quasi "love interest" and Anthony Anderson takes over for Angie Harmon as Banks' handler. In neither case are these the roles for which they will be remembered - a fact that I'm sure these actors will be forever grateful.

The idea of mind control has long been an element in mysteries and thrillers. It is also the crux of our spiritual battle. Our battlefield is, in essence, our mind. What we think determines what we do and really, who we are. If our enemy can gain an inroad to our minds, he will have gained an advantage from which we might not be able to recover.

Fortunately, the only mind control that need concern us lies in the reality of the free will that our God has given to us when He made us. We have the freedom to think whatever it is we choose to think. The only mind control that matters is the control we have over our mind. Therefore there is no excuse to allow ourselves to be manipulated to think ungodly thoughts. We can rise above the influences of this world and stay in control of our mind.

For to be carnally minded is death; but to be spiritually minded is life and peace. Romans 8:6 (KJV)

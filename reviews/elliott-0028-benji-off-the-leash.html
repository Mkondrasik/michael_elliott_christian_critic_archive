Benji: Off the Leash

Thirty years ago, producer Joe Camp, working outside the major studio system, introduced us to the lovable canine star Benji. He’s back with another Benji movie and while there are problematic elements to the film, it shows that we do reserve a soft spot in our heart for any sentimental story about an unhappy boy and a lost dog.

Borrowing from the Benji legend (the original dog was rescued from an animal shelter) Camp weaves this element into his story line. In the film, a nationwide search is taking place for the next dog to play Benji. Meanwhile, a vicious dog-breeder (Chris Kendrick) heartlessly tosses away a worthless (to him) mixed breed puppy and leaves it to die. His 14 year old son, Colby, (Nick Whitaker) secretly carts it away, hides it in the coolest fort since Swiss Family Robinson.

The dog, known only as “puppy,” grows up to be super smart with a caring and understanding heart. Living by his wits and depending upon the kindness of strangers, he helps a fellow mutt (named Lizard Tongue) to evade a pair of bumbling animal control officers, finds a friend for a lonely old man, and saves his own ailing mother from the clutches of that nasty dog-breeder.

As in the other Benji films, these dogs don’t talk and yet their furry little faces are so expressive it appears as though they communicate both emotion and thought. Unfortunately, the same cannot be said for their human costars who give us one-dimensional and completely unbelievable characterizations.

This may be acceptable in a children’s movie but Camp has tried to insert some pretty mature thematic elements into his film – elements such as domestic violence or illegal puppy mills - which deserve better handling.

Chris Kendrick plays an animal abuser whose violence extends to the members of his own family. He paints his character so black with no redeeming qualities that it’s a wonder he hasn’t already been put behind bars.

Randall Newsome and Duane Stephens as less than intelligent dogcatchers were cast to be the comic relief of the film. All the film asks of them is to be constantly outsmarted by their canine prey and fall face first into the occasional mud puddle.

Nick Whitaker as Colby is the generic “everykid” with whom we are to identify. He’s generous and caring although he clearly dislikes his father (stepfather?) and disobeys him at every opportunity.

But this film really isn’t about him. In fact, the movie works best when the humans aren’t on the screen and we can just follow the antics of the four-footed stars. The word I heard most as I was leaving the theater along with the parents who were there with their small children was “cute.” I assume they were talking about the dog and not the story itself.

Both animals used in the film were rescued from shelters and trained to “act” for this film. Camp, in interviews, says he hopes that the film, like the first film, will spark people’s interest in animal adoptions.

Puppy was considered by the breeder to be worthless because he wasn’t purebred. Unfortunately, people can make the same instant judgments about us based upon such insignificant things as the color of our skin or hair. They would be, as is the breeder in the film, wrong to do so.

The quality of our lives does not lie in our bloodlines or heritage. It lies in our believing and in what we choose to do with what we believe. Puppy’s actions are what led him to stardom. Our believing actions will do no less.

A man’s gift maketh room for him, and bringeth him before great men. Proverbs 18:16 (KJV)

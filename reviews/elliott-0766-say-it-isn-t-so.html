Author-verified transcription

Few filmmakers send the needle pinging off the negative end of the taste meter quite as consistently as the Farrelly Brothers (Me Myself and Irene). Even though their names do not appear in the writing or directing credits, their brand of outrageous humor is on full display in a new, crude comedy about incest and animal husbandry, Say It Isn't So!

Chris Klein (American Pie) stars as animal shelter employee Gilly Noble. A corn-fed Indiana orphan, he is searching for his birth parents while he simultaneously looks for a true love to call his own. He finds the latter first, in the shapely guise of a klutzy hairstylist/bikini waxer named Jo Wingfield (Heather Graham, Boogie Nights). After she slices his ear off during a particularly bad haircut, they fall deeply in love, consummating their relationship under a pinup poster of TV star and "thigh master" Suzanne Somers.

Naturally, once Gilly's parental origin is discovered, he is devastated to learn that he and Jo share more than sex. They apparently sit on the same branch of the family tree. This is news that causes their small Indiana town to develop an affinity for shouting out crude and cruel one-liners. Gilly and Jo separate: Gilly to join a road kill cleanup crew and Jo to marry a multimillionaire in Beaver, Oregon.

Clues are dropped (rather like a ten ton weight) and Gilly becomes convinced that he and Jo are not kin after all. He dashes off to Oregon to save her from a fate worse than incest.

James B. Rogers has served as assistant director on a number of previous Farrelly Brothers' projects. Here, he takes the director's helm, but the Farrelly influence is unmistakable and for good reason... the brothers have produced the film.

The humor is coarse and rather mean-spirited in an offhand and casual way. Jokes include references to incest, double amputees, stroke victims, bestiality, nipple piercing, body hair, redneck stereotypes and sexual dysfunction to name a few. Innuendo not being funny enough, the filmmakers are both graphic and direct when mining these topics for laughs.

The actors bring a considerable amount of conviction to their roles considering some of the silly and highly unlikely situations in which they find themselves. Both Chris Klein and Heather Graham play it straight-faced, leaving much of the outrageousness to their supporting cast.

Orlando Jones (Double Take) is the most effective as an energetically helpful though legless pilot who befriends Gilly and assists him in his attempts to get his girl back... that is, whenever his prosthetics aren't being stolen out from under him.

Sally Field (Eye For An Eye) plays Valdine Wingfield, the overbearing, foul-mouthed, blue-collar mom of Jo who sees her daughter's marriage to the Beaver multimillionaire as her ticket to park a double-wide on easy street. Richard Jenkins (What Planet Are You From?) is cast as her husband who is recovering from a stroke and must speak his profanities through a digitized voice box, making him sound like the robot from Lost In Space.

Developing a reputation for pushing the envelope of taste in order to shock an audience into laughter has a built-in flaw. Over time, audiences tend to develop tolerances or resistance to "shock humor." Filmmakers who rely upon this kind of humor must continually raise the bar (or lower it, depending on one's perspective) in order to elicit the desired audience response. The problem with having a sliding standard of decency is that once the line is moved, it becomes easier to move the next time. Ultimately, that standard will have to be removed altogether because it will have become meaningless.

But shun profane and vain babblings: for they will increase unto more ungodliness. 2 Timothy 2:16 (KJV)

The ever increasing amount of crudity in today's mainstream films only add to the growing perception that a moral decline is taking place in our society. Detractors can "say it isn't so" as much and as often as they like. The content of this film indicates otherwise.

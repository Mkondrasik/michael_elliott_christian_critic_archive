She's All That

Author-verified transcription

Miramax Films' teen comedy, She's All That, doesn't break any new ground, but it does cover the old territory with fresh new faces. Reminiscent of the George Bernard Shaw tale of Pygmalion (or My Fair Lady, or Pretty Woman, or any number of other comedies aimed at the teen-aged market), She's All That is a shallow but pleasant experience.

Zach Siler is the BMOC (Big Man on Campus): Class president, star athlete, babe magnet... He's a shoo-in for prom king. He also has a trophy girlfriend who, on the surface, seems to match him in every way.

When said girlfriend, Taylor, dumps him for an obnoxious MTV personality with whom she had a spring break fling, he foolishly boasts that he could take any girl in school and turn her into the prom queen.

Surely you can guess the rest. His buddies challenge him, betting that they can find someone that doesn't have a prayer of being chosen prom queen. Enter Laney Boggs, unanimous choice for Zach's project. She's an unpopular, darkly depressed art student. But as Zach gets to know her, he begins to genuinely feel something for her. Suddenly, with contact lenses, a new hair style, and form fitting clothes, drab Laney is transformed into a real "hottie." But then, (gasp!) she finds out about the bet and Zach is in the doghouse.

Both director Robert Iscove and screenwriter R. Lee Fleming are making their feature film debuts and have obviously chosen to stay on familiar ground. They have kept their product formulaic from the opening scene to the closing credits. The formula happens to work... largely because of the personable performances by this young and relatively unknown cast.

Freddie Prinze Jr. (I Know what You Did Last Summer), as Zach, uses a casual, confident manner which is very appealing on screen. Rachael Leigh Cook (Living Out Loud), as Laney, is adorably vulnerable. At one point her character asks the soulful question, "Am I kiss-able?" Miss Cook need not have the same worry.

The supporting characters all fall into the stereotypical teen-age molds: Matthew Lillard (The Curve) plays the self-centered and vacuous Brock Hudson, a character patterned after Puck from MTV's Real World. Jodi Lyn O'Keefe (Halloween H2O) is Taylor Vaughn, the shallow and self-centered favorite choice for prom queen. Anna Paquin (Hurlyburly) is Zach's wise younger sister, while Kieran Culkin (The Mighty) is Laney's awestruck younger brother.

Many of the actions and motives of the more immature characters are borderline offensive (thus giving the film its deserved PG-13 rating), but such infantile goings-on are balanced out by the straight-laced and more noble characteristics of Laney and Zach.

However, by having Laney turn physically from a wallflower to the belle of the ball, the filmmakers do appear to be sending a subliminal message that the worth or value of a person is dependent upon his or her physical appearance. That simply isn't true. Neither is the world's teaching that "beauty is only skin deep." The Word of God teaches that beauty emanates from within.

Your beauty should not come from outward adornment, such as braided hair and the wearing of gold jewelry and fine clothes. Instead, it should be that of your inner self, the unfading beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit, which is of great worth in God's sight. 1 Peter 3:3-4 [NIV]

If women are looking for an extra quality that will make them stand out in a crowd, or if they want that special inner glow that causes a room to light up as they enter, they aren't going to find it in a bottle or on a rack at Nieman Marcus. The beauty that is put on is false and temporary. The beauty that shines from within is real and eternal.

A woman that loves God and continuously manifests His light via her actions retains a beauty that will never fade with time.

Drop Dead Gorgeous

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 23, 1999

1 star

1 hr: 37 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Ambition; competition; humility; service

Author-verified transcription

Drop Dead Gorgeous could have been drop dead funny. It isn't. The filmmakers, striving to produce a Spinal Tap kind of "mockumentary," have only succeeded in making an uneven spoof of small town beauty pageants. It alternates between being moderately amusing to being drearily slow, painfully predictable, and more than occasionally offensive.

It is the 50th anniversary of the Sarah Rose Miss Teen Princess America Pageant and a faux documentary team has decided to visit Mount Rose, Minnesota to follow the dreams and aspirations of the young contestants as they compete in the local preliminary to see who will represent Minnesota in the national finals.

Odds on favorite to win is Becky Leeman (Denise Richards, Wild Things), daughter to an ex-Mount Rose Miss Teen Princess America winner, Gladys (Kirstie Alley, For Richer or Poorer). Becky has been groomed for this pageant since birth and it shows. With a plastic smile and her sanctimonious responses to the judges' ridiculous, Barbara Walters-type questions ("If you were a tree, what kind of tree would you be?"), Becky has been coached to superficial perfection by her mother.

If a girl with a little more depth to her has any possibility of beating Becky, it would have to be Amber Atkins (Kirsten Dunst, Small Soldiers). The sweet, hard working, talented, and personable daughter of a trailer trash mom (Ellen Barkin, The Big Easy), Amber is clearly slanted so as to be the audience's favorite choice to win the tiara... That is, if she survives. The "joke" of the film's title is that anyone who threatens to stand between Becky and the title of Miss Teen Princess America mysteriously ends up on the cold side of dead. This is a state of being with which Amber is well acquainted because of her after-school job as a mortuary make-up artist.

The acting, for the most part, is the saving grace of the film, although Ms. Ally never gets to the heart of her character, depending too much on the Minnesotan accent and phrasings ("Yah, you betcha") instead of developing an "inner life." In a documentary, even a mock one, we need to believe that the characters Miss Richards and Miss Dunst provide us with are real. Their performances are lost, however, by poor editing, a weakly structured framework, and an unnecessary reliance on "gross" humor on the part of the filmmakers and the supporting cast.

Surely we, as a movie going public, should have matured beyond the point where group vomiting would be considered amusing. We should be sensitive and emotionally sophisticated enough to recognize that the depiction of a mentally retarded man for comic relief is both infantile and demeaning. And the scene where one of the contestants sings "You're Just Too Good To Be True (Can't Take My Eyes Of Of You) to a life-sized dummy of a crucified Jesus is simply beyond the pale.

However, riding the crested wave on the boundaries of taste (and doing it beautifully) is Ellen Barkin in a howlingly funny performance. Her beer-swilling beautician with a mother's love (and a sailor's vocabulary), sees the pageant as her daughter's only ticket out of Mount Rose. She'd give her right arm (literally) to see her daughter leave their small podunk town to pursue her dream of being the next Diane Sawyer.

The film tries to take a scathing satirical look at ambition and the lengths people will go to win. While the filmmakers were not always successful, the topic is one that merits consideration. Jesus Christ's own disciples wrestled with ambition and competitiveness.

Also a dispute arose among them as to which of them was considered to be greatest. Luke 22:24 [NIV)

Competitiveness is a part of human nature. But we know that human nature is controllable. Jesus taught his disciples that spiritual greatness requires humility, meekness and a willingness to serve. Qualities that he, himself, exemplified.

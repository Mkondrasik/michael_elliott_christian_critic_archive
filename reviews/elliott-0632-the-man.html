Author-verified transcription

Super cool meets super dork in The Man. In fact, the entire premise of the film is based on a study in opposites. Cop vs. civilian. Black vs. white. Street smarts vs. Wisconsin naiveté.

ATF Federal agent Derrick Vann (Samuel L. Jackson) makes his own rules. Unfortunately so did his partner. The difference is that Vann is one of the good guys. His partner was dirty. I say “was” because he ended up dead after letting a band of arms dealers into the ATF vault. Now, Vann is feverishly trying to set up an undercover “buy” to catch the crooks and recover the weapons before they are sold to undesirables.

Vann learns that a plan made in haste does not always unfold as expected. For instance, he did not plan for Andy Fiddler (Eugene Levy), a talkative dental equipment salesman, to get in the middle of things. When Andy is mistaken by the chief bad guy (Luke Goss) as the “buyer,” Vann is reluctantly forced to take him in tow and follow through with the charade. Fiddler, already a fish out of water as a Wisconsin conventioneer visiting the Detroit 'hood, is moved further out of his element as he is forced to deal with hardened killers who mistakenly believe him to be “The Turk,” an international smuggler wanted by Interpol.

Eugene Levy's droll delivery is perfectly suited for a character whose optimistic attitude ("I never met anyone who didn't eventually become my friend.") and uneventful life ill prepares him for a walk on the wild side.

His family man friendliness is in direct contrast with the ill-tempered, non-trusting Vann which Samuel L. Jackson plays with streetwise intensity. The differences between these characters are played for laughs. Taken individually, there's nothing about either performance that is particularly funny or noteworthy. The beauty comes from the combination. Opposites do attract - especially in a comedy.

Director Les Mayfield wisely keeps the action moving at a brisk pace for a comparatively short time. The film clocks in at well under 90 minutes. Anything more would have stretched the premise to its breaking point. As it is, The Man gives two strong actors the opportunity to play off of one another to humorous effect and does so before our laughs start to feel hollow.

Andy, with his family values, teaches Vann a thing or two about priorities. Vann's career dominated his life to the point that it destroyed his marriage and threatened to destroy his relationship with his young daughter. It nearly succeeded until Andy, with his wise but unwanted counsel, taught him what needs to be cherished in our life.

Jobs are important but interchangeable. Raising a child or building a family takes an investment of time and focused energy - in other words, a commitment.

And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up. Deuteronomy 6:7 (KJV)

A father's primary "job" is to teach his children. He can't do that if he's spending all his time at the office.

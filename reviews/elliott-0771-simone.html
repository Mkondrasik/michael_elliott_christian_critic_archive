Author-verified transcription

The Hollywood machine makes it a point to glamorize its stars so that they might appear larger than life and thus somehow worthy of our adulation. In Simone, a satirical comedy by Andrew Niccol (The Truman Show), a new actress is so idolized by her fans that the reality of her life could never measure up to the fantasy that is presented. This is especially true because she neither real nor living.

Viktor Taransky (Al Pacino, Insomnia), is a Hollywood director who is frustrated with the insane demands of egocentric actors for whom popularity equates to industry power. When it looks like his latest project may be shelved by the studio when his leading lady quits over "artistic differences," Taransky stumbles upon a "pixelated" solution. He manufactures a computer generated actress he names "Simone" (short for Simulation One) and plugs her into his film.

The film becomes a hit and Simone (Rachel Roberts in her film debut) becomes a star. Thrilled with the prospect of having an leading actress without an ego or agenda, Taransky "casts" Simone in his next film while making sure that neither cast, crew, press, or public find out that she is merely a computer program.

It isn't long before Simone becomes a cultural icon -- her popularity soaring despite (or is it due to) the fact that she never makes public appearances. Soon, Simone's presence in a film is headline news, eclipsing the film itself. Taransky's work is being sublimated by the popularity of an actress who is a figment of his own imagination. In order for his work to be heard, he must destroy what might be his greatest work of all.

Al Pacino is not generally known for playing comedy, but he is quite enjoyable here as the beleagured director who trades reality for virtual reality only to find that both result in a loss of creative control. Rachel Roberts is attractive but her role doesn't call for much more than the thinnest of characterizations.

The supporting players are solid: Catherine Keener (Being John Malkovich) as Taransky's ex-wife and current boss; Evan Rachel Wood (Little Secrets) as their savvy daughter; Winona Ryder (Mr. Deeds) as a prima donna actress whose absurd contract demands drives Taransky to a non-human alternative; Elias Koteas as the one-eyed eccentric inventor who spent eight years in front of a computer designing the code that made Simone possible; and Pruitt Taylor Vance (The Legend of 1900) as an unscrupulous tabloid magazine editor and obsessed fan who will stop at nothing to get the inside scoop on Simone.

Simone skewers the idol worshipping insanity which fuels the Hollywood scene. Some of the humor may be too pointed or "inside" to be appreciated by all but there is much that will be universally enjoyed. There is also a lesson buried beneath the exaggerated humor.

As we watch a sophisticated California crowd turn to mob frenzy at the mere mention of Simone's name, we might ask ourselves if today's version of "idol worship" is any different than what was practiced in the Old Testament. An idol does not have to be a "golden calf" or a religious icon. It is anything that takes up residence in our hearts as the ultimate object of our desire. There's no question that the devotion often displayed by followers of today's pop star celebrities exceeds reason and reaches, for them, a religious experience.

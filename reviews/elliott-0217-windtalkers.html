Windtalkers

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 14, 2002
Rating
2 stars

Original Author Copy

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: War; peace; reconciliation; unity; God's kingdom

Author-verified transcription

There must be thousands of true life stories set amidst the backdrop of WWII. One such story serves as a framework for the movie Windtalkers. This film features the Navaho nation and their largely forgotten, but nonetheless crucial, involvement during the fight in the Pacific. It is a story which deserves to be told but told better than it is in this film.

According to the movie, the Japanese were successfully breaking the American's coded transmissions, giving them a distinct advantage over US interests. The American military finally turned to the Navahos and using a code based upon their Indian language, gained the ability to transmit information over the radio without the enemy becoming the wiser.

This code proved to be extremely successful. So much so that great lengths had to be taken to keep the code from falling into the enemy's hands. Thus, a marine was assigned to each Navaho radioman. His responsibility was to protect... not the man, but the code. If it became likely that his Navaho was going to be captured by the enemy, his job was to prevent his man from revealing the code secrets. Never overtly stated, but always understood, this meant that the marine would need to be able to kill his "codetalker" should capture appear eminent.

I am grateful for the history reminder and plan on researching further the Navaho contribution to the war in the Pacific. But that isn't really what this movie is about. The main story deals with Sgt. Joe Enders (Nicholas Cage, The Family Man), who overcomes the ghosts of his past to be able to function in the present.

Joe is the sole survivor of his first combat command. Physically and emotionally scarred as a result, he is assigned to "baby sit" Private Ben Yahzee (Adam Beach, Smoke Signals) with the understanding that he may very well need to exercise the unthinkable option. In a similar situation is Peter "Ox" Henderson (Christian Slater, The Contender) and his Navaho assignment, Charles Whitehorse (Roger Willie, Adaptation).

Director John Woo takes us through bloody skirmish after bloody skirmish, using now familiar (AKA clichéd) conventions and stereotypical characters to move this story along. As time progresses, so does the relationship between the white marines and their Indian counterparts.

All of the actors involved present complete and recognizable characters but they are working from a script that leaves too much unexplored. We leave the theater wanting to know more about the Navahos' role in the war while wishing that there were less scenes of explosive gore and carnage.

The film does present an interesting perspective on war and its participants. One of the soldiers reflects on the fact that less than one hundred years before, the white man (or cowboy) and the Indian were seen as enemies but now they are fighting together on the same side. He wonders if, despite the fact that they are currently doing the best to kill one another, the Americans and the Japanese might one day be sitting together as friends instead of adversaries.

One day, of course, the very thought of war will be a thing of the past. Peace shall reign over the land as we learn to live in harmony with all who are welcomed into our Father's house.

And he shall judge among the nations, and shall rebuke many people: and they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more. Isaiah 2:4 (KJV)

After being subjected to so many images of death and destruction via this year's plethora of war movies while living in a world where violent terrorist acts might occur at any time, an eternity of peace and love sounds pretty good to me.

Author-verified transcription

Boston’s Wellesley College in 1953 was a bastion of conservatism and traditional morals. It had the reputation of attracting the best and brightest women of the country to drink from their fount of knowledge. In Mona Lisa Smile, a progressive art history teacher joins the faculty and is dismayed to learn that most of the students there are majoring in pre-marriage.

Katherine Wilson (Julia Roberts) thought she was coming to an institution that was helping to educationally develop the leaders of tomorrow. She grows to learn that the focus from the faculty and the alumni is aimed more at developing leaders’ wives. It is a mindset that has been religiously taught to the students and adopted by them without question.

Betty (Kirsten Dunst) is in the midst of planning for her impending wedding but has no plans for herself beyond what she imagines as being a storybook marriage. Joan (Julia Stiles) is taking pre-law courses and is considering Yale for her graduate work unless her boyfriend pops the question by the end of the school year.

Connie (Ginnifer Goodwin) dreams of marriage but, after years of negative reinforcement because she does not fit the physical image of the “ideal woman,” is beginning to feel that the dream may be out of reach. Giselle (Maggie Gyllenhaal) is having too much fun in the present to think much about the future. The bad girl of the group, Giselle has no qualms about having affairs with older men, whether they be faculty members or married.

Wilson sees the potential in these women going untapped because they stifle it in order to advance the careers and development of the men in their lives. She believes that women should be allowed to explore and develop themselves as individuals and she is not hesitant to advance those views to her students even though such “subversive” talk is grounds for immediate dismissal.

If we want to talk about wasted potential, we need look only at the number of female roles in this film that could have been explored with richness and depth. Instead we get a lot of superficial playacting and posing that I suppose is supposed to represent the style of the times. It just didn’t connect with this viewer.

Kirsten Dunst overplays the catty nature of her character to such an extent that when her comeuppance arrives, we feel neither compassion nor pity. Julia Stiles adopts a strange upper crust speech pattern - as if her character took one too many elocution classes. I found it to be a distracting and distancing element that weakens her performance.

Maggie Gyllenhaal manages to avoid the missteps of her costars and turns in a commanding performance. Her screen presence is undeniable.

Marcia Gay Harden, as the etiquette, poise, and diction instructor, plays a repressed, unhappy caricature that doesn’t quite come alive for us.

Julia Roberts may be a better movie star than she is an actress and she’s fine in this role. Recognizable and charming. But Katherine Wilson is not a character that audiences will necessarily love and embrace. Perhaps it is because, for much of the film, she seems to denigrate women who choose to be wives and mothers instead of having a career. The filmmakers do eventually concede the point that being “just a homemaker” is a valid choice but they stack their cards so strongly in favor of the feminist view that it comes off almost as being an afterthought rather than a revelation.

A woman who chooses to make her family “a career” should never be made to feel inferior to those who don’t. God certainly doesn’t feel that way.

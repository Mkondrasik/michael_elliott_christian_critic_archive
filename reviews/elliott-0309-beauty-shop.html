Beauty Shop

Author-verified transcription

The premise was a slice of marketing brilliance. Take the hit urban comedy Barbershop; do a gender flip by setting it in a woman's salon; tell the same kind of story with outspoken, sassy women dishin' dirt and washin' hair. Beauty Shop should have been a slam dunk success. It isn't.

Queen Latifah plays the female version of Ice Cube as Gina, hair stylist exemplar working for an effeminate piece of Euro trash called Jorge (Kevin Bacon). Tired of her boss's verbal abuse and unappreciative manner, Gina strikes out on her own. After getting a modest loan from an uncooperative bank teller by giving her a makeover, Gina opens her own salon and alienates half her employees by adding Lynn (Alicia Silverstone), a Southern white shampoo girl with a license to cut hair, to the staff.

That's pretty much the framework to the film. The extraneous side stories simply pad the running time and give the projectionist reason to change reels.

The film strengths should be the various characters who flit in and out of Gina's shop: the "is he gay or isn't he" gorgeous male stylist (Bryce Wilson); the Maya Angelou-spouting older stylist (Alfre Woodard); the young tart (played by a grown up Keshia Knight-Pulliam who was Rudy on The Cosby Show); and many others.

The problem is that none of these characters make much of an impression and watching their stories play out is as interesting as watching hair dry.

Kevin Bacon sashays his way into some amusing moments as an affected but trendy salon owner but he's about the only actor who makes something remotely interesting happen. Alicia Silverstone puts on a thick Southern drawl; Mena Suvari puts on a padded bra; Andie MacDowell puts on padded pants; and Djimon Hounsou puts on a game face as Gina's piano playing electrician boyfriend who fails to make sparks fly between them.

The film is punctuated with comments from a neighborhood DJ called Hollerin' Helen whose radio show is continually piped into the shop. Unfortunately most of those comments are hardly noteworthy.

The problem seems to be in the script which, although it copies the general framework of Barbershop, is totally missing the energy and edgy humor that made that film successful. There is also no effective counterpart to Cedric the Entertainer's role of Eddie which took that 2002 comedy to a much higher plane.

The sitcom level writing doesn't allow for much of an in depth analysis for biblical principles at work. There's some superficial nods as Queen Latifah instructs those who walk into her shop on how to treat one another with respect and courtesy. It's a bit hypocritical because most of the laughs come when the characters fail to do so. Still... it is a moral that, even when poorly presented, can communicate a valuable truth.

When we disrespect people, we dishonor ourselves and the God in whose image we are made. We can and should live up to the standard He set when he established the principles summed up in what we call "the golden rule."

And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise. Luke 6:31 (KJV)

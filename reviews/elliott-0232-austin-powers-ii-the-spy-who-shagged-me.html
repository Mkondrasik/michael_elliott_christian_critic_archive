Austin Powers II: The Spy Who Shagged Me

Michael Elliott

June 11, 1999

1½ stars

Author-verified transcription

From the opening credits of Austin Powers II, which shows the British secret spy cavorting through a hotel in the nude with his private parts being surreptitiously blocked from view by various phallic items ranging from a banana split to a rump roast, we get a clear indication of what to expect from the next ninety minutes. Subtitled The Spy Who Shagged Me, Mike Myers latest offering is nothing more than one very long sexual innuendo, no pun intended (NPI).

Using a series of jokes most of us left behind in grade school (She: "Do you smoke after sex?" He: "I don't know, I never looked."), Austin Powers II is to movies what puberty was to many of us - A lot of titillation interrupted by moments of extreme embarrassment. (Or was that the other way around?)

The story, such as it is, deals with the return of Dr. Evil (Mike Myers), last seen orbiting the earth in his Big Boy statue. He immediately looks to dominate the world while seeking revenge on Austin Powers (also Mike Myers).

Traveling back through time to 1969, he hires Fat Bastard (again, Mike Myers) to steal the cryogenically sealed Austin Powers' mojo, otherwise known as his libido. The Austin Powers of the 90's is forced to return to the 60's to set things straight (again, NPI.)

For the record, Mike Myers does not play all the roles himself. Helping Dr. Evil are Robert Wagner as the eye patch wearing Number 2, Rob Lowe (doing an uncannily accurate Robert Wagner impersonation) as young Number 2, Mindy Sterling as Frau Farbissina, Seth Green as Scott Evil, Kristen Johnson as Ivana Humpalot, and Verne Troyer as Dr. Evil's 2 foot clone that he calls Mini-me.

On the side of Austin Powers, is Heather Graham as Felicity Shagwell, Michael York as Basil Exposition, and a host of scantily clad go-go girls.

It is impossible to take such an offering seriously mainly because the filmmakers don't. Tongue planted firmly in cheek (NPI), Mr. Myers and company are completely uninterested in continuity, character integrity, or story line. To paraphrase one of the characters, "Don't worry so much about it. Just have fun."

And taken in moments, such advice is sometimes easy to follow. Scott's appearance on the Jerry Springer show (Topic: My Father is Evil and Wants To Take Over The World) where he is surprised by the entrance of Dr. Evil is riotous fun. Mr. Myers' portrayal of this cloying yet evil character remains amusingly incongruous.

But then, his 500 pound Fat Bastard character, enhanced by a prosthetic suit and makeup, which is almost as thick as his Scottish accent, pushes the limits of taste way over the edge to merely become gross and unfunny.

Most of the humor is derivative, geared more for immature, sexually obsessed and intellectually unchallenged minds. As such, it will undoubtedly be wildly successful in the American theaters. Mr. Myers has chosen to tap into a comic vein that, although not particularly rich, is certainly in no danger of being depleted.

It's not so much that it is bad humor, just that it's so one-leveled and surprisingly unoriginal. Much time is spent on jokes focused upon a particular part of the male anatomy which, throughout the movie, is kicked, bitten, discussed at length (NPI), and figuratively seen through the depiction of various familiarly shaped objects, including a rocket ship.

The talent of the outrageous Mr. Myers is undeniable and there are moments in the film which are enormously funny due to his comic efforts. But spiritually, there is shamefully little to recommend or highlight.

In one scene, Dr. Evil invokes the name of our Lord in order to control a misbehaving mechanical chair. It's an ill-advised moment which only indicates the lack of spiritual awareness or sensitivity on the part of the filmmakers. As the false exorcists in Acts 19 discovered, calling on the name of Jesus Christ without authority does not activate the power of God. Spiritually speaking, Austin Powers II is as limp as its mojo-less title character.

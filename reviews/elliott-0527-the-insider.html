Author-verified transcription

Some movies grab you with the speed of a bullet train and immediately take you on a wild, hair-raising ride. Others move with the lumbering pace of a freight train. Moving slowly under the weight of their cargo, they take time to establish their momentum, but once moving, they prove to be virtually unstoppable. The Insider belongs to the latter category, offering us an intelligent look at a ponderous and worthwhile subject.

The movie is based upon a Vanity Fair article entitled "The Man Who Knew Too Much" which related the true story of one individual's struggle to reveal truth regardless of the enormous personal cost.

Jeffrey Wigand (Russell Crowe, LA Confidential) was a corporate executive and chemist for Brown & Williamson, one of the "Big Three" tobacco companies. Fired for his lack of self-censorship abilities, he finds himself on the outside, holding insider information regarding the truth behind his ex-firm's callous attitude on matters of public health and the blatant manipulation of tobacco products he calls "nicotine delivery devices."

Wigand is bound by his confidentiality agreement not to reveal his insider knowledge, and reliant upon his severance package to provide for his family. But he also feels a compulsion to tell what he knows in the most public way.

Enter Lowell Bergman, (Al Pacino, The Devil's Advocate) a producer for 60 Minutes. Bergman works with Wigand patiently and honestly, building a relationship based on trust and integrity. Wigand relents and, risking all he holds dear, agrees to tape a segment for the popular TV news magazine.

The inflammatory segment will no doubt rock the world and the tobacco industry if it ever sees the light of day. Because of intimidation, threat of litigation, and the potential ruination of a multi-million dollar acquisition, CBS bows to corporate pressure and yanks the interview, leaving Wigand out to hang as the subject of the biggest "smear" campaign in modern day history.

Al Pacino is marvelous as Bergman, a man of integrity in a corporate culture where said quality is quickly becoming more a liability than a positive trait. His struggle to "do the right thing" is as much the center of this story as Wigand's.

Russell Crowe gives a richly textured performance as the whistle-blower. The paranoia and fear of the character contrasts nicely with his sense of umbrage and a hint of self-importance fueled by ego. This is no storybook hero. As Bergman explains it, Wigand and his wife are "ordinary people under extraordinary pressure." Mr. Crowe captures the description flawlessly.

A performance receiving much of the attention (and justly so) belongs to Christopher Plummer (Blackheart) who nails the persona of 60 Minutes correspondent Mike Wallace. Mr. Plummer affectionately plays every nuance of a high profile newsman at the pinnacle of his career, looking forward to the legacy which he will leave. It is a fully professional treatment and one with which Mr. Wallace should be well pleased.

Director Michael Mann (Heat), who also co-wrote the script with Eric Roth (The Horse Whisperer) handles the material with uncompromising frankness. The characters are seen, warts and all. The movie could have benefited from a trim here or tightening there, but that is a small criticism of a work of such uniform honesty.

Truth is a funny animal. As a constant, it stands inviolate. Those who attempt to manipulate "truth" for selfish purposes are ultimately disappointed for truth itself cannot be changed. Truth can be suppressed and truth can be ignored. But truth is truth eternally. In the movie, the legal department of CBS, using the highest degree of convoluted logic, tried to explain that the reason they had to pull the interview off the air was that it contained too much truth. "The greater the truth, the greater the damage."

Truth be told, truth only causes damage to those who choose to operate outside of its parameters. In one of life's greatest paradoxes, adherence to the confines of truth grants us the greatest freedom we could ever desire.

And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free. John 8:32 (KJV)

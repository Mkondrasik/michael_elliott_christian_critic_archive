Author-verified transcription

In 1966, an unknown ladies' basketball coach from Kansas was tapped by Texas Western University to coach their Division I NCAA basketball team. Don Haskins (Josh Lucas) wasn't expected to accomplish much more than to keep the existing players from embarrassing themselves while on the court with bigger, more talented teams. As Glory Road shows us, he did far more... he changed the face of the game forever.

With no budget and less support, Haskins sought out the best players he could recruit. What he found crossed the color barrier. Bringing no less than seven black players to the school in the season of 1966, he single-handedly integrated NCAA basketball (at least in the Southern division). His attempt, though successful, was met with serious, racially motivated obstacles. Haskins challenged the common belief that black players did not have the intelligence to compete with whites. The unspoken rule of thumb is explained in the film: It is okay to play one black on the road; two at home; and three if you're losing. Haskins ripped the color card up and simply played the best he had.

As they begin to win, the efforts of this tiny Texan team begin to take on epic proportions. The film culminates, as does the team's season, with a national championship game that is still described as the biggest upset in NCAA history and a game that changed the thinking of a nation. Stay through the credits for some sound bites from those who were there, including NBA coach Pat Riley and the real Don Haskins, accompanied by film clips of the actual game.

This Jerry Bruckheimer production for Disney Studios does provide an uncontroversial look at what was surely a controversial time in history. The script by Chris Cleveland and Gregory Allen Howard is a bit simplistic. The team is sweet and affable, the coach is a hard taskmaster who drills them unmercifully, and the few folks we meet who object to the integration have no problem changing their tune when the wins start mounting up.

Still, the film is surprisingly amusing with decent action footage and some nice ensemble work by a talented cast. Lucas has the intensity needed to communicate Haskins' single-minded determination. The group of actors who portray the players unselfishly share the screen while developing rounded and easily identifiable personalities.

Defining others (or ourselves) by ethnicity, cultural background, or skin color is to have a narrow and cripplingly limited view of the world. There is so much more to people than what can be seen at first glance. When we take the time to see what's beneath the surface, we begin to see that we have more things in common than the differences between us.

And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation. Acts 17:26 (KJV)

The earth's different races did not require separate acts of creation. The same life blood courses through all our veins. As we look at one another honestly, without prejudice or fear, we can understand that the differences we see are largely formed first in our own minds. Fortunately, these can be changed.

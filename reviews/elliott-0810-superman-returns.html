If the Christ symbolism in the first Superman (1973) was too subtle, Superman Returns, the latest remake/tribute to the Man of Steel, strips away the pretense and hangs the messianic message out for all to see.

Director Bryan Singer, who left the X-Men franchise to take on America's most beloved super-hero, delivers a sentimental and reverential take on a classic tale.

Though it has been almost 20 years since Superman last visited the silver screen, in this movie he has only been away for 5 - but he did leave without saying good-bye. Having gone off to search for more Krypton survivors, he left our world wondering where he was and if he would ever come back. He finally returns to earth (after an unsuccessful search) ready to take up where he left off.

The trouble is that the world has moved on without him. The media has found other stories to cover. Lois Lane (Kate Bosworth) is in a new relationship with a co-worker (James Marsden) and has a young son. Lex Luthor (Kevin Spacey) is out of prison and up to his old tricks - planning world domination. All that is left for Superman to do is to come to terms with his place in our alien world.

Superman Returns is difficult to pin down from a critical standpoint. It is a film that, as a whole, makes for an entertaining time at the theater. However, when deconstructing it, it is hard to identify the single reason for its success.

Brandon Routh certainly looks the part, as the title character, but his performance falls well shy of the standard set by Christopher Reeve. Kevin Spacey chews more scenery than a nest of termites but he never conveys a real sense of threat or danger. The actors portraying Jimmy Olson, Perry White, and Kitty Kowalski also pale in comparison to their 1973 counterparts.

Only Kate Bosworth as Lois Lane brings something a little new to the character. She adds a gravitas - a sad and knowing quality that comes from disappointment and maturity, despite her own fresh-faced, too youthful appearance.

The special effects are impressive - even when expected for a summer blockbuster film - but they are not as plentiful as we might want. The scene of him wrestling to gain control over a plummeting airline is especially noteworthy.

What is most surprising is the blatant nod to Christian beliefs. The spiritual references are numerous and obvious. Time and space prevents me from listing them all here. So I'll focus on just one.

To help her get over her disappointment at Superman's absence, Lois Lane wrote an article called "Why the World Doesn't Need Superman" for which she won a Pulitzer Prize. Apparently she half believed what she had written. "The world doesn't need a savior... and neither do I," she declares at one point. Of course she's wrong. We know it and, deep down, she knows it too.

Lois, through her reluctance to admit her need, mirrors many of us who were slow to accept Christ's love and sacrifice (or even those of us who have yet to do so). But our reluctance or hesitation that doesn't mean that God withdraws his promise of salvation or denies anyone the opportunity to believe. His offer remains on the spiritual table. All we need to do is claim it for our own. It is when we do that we find ourselves wondering what took us so long to come to the light.

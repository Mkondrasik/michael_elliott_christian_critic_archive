Author-verified transcription

What's a girl supposed to do when she suddenly discovers that the guy of her dreams has become the guy of her nightmares? That's the question haunting Jennifer Lopez (The Wedding Planner) as a spousal abuse victim who finally decides she has had Enough.

Lopez plays "Slim," a greasy spoon waitress who believes she has hit the romance jackpot when she falls for an affluent, personable, and handsome diner. Mitch (Billy Campbell, Once and Again) does indeed seem to be the ideal "white knight in shining armor" until about five years after the birth of their child, Grace (Tessa Allen, Providence). It is then that Mitch chooses to reveal his true colors as a violently abusive, controlling, and two-timing creep.

When Slim and Grace finally are able to slip away from Mitch, he will stop at nothing and bar no expense to get them back... dead or alive. Slim keeps changing towns, identities, and appearances in order to stay away from him but at some point, she knows that she's going to have to stop running.

With the police and legal system being of little to no help, Slim decides she must take matters into her own hands. First, she must turn those hands into lethal weapons so she takes a crash course in the self-defense art of Krav Maga. Once J-Lo turns into Jet Li she is ready to kick some serious wife-beating butt.

It is difficult to summarize the story of Enough without making it sound somewhat ridiculous. But we cannot dismiss it entirely. Director Michael Apted (The World Is Not Enough) has the talent to keep the suspense taut and pace clipped. Unfortunately, his skills behind the camera can do little to change what screenwriter Nicholas Kazan (Bicentennial Man) has left on the printed page.

Not only is character development an oxymoron here, the characters, undeveloped as they are, don't make much sense. For example, Slim won't check into a woman's shelter because she wants to protect her daughter from those negative surroundings, but then gives nary a thought to engaging in a high speed car chase with said daughter bucked in the back seat.

Within the restrictive constraints of the script, the actors perform adequately enough. Jennifer Lopez makes for a decent action heroine and Noah Wyle (ER) shines in a supporting role as a rogue cop on Mitch's payroll. Other supporting characters include Juliette Lewis (The Other Sister) as Slim's waitressing buddy, Dan Futterman (Urbania) as the guy Slim should have married, and Fred Ward (Tremors) as Slim's strange and estranged father.

Fear plays a big part in Enough. Slim is genuinely terrorized by the relentless pursuit of her husband. As long as she is being victimized by him, her fear is shared by those of us in the audience. As she begins her training, planning to end the abuse she has suffered, the fear begins to dissipate. The final conflict between husband and wife was more violent than any other previous scene, but the fear level was nonexistent. Why? Because Slim was no longer a victim. She was taking action.

We can question whether her vigilante action was the proper course, but the principle remains the same. Overcoming fear requires action coupled with believing.

Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid. John 14:27 (KJV)

We control the fear that resides in our hearts. By believing the promises made to us by God, and following the example set for us by Jesus Christ, it is available to live a life freed from fear.

Be strong and of a good courage, fear not, nor be afraid of them: for the LORD thy God, he it is that doth go with thee; he wil not fail thee, nor forsake thee. Deuteronomy 31:6 (KJV)

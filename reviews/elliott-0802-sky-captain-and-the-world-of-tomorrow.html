Sky Captain and the World of Tomorrow

Author-verified transcription

Sky Captain and the World of Tomorrow is a film that you dearly want to like, especially if you're a movie lover. The film obviously pays homage to a number of sources - 1940s film noir, B-movie adventures, and comic book plots among them. Watching it brings to mind such films as Fritz Lang's Metropolis, The Wizard of Oz, The Adventures of Superman and King Kong to name just a few.

The production design is undeniably stunning. It is doubly amazing when we realize that everything was done on a computer. All live action sequences were filmed in a single room in front of a blue screen. The actors' performances were then projected into a pre-existing computer generated backdrop using a program and digital process developed by director Kerry Conran.

For four years, Conran worked on his story idea, using nothing but his artistic vision and his laptop Apple computer. In that time, he succeeded in producing only six minutes of footage. It was enough. When producer Jon Avnet saw those six minutes, he agreed to bankroll Conran to develop his idea into a full length feature film that would ultimately take him six years to complete.

The story is simple enough. Intrepid reporter Polly Perkins (Gwyneth Paltrow) is investigating the disappearances of world-renowned scientists when an army of giant flying robots invade NYC. She teams up with ex-boyfriend Joe Sullivan (Jude Law), AKA Sky Captain, to do battle with the machines and find out who's controlling them.

They are assisted by Joe's friend and sidekick Dex (Giovanni Ribisi) and, later, by a British flying ace played by Angelina Jolie as they discover that the entire world is in grave danger.

Jude Law (who also co-produced the film) and Gwyneth Paltrow have the classic look of old time movie stars which serves the retro-style of the movie well. Ribisi provides some needed comic relief as the bland but faithful best friend. The late Laurence Olivier also shows up in a digitally created posthumous performance which, though brief, is sure to fire up a few heated discussions in Internet chat rooms as to its appropriateness.

Unfortunately, as brilliantly conceived and executed as the production design is, that element alone cannot carry a picture. Where Sky Captain falls short is in delivering a story that will thoroughly captivate and engage a modern audience. Those not busy picking out the various cinematic influences or looking for the presumably intentional continuity errors - an ever present element of the old Saturday matinee serials - may find that they are a bit disappointed by the fact that the film doesn't really seem to build momentum or sustain dramatic tension.

Even the basic thematic device of man's propensity for self-destruction and his desire for Shangra-La is somewhat glossed over. Not since the days of Eden has man had the experience of living in a paradise. When it does again finally occur, it will not be because of the plans or actions of men. A corruptible soul can never build or manufacture an incorruptible world. God has a world in store for us where we shall be privileged to dwell. Until that time, we must make do with this imperfect place called Earth.

But we remain hopeful that we shall one day live in a world that can be called a "paradise."

Nevertheless we, according to his promise, look for new heavens and a new earth, wherein dwelleth righteousness. 2 Peter 3:13 (KJV)

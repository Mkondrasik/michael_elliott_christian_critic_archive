Dreamgirls

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
October 21, 2005
Rating
3½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Talents; integrity; compromise; success

Author-verified transcription

It’s unusual to begin a review with kudos to the casting director but Debra Zane did a stellar job in assembling the perfect cast for the Hollywood remake of the Broadway hit, Dreamgirls. Of course she should thank whoever dropped Jennifer Hudson in her lap because not only does she steal every scene that she is in, she will be the word-of-mouth drawing power that will add to this film’s box office legs.

The story is basic Broadway musical fare and tells the story of a Motown girls’ group (The Dreamettes) who go from being unknown to recording megastars in just a few years. Of course they face plenty of emotional bumps along the way.

The voice in front of the trio of girls belongs to Effie (Jennifer Hudson), a sassy, brassy diva with the vocal chops to back up her in-your-face attitude. But with all her talent, the girls can’t catch a break… until they are asked to be the back up singers for James “Thunder” Early (Eddie Murphy) who is not the godfather of soul but just may be a distant cousin.

Backed by Cadillac salesman turned group manager Curtis Taylor Jr. (Jamie Foxx) and using the songs by Effie’s talented brother CC (Keith Robinson), The Dreamettes go from singing backup to realizing their dream of being Supreme-like headliners… with one small change. Much to Effie’s dismay, Curtis thinks that the svelte Deena Jones (Beyonce Knowles) would be a better choice to be the lead singer.

This is one fine adaptation of a Broadway musical and should help inspire other producers to continue the recent trend of bringing theatrical works to the screen. Director Bill Condon (Gods and Monsters) does a great job in developing the characters and having the music add to the telling of the story instead of simply being the frame in which the story is told.

But Dreamgirls is a performer’s production and Condon’s real contribution is providing a cinematic environment where his actors had the artistic freedom to stretch their talents to the full extent of their limits.

Foxx unselfishly takes on the crucial but less charismatic role of the group’s manipulative business manager. Though not showy, it is his role that anchors the film and keeps every other character tethered to the story.

Beyonce Knowles, like her character, is bland or non-descript during the first half of the film. As she morphs into Curtis’s image of a lead singer it is easy to see that she modeled her persona after Diana Ross. But it isn’t until her final number (‘Listen’) that she lets loose with a show-stopping number and reveals the full strength of her talent.

In contrast, both Jennifer Hudson and Eddie Murphy arrest our attention from the moment they appear on screen, both of them giving award winning performances. American Idol contestant Hudson brings the house down with a couple of numbers (notably ‘And I Am Telling You I’m Not Going’) and Murphy delivers what may be the best performance of his career.

The lesson of the film comes from watching the pureness of the singers’ dreams becoming tarnished as talent gets co-opted by greed. Instead of being free to express their souls through their music, the characters find themselves being molded and shaped in order to appeal to wider and whiter audiences. In the end, some may have found professional success but it comes at the cost of many personal failures.

A person with a highly developed talent (such as on display in this film) need not compromise his or her integrity. God promises:

A man's gift taketh room for him, and bringeth him before great men. Proverbs 18:16 (KJV)

Cold Mountain

Author-verified transcription

Charles Frazer's best-selling Civil War novel, Cold Mountain, is wonderfully adapted by director Anthony Minghella who uses an A-list of Hollywood talent to bring this story and its characters to life.

As the Civil War draws painfully to an end, Inman, a wounded Confederate soldier (Jude Law), struggles to make his way back to his North Carolina home and the woman who has promised to wait for him.

Meanwhile, that woman, the refined Ada Monroe (Nicole Kidman) finds herself totally unprepared to deal with the hardships and harsh conditions which face the womenfolk who were left behind.

With a premise and themes similar to Homer's Odyssey, the action bounces back and forth between Inman's association with death and war and Ada's growth and development into an independent, self-sufficient woman. Survival for either is not a guarantee and both will be changed by their experiences.

During Inman's difficult journey, he encounters many different people, any one of whom could cost him his freedom and his life: the fallen minister (Philip Seymour Hoffman); a young mother and widow (Natalie Portman); a hillbilly (Giovanni Ribisi) living in a house of females who are starved for a man's attention; and a mountain woman (Eileen Atkins) who stumbles upon the wounded and unconscious Inman.

Ada encounters her own odd assortment of characters: Ruby (Renee Zellweger), an earthy woman who is as unrefined as Ada is cultured. It is she who helps Ada learn how to work the farm and survive the winters; Stobrod (Brendan Gleeson), Ruby's estranged father and Confederate deserter; and Teague (Ray Winstone), the self-appointed, self-serving "home guard" leader who uses the war as an excuse to terrorize the community.

Filmed in Romania, where the landscape is remarkably similar to the unspoiled south of the 1860's, Cold Mountain is not so much an epic war story as it is an intimate romance drama set during a time of war. The one exception is the incredible depiction of the Siege at Petersburg, Virginia which opens the film and shows us some of the horrors of war that Inman saw.

Minghella's greatest contribution to the film is the casting. While the leads are certainly able to carry the film, they don't have to. The supporting cast is brilliant and adds the flavor and texture that the story needs and deserves.

We see in flashbacks the shy and demure beginnings of the relationship between Inman and Ada.

It reminds us that they didn't know each other very well or for very long before Inman went to the war. And yet both clung to the possibility of the promise of a life after the war that their short time together suggested to them. It was this hope that gave Inman the drive and strength to survive. It was this hope that gave Ada the courage to continue on when all was bleak and dismal.

Hope is the backbone of our faith; the steel in our believing. Those without hope do not have the strength to endure during the hard times. This world can often be an unpleasant place in which to live. During those times, we can remind ourselves that we are not of this world. We have the promise of a life beyond this one.

For we are saved by hope: but hope that is seen is not hope: for what a man seeth, why doth he yet hope for? But if we hope for that we see not, then do we with patience wait for it. Romans 8:24-25 (KJV)

As we rest in the hope that God promises, we will find that we can endure anything that may happen in this world because we know in our hearts that it is merely temporary. We can see beyond it to that which is still to come.

Author-verified transcription

In 1999, producer Matthew Crouch released The Omega Code, a flawed and largely forgettable film which proved to be important only in that it proved there to be a ready audience for Christian-based entertainment. Surprising industry pundits, The Omega Code appeared in the top ten list of box office hits during its opening weekend.

In many ways, its sequel, Megiddo, is a far better film. A larger budget equates to better visual effects. A tighter script equates to a more cohesive and understandable story. While the acting of Megiddo, as was seen in its predecessor, can best be described as over-the-top scenery chewing of B-movie distinction, it is nonetheless consistent throughout and strangely does not seem inappropriate to the epic nature of the film's themes.

The movie follows the life of Stone Alexander (Gavin Fink, Prancer Returns), a young boy selected by the devil to be his agent or vessel on this earth. As he grows to manhood, where he is played at different ages by Noah Huntley (Event Horizon) and Michael York (The Three Musketeers), he positions himself to be the "benevolent" dictator of a New World Order. His estranged brother, David (Michael Biehn, The Terminator), just happens to be the vice-president of the United States and vigorously supports America's opposition to this demagoguery thus setting up a Cain/Abel conflict which will be ultimately settled on the Mid-Eastern plains of Megiddo where, we are told, the final battle... Armageddon... will be won.

There are many ways this film could and should be considered. First and foremost, from the perspective of scriptural accuracy, the film cannot be taken as gospel. Many liberties and shortcuts were taken in order to keep the narrative flowing and the relationships somewhat clear. Most glaring is the omission of the truth upon which the Left Behind series is based... that before Armageddon begins, all born again Christians will be transformed and leave the earth. We will not be around to experience the tribulation which leads to Satan's ultimate defeat.

The film also depicts an unrealistic and unlearned view of how governments function. The actions of this particular president and his cabinet are beyond ridiculous... even for a film of the action/adventure genre to which Megiddo aspires. As a result the film suffers from a credibility problem which, I suspect, could spill over to lessen the spiritual impact the film could carry.

There's also the question of the appropriateness of releasing a film at this time in the world's history whose main theme is one of apocalyptic devastation. Even secular studios are rethinking their projects and shelving or editing films which may invoke painful memories of September 11th's terrorism. Judging from the reaction of the largely Christian audience of the screening I attended, the ultimate reward of seeing good triumph over evil satisfactorily resolved any apprehension that may have existed about the violent, terrorist-like content of the film.

That, of course, is the major success of Megiddo. From the look of the film, it was not designed to be overly evangelistic. Unbelievers will undoubtedly roll their eyes at the fanciful depiction of Satan and the deus ex machina finale. But the scriptures prepare us for the unbelief of those who cannot see or understand spiritual matters. Megiddo appears to be made for those who already believe. It's a matter of preaching to the choir. And when all is said and done, what's wrong with that? It's a story the choir loves to hear told time and time again. Especially in these troubled and uncertain times, it is comforting to skip ahead a few chapters and remind ourselves how it will all end.

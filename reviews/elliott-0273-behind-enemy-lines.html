Behind Enemy Lines

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 30, 2001
Rating
2 stars

1 hr: 45 min

Published Web Version

Sermon / spiritual topic: Alliances; compromise; obedience; integrity; political agreements

Author-verified transcription

There are some war dramas which explore the hidden darkness lurking within men's souls; others which focus upon the courage and sacrifice of soldiers fighting for causes more important than self-preservation. Then there are films like Behind Enemy Lines which merely skim the surface of such ponderous matters in order to get to what appears to be its main purpose... to blow stuff up.

Owen Wilson (Zoolander) is cast as Lt. Burnett, a Navy flight navigator who has become disillusioned with military service because of the political factions which hamper the ability of our armed forces to do their jobs. Assigned with his pilot, Lt. Stackhouse (Gabriel Macht, American Outlaws) to a holiday reconnaissance mission, Burnett is suddenly thrust in harm's way when his plane is shot down over Bosnia by Serbian fighters. It seems that his camera has captured images which the enemy doesn't wish the world to see.

Now trapped behind enemy lines, Burnett must rely upon his own resources to survive because the cavalry is not coming. Even though the entire Serb army is searching for him, the rescue team formed from American troops must remain sitting on the sidelines waiting for the green light from a UN appointed admiral (Joaquim de Almeida, Clear and Present Danger) who doesn't wish to disturb possible peace talks.

Caught in the middle is Admiral Reigart (Gene Hackman, Heist), commanding officer on board the USS Carl Vinson which launched the ill-fated mission. He wants to bring his "boys" home, but cannot gain the authority to make it happen.

None of the actors get much opportunity to explore the nuances of their characters. Wilson spends the majority of his time sprinting through the war-ravaged landscape of Bosnia, dodging bullets and outrunning explosions. Hackman, meanwhile, stews onboard the aircraft carrier, gruffly pacing the passageways and tersely barking out orders.

Director John Moore, helming his first feature film, relies on many of the MTV (all style, little substance) camera tricks which he honed while making commercials for television. The action is fast paced and designed to be adrenaline-pumping. These scenes (an F-18 dodging a couple of heat seeking missiles; Barnett sprinting through an alleyway lined with trip mines which begin setting themselves off in a domino fashion) certainly do get our attention. But they don't lead us to anything more worthwhile. Maybe it's the times in which we live, but explosions for explosions' sake don't seem to hold the same fascination they once might have.

As the rescue efforts of the American soldiers are continually thwarted by the non-American leadership of this UN sponsored action, we see the clear illustration of the double-edged sword that is shouldered whenever an alliance is formed between two separate factions. There is strength to be gained from aligning ourselves with allies, but there also may be a weakening due to a requested compromise. Careful consideration must be given before entering into any treaty or agreement with other nations to ensure that said agreement does not work to the detriment of the needs of our own country. Placing our military forces under foreign command, no matter how friendly they may be, will not necessarily guarantee that the interests of our nation or its citizens will be served.

Know ye not, that to whom ye yield yourselves servants to obey, his servants ye are to whom ye obey; whether of sin unto death, or of obedience unto righteousness? Romans 6:16 (KJV)

If to enter into an agreement, treaty or alliance, we must set aside our integrity or sense of righteousness along with our national will, there should be no hesitation. Regardless of any existing political expediency, such an agreement should be soundly rejected.

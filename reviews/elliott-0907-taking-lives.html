Taking Lives

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 19, 2004
Rating
1½ stars

1 hr: 40 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Negative Thoughts; Heart; Edification

Author-verified transcription

At least there was a point and a message behind the bloody violence we saw in The Passion of the Christ. In Taking Lives, it is merely a matter of gore for gore's sake.

Angelina Jolie stars as Illeana Scott, an FBI special agent called in to help the Montreal police solve a string of troubling murders. Any murder is troubling, of course, but the gruesome nature of these killings indicate a sick and twisted mind at work.

All the victims have somehow been so abused that physical identification is next to impossible. Scott realizes that the killer is mutilating the bodies because he wants them to remain unidentified. She figures he is doing this because he's been adopting the identity of his victims and living their lives until he tires of them. When he does, he starts looking for his next victim (and his next persona.)

The police get their first break in the case when an eyewitness named Costa (Ethan Hawke) spots the killer in the act. Naturally, the police immediately set Costa up as bait in order to draw the killer to them. Should we be surprised when things do not go according to plan?

Scott's unconventional methodology is not well received by Paquette (Olivier Martinez), a French Canadian cop originally assigned to the case. He makes it clear that her presence is unwelcome. To make matters even worse, she becomes emotionally attracted to the primary witness which she fears will begin to cloud her judgment. Meanwhile, the killer is still on the loose and hasn't stopped killing.

In any film of this ilk, there will be many twists and turns before a conclusion is reached. A school of red herrings populate this film and, as they are swimming about, there is more than one reason to avert your eyes from the screen. Director D.J. Caruso seems to make a point of showing the mangled features of every victim.

Despite the attempts of misdirection, the identity of the killer is not that well-masked. Anyone who is familiar with the genre and is halfway perceptive should be able to figure out who did what to whom long before it is revealed.

The performances are decent enough... even if a bit stereotypical. Jolie postures and pouts as the tough female lead; Sutherland is silently menacing as the primary suspect; Hawke as the art dealer turned witness is a bit bland and nondescript; Martinez and Tcheky Karyo, as the two main French Canadian cops, both have wonderful accents.

Scott's job continually subjects her to some pretty horrific sights as she visits one grisly crime scene after another. Such exposure obviously has an effect on how she views the world around her. She even goes so far as to paste photos of the victims' remains above her bed so she might look at them as she drops off to sleep. It isn't the best way to induce pleasant dreams.

I suppose it is possible to become accustomed to just about anything but at what cost? Scriptures remind us that we are what we think.

"For as he thinketh in his heart, so is he." Proverbs 23:7a (KJV)

Hundreds of thousands of thoughts pass through our minds any given day. The thoughts that take up residency in our minds - the ones we continually dwell upon or obsess over - they are the ones that work their way into our hearts. The kind of people we are and the types of qualities we manifest are largely a result of the things we've chosen to let remain in our hearts. Therefore, we should choose wisely and cast off those thoughts and images which would not be profitable or edifying for us.

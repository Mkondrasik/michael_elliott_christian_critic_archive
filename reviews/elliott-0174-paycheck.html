Paycheck

Author-verified transcription

The prolific sci-fi author Philip K. Dick may have died over twenty years ago, but his stories live on and are even gaining popularity as Hollywood keeps dipping into his archives for their movie ideas. The latest Dick premise to be adapted into film is Paycheck, based on a short story written in 1952.

Ben Affleck stars as Michael Jennings, a computer engineer who works on highly classified and illegal projects with the condition that he will allow his memory to be erased once he's completed them. Up to now, this futuristic solution to security leaks hasn't been much of a problem. The projects themselves only take a few weeks and the technology is such that only the memory of those weeks are affected. Besides which, he's being handsomely rewarded for his troubles.

Coming off of his most lucrative project yet, he finds himself, after the memory wipe, with more than a few unanswered and troubling questions. He discovers that for some reason, he forfeited his 92 million dollar payment and instead sent himself an envelope containing 20 miscellaneous common objects.

Michael, knowing that he would not be able to remember anything about the project, sent himself the objects to serve as clues or aids to lead him back to his newly-forgotten past and discover something that he must do or undo. The fact that he's being pursued by both the FBI and hit men hired by his ex-employer isn't making matters any easier.

Director John Woo is in full, high-octane mode here, probably hoping that amid all the gunplay, explosions, and stylishly filmed high speed chases, we'll overlook the many apparent flaws in the script. A combination of Minority Report and Memento, part of the fun of watching Paycheck is to see just how the 20 objects fit into the story and help Michael to accomplish his goals.

Ben Affleck, who looks like he's posing for the cover of GQ throughout most of the film, manages to avoid getting in the way of the fun. His characterization is a bit superficial but at least it is keeping in tone with the rest of the picture.

Uma Thurman is on hand as Michael's forgotten love interest. Aaron Eckhart plays the villainous millionaire entrepreneur who hires Michael. Paul Giamatti provides the comic relief as Shorty, Michael's friend and partner.

The futuristic project at the core of Paycheck has to do with a device that can peer around the curvature of the universe and look into the future. After he built it, Michael discovered that knowing one's future is not necessarily a good thing. Spiritually speaking, that isn't true at all.

God, through His foreknowledge, has already informed us of our futures. Not specifically, or moment by moment, but in the broader sense of certain future events and what they will mean to us.

For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first: Then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air: and so shall we ever be with the Lord. 1 Thessalonians 4:16-17 (KJV)

It is our knowledge of these future events: (our Lord's return; our gathering together with him; our eternal rewards; the final and complete elimination of the devil) that fills us with hope and gives us the ability to endure the transitory trials and tribulations of this world.

Michael, at one point in the film, makes the statement, "When you tell somebody their future, you deprive them of it." In actuality, the opposite is true. When God tells us our future, He is making us a promise and establishing it so we know with full assurance that it will most definitely come to pass.

Author-verified transcription

It is a classic story. Originally a novel by Victor Hugo, Les Miserables has been adapted to fit just about every medium there is. From comic books to a Broadway musical extravaganza, Les Miserables has crept into our collective consciousness with all the subtlety of a two-ton bulldozer.

Its story line is familiar: A petty thief, Jean Valjean, is arrested for stealing a loaf of bread and thrown into prison for twenty years. The time spent behind bars has turned him into an animal. Or so he says. Upon his release, he steals again. Again he is caught and brought before his victim, a simple priest. This time, his victim covers for him, thereby giving Valjean a reason and the means to start his life anew. which he does successfully. Life would be perfect if it only wasn’t for that pesky Lieutenant Javert who year after year continues to hound Valjean with the aim to arrest him and let “justice” be served.

Without the music of the popular Broadway adaptation, this movie version both gains and loses something. While the nuances of the story are easier to follow and the sense of time and place are more firmly established, an element of passion appears to be missing from the screen. The movie, while visually stunning and mentally engaging, fails to reach our heart.

I don’t believe this to be the fault of the individual actors involved. Liam Neeson (Schindler’s List) plays the reformed thief, Jean Valjean, with so much compassion and forgiveness he makes you forget the prison animal that he once was.

Uma Thurman (Pulp Fiction) gives an outstanding performance as Fantine, a worker in Jean Valjean’s factory, who turns to a life of prostitution in order to support her child, Cosette, eventually played by the lovely Claire Danes (The Rainmaker).

Geoffery Rush (Shine) is Valjean’s nemesis, Lt. Javert. Here perhaps is where the movie loses an essential quality. Not that Mr. Rush disappoints; not at all - his performance is riveting. But his portrayal lacks an emotionally driven foundation of the character’s obsession to capture Valjean. His approach is more intellectual, more logical. Valjean broke the law, therefore Valjean must be punished. This is a valid approach, but not one that will viscerally move the audience.

Les Miserables, in this version, does not tell the story of right vs. wrong. It tells a tale about a man trying to be good vs. a man trying to be right. This is a story that must be told from the head and not from the heart. In that regard, this is a movie that can be greatly appreciated, but not loved.

Spiritually, it is easy to see this as a tale relating to all men - a tale of redemption. The priest, while securing Valjean’s freedom said to him, “I have ransomed your soul from fear and hatred. Now I give it to God.” Valjean is given a “second life”. A life that is free from the devilish influences which filled the first.

So it is with all men. God has redeemed us, paid our ransom with the price of His son. We all have an opportunity for a second life - a life immersed in the light and love of God’s presence, protected from the devilish forces at work in the world around us.

Like Valjean, what we have been given is merely an opportunity. It is our choice as to what we do with it. We could embrace it, as Valjean did. Or we could ignore it, as so many today do.

Regardless of how we choose, we should recognize that our past, like Valjean’s, will not escape us. Our pursuer, our enemy, will remind us of our past (what the Bible calls our “old man nature”) at every opportunity, just as Lt. Javert did for Jean Valjean. He does so to discourage us from living the lives God would have us live. It is a constant, daily struggle to renew our minds to the spiritual goodness of God.

As we succeed in putting the “old man” behind us, we have a godly impact upon those whose lives we touch. Yes, the redemption expressed in Les Miserables is a story for all men. It is a story that dates back to God and His son. It is a classic story.

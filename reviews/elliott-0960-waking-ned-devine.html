Waking Ned Devine

Sermon / spiritual topic: Love of Money; Gambling

Author-verified transcription

Who hasn't dreamed of winning the lottery? The reality is that few of us, if any, actually ever win. The next best thing to being the winner is, of course, to be the best friend of the one who holds the right ticket.

In Waking Ned Devine, such is the aspiration of best mates Jackie O'Shea (Ian Bannen, Braveheart) and Michael O'Sullivan (David Kelly, Fawlty Towers). They've received word that the latest lottery winner hails from their small village of Tullymore - population 52. Since they know that neither of them won, and Jackie's wife didn't win, that leaves only 49 possible candidates remaining. They are determined to find the real winner and befriend him (or her) even if it means buying everyone in town a pint to do it.

It turns out that the one and only Ned Devine has the winning ticket. But it's going to be difficult to befriend him... seeing as he's dead. The excitement of winning the lottery proved to be too much for him. Jackie found him seated in front of the television, signed winning ticket clutched firmly in his cold, cold fingers.

Since the ticket was signed, only Ned can redeem it. So... why not pretend to be Ned?

Writer/Director Kirk Jones, in his feature film debut, has put together a delightfully light and airy comedy that will tickle anyone's fancy. Much of our enjoyment of the film comes from the charming and ingratiating manner of Ian Bannen as Jackie O'Shea. If you went to central casting with the instruction to find a person who you would most likely expect to see chumming it up in an Irish pub, chances are they'd send you Ian Bannen.

David Kelly as Michael O'Sullivan is wryly amusing. His facial expressions are priceless.

The village of Tullymore is filled with rich and unique characters who, along with the picturesque Irish landscape, provide a quaint background against which the story may be told. Standouts include James Nesbitt (Welcome to Sarajevo) as Finn, the pig farmer, whose occupation you can detect odoriferously from across the room and Eileen Dromey as Lizzy Queen, the "witch" of the village who threatens to blow the whistle on the whole scheme.

Of course, at the center of the movie is the unseen, uncast "character" called the lottery. Popular beyond reason, taking money from those who can ill-afford to spend it but (in almost every case) giving nothing back in return, dasher of hopes and destroyer of dreams, the lottery remains one of the most prevalent and pervasive lies that has spread throughout our modern day society.

For every winner of the lottery, there are millions of losers. That is simply not God's way. While there is nothing evil about money per se, loving money (or exalting it above all else) is at the root of all evil.

On the U.S. currency there reads this phrase: "In God We Trust." That phrase is becoming less and less truthful. Today, more and more people are choosing to put their trust in the lottery as if it was going to be their ticket to a better way of life. There's the lie. Ned Devine was one of the few that actually won the jackpot and look what it brought him. He's dead.

The scriptures warn us of the danger:

"He that trusteth in riches shall fall: but the righteous shall flourish as a branch." Proverbs 11:28 [KJV]
"But they that will [to] be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil..." 1 Timothy 6:9-10 [KJV]

There are three godly reasons to receive money: One, in exchange for a service rendered. Two, in exchange for a product sold. Three, as a gift given in love. The lottery provides no service, it makes no product, and it certainly offers no love to those who lust after its false promises. It is too late for Ned Devine to wake up to the reality of the lottery lie. We can pray that it is not too late for us.

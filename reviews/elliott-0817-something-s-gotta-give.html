Something's Gotta Give

Author-verified transcription

What's age got to do with it? That seems to be the question on writer/director Nancy Meyers' mind in her new ribald comedy, Something's Gotta Give. In it, a 63 year old man only dates women under 30. That is, until he begins to fall for the mother of one of them. But he's not the only one interested in her. His 30-something doctor also gives him competition for her attention.

Jack Nicholson stars as Harry Sanborn, the aging lothario with a weak heart whose affinity for younger women has kept him a bachelor, safe from any kind of real emotional commitment. When Marin, his latest young piece of eye candy (Amanda Peet), invites him to the Hamptons for a romantic weekend, their tryst is interrupted by Marin's mother, Erica (Diane Keaton), a successful playwright who owns the beach house.

Not surprisingly, the awkwardness of the situation leads to a bit of stress and a subsequent heart attack for old Harry. His young doctor (Keanu Reeves) becomes smitten with Erica and suggests that Harry stay at the beach house while recuperating so he would be close by in case of a relapse.

It is during his convalescence that Harry begins to recognize, with no small degree of surprise, just how attracted he is to a woman who is closer to his own age.

Diane Keaton is wonderful in the role. Smart and sexy in her maturity, she communicates an appeal that transcends age. She really is perfect for the part, which is understandable seeing as it was written with her in mind.

Jack Nicholson is a good match for her as they build a nice comedic chemistry between them. With solid support from Amanda Peet, Keanu Reeves and Frances McDormand (who plays Erica's sister), Something's Gotta Give is an adult-oriented comedy able to generate plenty of laughs.

While it is true that many of the jokes come from sexual references or situations, it would be a mistake to think that there isn't a deeper or richer vein of comedy that gets mined here. Neither Keaton nor Nicholson take themselves so seriously that they aren't willing to explore the humor that can be found in the process of growing older.

One of the elements that makes aging more tolerable is to do it with someone else. Harry, for all his womanizing, runs the risk of growing old alone unless he can learn to invest an emotional, mental, and physical commitment into a relationship instead of a series of dates.

The film brings to my mind the opening stanza of Robert Browning's classic poem Rabbi Ben Ezra:

Grow old along with me! The best is yet to be, The last of life, for which the first was made: Our times are in His hand Who saith, "A whole I planned, Youth shows but half; trust God: see all nor be afraid!"

God designed this life to be fully experienced. It is true that aging does have its challenges, but it is part of the ride. And that ride is certainly more enjoyable when you're sitting beside someone for the duration.

Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing, and obtaineth favour of the LORD. Proverbs 18:22 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

There are some problems that cannot be solved by tackling them alone. Home Run tells the story of a successful minor league ballplayer whose life is much less successful off the field. It is a life-affirming story of addiction, forgiveness, recovery, and deliverance that will resonate with many people who have overcome challenges with the help of a support group.

Cory Brand (Scott Elrod) is a popular, league-leading All-Star for the Denver Grizzlies baseball team. What people don’t know is that his childhood was anything but idyllic. Raised by an alcoholic and abusive father, Cory has buried the pain of his past by over-indulging in alcohol and living up to his wild-child image. But the emptiness within him remains.

When his drinking affects his play on the field, his team has no choice but to suspend him. In an attempt to remove him from the public eye and allow for his PR image to be rebuilt, his agent (Vivica A. Fox) sends him back to his hometown in Oklahoma and there arranges for him to coach a little league team and enroll in a 12-step program. It is here, in the slower-paced, small town environment, that Cory begins to discover that his personal demons have a greater hold on him than he originally realized.

Scott Elrod certainly looks the part of a professional ballplayer, in a Jose Canseco kind of way. But he also provides a very nice, restrained job of a troubled man living in the spotlight who is almost able to hide his suppressed pain and bitterness from the world. He is given ample support from the cast, all of whom (including the kids) give natural, honest portrayals that serve the story and the underlying message of the film. It is clearly a Christian oriented film, and delivers its view in a tasteful, non-threatening, and entertaining way. Kudos to those involved.

It is difficult to kick a habit. It is several times harder to kick an addiction, as Cory learns in the course of the film. It often feels to those trying that it is an impossibility… that there is no way out and no light at the end of the dark tunnel they are in. And, if they are trying to do it on their own, through the sheer force of will, they are usually right.

“For I know that good itself does not dwell in me, that is, in my sinful nature.[a] For I have the desire to do what is good, but I cannot carry it out.” Romans 7:18 (NIV)

This is an underlying principle in the Twelve Step Program originally produced by Alcoholics Anonymous. That the addiction is beyond the individual’s ability to control and therefore the only way to resist the addiction is to turn one’s “control” over to a higher power.

God is able to provide strength when we are weak. He is able to embolden our spirit and renew our soul. We may not possess the power to stop an addictive behavior but with God’s help, we can claim the power to do just that.

“But he said to me, “My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.” 2 Corinthians 12:9 (NIV)

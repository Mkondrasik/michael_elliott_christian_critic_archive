Author-verified transcription

There’s no denying that parenthood is a tough job. Ask any parent. But let us imagine how difficult it would be if the child’s intelligence was greater than that of the parent. I Am Sam asks us to consider this… but not in the way we might expect because the child in question is of normal intelligence.

Sam Dawson (Sean Penn, Sweet and Lowdown) is a mentally challenged individual who, after having sexual relations with a homeless woman, conceived a child (Dakota Fanning, 24 Hours). After giving birth, the woman immediately leaves the scene. Sam must raise his daughter, whom he names Lucy (after a Beatles song), alone. This he does with the help of his next door neighbor (Dianne Wiest, The Horse Whisperer) and a quartet of similarly challenged friends.

When Lucy turns seven, the age where Sam’s mental development became arrested, social workers step in to remove the child from his custody, citing that Sam is incapable of providing for Lucy’s needs. Sam turns to lawyer Rita Harrison (Michelle Pfeiffer, What Lies Beneath) who is shamed into taking his case pro bono.

That I Am Sam is shameless in its emotional manipulation should come as no surprise seeing how it is directed by Jessie Nelson, the screenwriter of such tearjerkers as Stepmom and The Story of Us. But its obvious sentimentality does not mean that the film isn’t effective. I Am Sam works largely because the loving relationship between Sam and his daughter Lucy is so heartwarmingly real.

Sean Penn walks a very fine line in his portrayal of a man who has the mental capacity of a child. He takes what could have easily become a caricature of questionable taste and turns it into a complete and controlled study of a man all too aware of his own limitations.

Michelle Pfeiffer is not quite as believable in her role of legal beagle Rita Harrison. She seems to be far more comfortable with the aspects that explores Rita’s failing marriage and emotionally strained relations with her son than with the profession in which her character is supposed to excel.

If there’s a reason that the word “precious” is so often used as an adjective to describe a child, that reason is Dakota Fanning. I’m not even related to her but after watching her in this film, I wanted to approach everyone outside the theater with pictures in hand so I could brag about how special she is. “Adorable” doesn’t do her justice.

If there’s a caveat to be seen it is in the direction of Jessie Nelson. Her directorial hand appears far too frequently in the way she uses hand held “shaky cam” shots, abrupt cuts and unusual edits. She oftentimes gets in the way of telling her story by bringing our attention to the telling of it rather than keeping the focus on the story itself.

Credit the work of Sean Penn and Dakota Fanning for elevating the film above the schmaltz. I Am Sam rides totally upon the strength and believability of their relationship. We buy into it completely.

In addition to his challenges, or perhaps because of them, Sam demonstrates attributes which we would be wise to emulate. Among them are his joy and passion for life; his loyalty to his friends and his willingness to include them in his life; the obvious love he has for his daughter; and his trust or faith that everything will work out because he follows the rules he knows and expects fairness. He also knows when to ask for help.

There comes a time in each of our lives when we must admit to ourselves and to God that we don’t have all the answers… that we are aware of our limitations… that we need help. It’s then, at that moment when we recognize our own childlike helplessness, that we are at our most mature.

And said, Verily I say unto you, Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. Matthew 18:3 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

Two Zorros for the price of one. You just can't beat that deal. Especially when those Zorros are played by the immensely talented Anthony Hopkins (The Edge) and the immensely appealing Antonio Banderas (Evita).

In the early 1800's, the land that would one day become California is under oppressive rule by the Spaniards. A masked man known as Zorro fights for the people who are too weak and weary to fight for themselves. But this mystery man, as portrayed by Mr. Hopkins is getting older. After completing what he promises his beautiful wife to be Zorro's last ride, he is captured in his home by the ruling government. His wife is killed, his young daughter stolen from him. He is thrown into prison and promptly forgotten.

Twenty years later, he escapes to find that the people are once again in need of a Zorro. So... he makes one. Starting with the raw material provided by a charming thief (Mr. Banderas), he passes his skills and knowledge onto the younger man and it isn't long before Zorro rides again.

Mr. Hopkins appears to be having the time of his life as the aging swordsman and protector of the people. As he explains to his young protégé, to be effective as Zorro, it is not enough to be trained as a fighter. One must also learn how to be a nobleman. Mr. Hopkins proves himself to be expert in both categories.

Mr. Banderas is marvelous, providing a vitality and a self-deprecating humor which helps to make this movie so much fun to watch. Whether engaging in swordplay, wordplay, or even foreplay (cleverly disguised as the tango), Mr. Banderas excels - much to our delight.

Catherine Zeta-Jones (The Phantom) is absolutely breathtaking as Elena, a woman in the unique position of being both the daughter as well as the romantic interest of Zorro. We're talking of two different Zorros of course, and Miss Zeta-Jones is well worthy of both.

Stuart Wilson (Crossworlds) takes on the bad guy role of Don Rafael Montero, the Spanish governor driven from office only to return many years later to stake claim on this prosperous land.

Director Martin Campbell (Goldeneye) keeps a tight rein on the action, never letting the pace falter. He also keeps the mood light via the many humorous exchanges, most notably in the banter between the two Zorros.

The spiritual lesson of the film can be found in the intensive training that Mr. Banderas' character underwent in order to properly act as Zorro. Just putting on a costume does not make one a super hero. Natural ability, no matter how great, is not enough to elevate us to the highest levels of achievement. If we want to excel in our field of study, it will take work, practice, training, discipline, and time.

The same is true in any enterprise. Professional athletes have to continually practice in order to stay at the top of their game. Top level executives still attend conferences and seminars in order to stay sharp and informed as to their profession.

If this is true for our professional lives, what about our spiritual lives? How are we to excel in the spiritual competition if we do not faithfully practice living, speaking and acting spiritually?

It would seem that we understand the concept of training and practice in every field except the spiritual. And yet it is this field that carries the gravest consequences. In sports, failure to train may lose you the game. In business, it may lose you your job. In Zorro's case, he could lose his life. But when speaking spiritually, non-practice, (or the wrong practice) could result in the forfeiture of eternal life or its rewards. That's a consequence no one intentionally pays.

Zorro had to diligently practice with his sword before engaging the enemy. We too must be as diligent to become familiar with the sword of God, which is His written Word. As we become expert in wielding it, the mark we shall leave will have greater impact than just the initial "Z".

Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 Timothy 2:15 (KJV)

In the scriptures, the number ten figuratively represents ordinal perfection or the divine completion of a cycle. This is an interesting piece of trivia because, having now reached the tenth installment of the Star Trek series of films, we do get the distinct impression that this franchise is drawing to a close. No doubt there will be more films to follow so perhaps it is just our interest level that is beginning to wane.

In Nemesis, Jean Luc Picard (Patrick Stewart, X-Men) and the rest of the Enterprise crew are on the verge of going their separate ways but before they do, they manage to squeeze in one more opportunity to save Earth from global destruction.

Their mission starts innocently enough. On the way to Betazed to participate in a marriage ceremony, the Enterprise is ordered to cross the Neutral Zone and enter Romulan space because of the possibility of obtaining a diplomatic peace with this warlike people. After years of conflict, Shinzon (Tom Hardy, Black Hawk Down), the new Romulan praetor, appears to be extending his hand to the Federation in friendship. It is a hand that is both new and strangely familiar.

It turns out that Shinzon is a clone... Picard's clone to be exact. The product of an abandoned Romulan plot to infiltrate Star Fleet, Shinzon has not only survived, he has managed to rise to stage a coup against the Romulan Senate who banished him to the dilithium slave mines of Remus. Now, after years of a tortuous existence, he comes face to face with the source of his DNA in what must be the ultimate in narcissistic competition: Pitting one's self against a different version of one's self.

If one doppleganger wasn't enough, Data (Brett Spiner, The Master of Disguise) is given an identity crisis of his own. An away team has discovered a dismembered but functioning positronic android which can only be considered an early prototype of the Enterprise's science officer.

Director Stuart Baird (Executive Decision) is re-treading on familiar ground. As with this year's James Bond film, there are elements in Nemesis which are borrowed from past episodes or movies.

As should be expected, the cast wear their characters as comfortably as an old sweater. Although some of the special effects are grand spectacles, the overall production has the feel of a made for TV movie, a quality which all the best Trekkie films have in common.

With two pair of "cloned" entities being featured in this film, the dominant theme is obvious. What makes a person unique? What percentage of us is ingrained or genetically programmed? How much is learned or a result of our experiences? These are questions which cause Picard more than a little troubling introspection as he sees himself (through his clone's behavior) capable of unspeakable evil.

Data makes the observation that our character is defined by our desire and drive to improve ourselves... To be better than we are. It is a fine sentiment, but it misses the spiritual point.

What the film never contemplates is the one element which makes a man or woman truly unique in all the world. That element is none other than the spirit of God which dwells within a believer. With God's spirit within, a man is automatically elevated to a new level. He gains the ability to renew his mind and understand truths beyond the limits of human comprehension. Above all, he now has the ability to resist evil which is born of a spiritual source.

For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places. Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand. Ephesians 6:12-13 (KJV)

It is not simply our genetic makeup which determines who we are or who we will be. Rather, it is the spirit of God which is present within all who believe.

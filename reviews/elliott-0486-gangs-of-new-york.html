Author-verified transcription

"We were born of blood and tribulation... So was our city." This is a line spoken in Gangs of New York, Martin Scorsese's epic look at the tumultuous birth of a place called New York City. In this Hollywood history lesson we learn that America's greatest city was forged from the fires of greed, racial intolerance, and violent actions.

Building towards the infamous draft riots of 1863, the film begins in gang-ridden New York City circa 1846. Two particular gangs, the Dead Rabbits (Irish immigrants) and the Natives (American born citizens), vie for dominant control over a section of lower NYC called the Five Points. After a violent and bloody conflagration, the Natives, led by Bill "The Butcher" Cutter (Daniel Day-Lewis, Last of the Mohicans), win their victory by killing the moral leader of their opposition (Liam Neeson, Schindler's List). In an act of compassion and respect for his opponent, he allows the son of the leader, a young lad of five or six, to live.

Sixteen years later, that lad, going by the name of Amsterdam (Leonardo DiCaprio, The Beach), returns to Five Points. Originally intent on revenge for his father's death, he quickly realizes that Bill now holds sway over all that is important. Above the law, above the politicians, Bill Cutter is Five Points and anyone who wishes anything must go through him.

Amsterdam manages to infiltrate Cutter's camp and gain his confidence to become his right hand man and surrogate son. The Dead Rabbits gang is outlawed. Amsterdam's father is long dead. The only person left with the "sand" to stand up to Bill "The Butcher" has apparently joined his camp. But in a world where alliances are formed and broken more easily than a bunch of twigs, who knows what is taking place beneath the surface?

While DiCaprio does a fine job playing the conflicted Amsterdam, this film belongs to Daniel Day-Lewis who presents one of the most complex and richly shaded villains in recent memory. Also in the cast is Cameron Diaz (Charlie's Angels) as a kindly prostitute skilled in the art of the con, Jim Broadbent (Iris) as Boss Tweed, NY's political force, and Henry Thomas (All The Pretty Horses) as yet another gang member scratching out an existence for himself in the violent world of the Five Points.

And violent it is. At this time in history, power is won and kept through the use of lethal force and there is plenty on display throughout the film. This is the fifth time in ten years that director Martin Scorsese (Casino) and production designer Dante Ferretti (Bringing Out The Dead) have worked together and their shared vision of what Manhattan might have looked like over 150 years ago is magnificently realized.

Scorsese paints his cinematic canvas with broad strokes, showcasing political graft, racial intolerance, sexual diversity, and the religious fervor of fanatical men. In an effective scene, we see three characters on opposing sides all giving thanks to God and asking for his blessing just prior to one of the bloodiest riots to take place on US soil.

Mob violence and the confusion and chaos it brings cannot be laid at the feet of God our Father. The frenzy that accompanies such actions is often said to have a life of its own. This is true and there's no doubt that it is spiritual. It just isn't of God.

For God is not the author of confusion, but of peace, as in all churches of the saints. I Corinthians 14:33 (KJV)

We can pray all we want for God's blessing and protection. But if we insist upon taking part in devilish and destructive behavior, we shouldn't expect those prayers to be answered.

Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts. James 4:3 (KJV)

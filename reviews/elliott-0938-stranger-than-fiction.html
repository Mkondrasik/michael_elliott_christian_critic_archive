Stranger than Fiction

Sermon / spiritual topic: Death; Awareness

Author-verified transcription

Stranger than Fiction is a strange film to categorize. Is it a comedy or a tragedy? According to a literary expert, that all depends on whether the hero lives or dies... and that is the central question that drives the plot.

Harold Crick (Will Ferrell) is a singularly unspectacular individual. A regimented IRS auditor, his life has devolved into a series of predictable patterns. He even counts his toothbrush strokes as he performs his morning ablution. One day, Harold begins hearing (in his head) a play-by-play commentary as he goes about his dreary life.

The voice is none other than novelist Kay Eiffel (Emma Thompson) whose latest work features a main character named Harold Crick. As she writes her story, the real Crick hears her voice giving the narration as he lives it out. It's a bit weird and he has trouble assimilating this new wrinkle in his otherwise wrinkle-free life.

But it is when her narrative reveals his impending death that Harold becomes obsessed with trying to find out what is happening to him and how to stop it before his fictional death becomes his reality.

He is assisted by Professor Jules Hilbert (Dustin Hoffman), the aforementioned literary expert who tries to guide Crick into knowing whose story he's a character in and what may lay in store for him. He is given a reason for living when he meets an attractive, tax protesting baker named Anna (Maggie Gyllenhaal) whose returns he is called to audit.

This odd film has many charms thanks to its inventive screenplay by Zach Helm, interesting direction by Marc Forster, and solid performances by its cast... including an amazing one by Emma Thompson.

Thompson magnifies the doubts and insecurities of an author with writer's block and manifests them physically for comic effect without ever once losing the soul of the character. It is a brilliant, award-worthy performance.

Will Ferrell underplays beautifully here, playing a man who comfortably goes through the motions of living his life until he realizes that he's about to lose it. His deadpan reactions are flawless and, as Buster Keaton proved more than a half century ago, he shows us that comedy can coexist with pathos.

Both Dustin Hoffman and Maggie Gyllenhaal provide ample support with Queen Latifah showing up in a minor role. But it is Thompson and Ferrell who sell this odd premise and walk the fine line between comedy and tragedy which makes this film such a rewarding experience.

Man is a unique creature in that he is aware of his mortality and can make decisions based on that awareness. Crick is not only aware that he's going to die but, thanks to the narrative playing out in his head, he also becomes aware of when, where and how that death would occur. His decisions therefore become even more pronounced and poignant.

Jesus Christ was once faced with a similar decision. Through his study of the prophecies of old and by revelation from his Father God, he knew that he would have to die.

He willingly allowed himself to be the sacrifice for all men counting our lives to be more important than his own.

As the Father knoweth me, even so know I the Father: and I lay down my life for the sheep. John 10:15 (KJV)

Cellular

Author-verified transcription

Cell phones have become such a familiar part of the American landscape that it's no surprise to see one finally starring in its own film. Cellular uses the hand held communication device as the central "character" in an entertaining though largely implausible action/adventure.

Science teacher and LA housewife Jessica Martin (Kim Basinger) is roughly abducted by a band of unknown thugs. Her only hope of rescue lies in if she can manage to utilize the smashed extension phone that is in the attic where she's being held hostage. By tapping some wires together she is finally able to make a random call. A young man named Ryan (Chris Evans), who is described by his ex-girl-friend (Jessica Biel) as "irresponsible, self-centered and totally childish, is about to get the strangest "wrong number" of his life.

At first skeptical of Jessica's pleas for help, Ryan stays on the line long enough to become convinced of her plight. From that time on, he is drawn into an increasingly desperate race against time to save Jessica and her family from certain death.

Director David R. Ellis (Final Destination 2) crafts a thoroughly enjoyable picture out of cheese cloth. It is at times thrilling, chilling, comical, and completely illogical but after the dog days of summer, it is refreshing to watch a picture that knows how to entertain.

Chris Evans, in what should be a star-making performance, is a likable lead. His Ryan is a charming slacker who is comfortably at home in the laid back LA beachside environment. Watching him "morph" into action hero mode is a large part of the movie's attraction.

Kim Basinger adeptly handles the emotionally delicate role of the terrorized victim. While essentially a "one-note" performance, she is quite believable and balances the film's humor with the proper amount of tension.

The film is aided by the steady work of William H. Macy as a cop nearing retirement who is a few steps behind Ryan in figuring out what is going on and why. Jason Statham is a threatening presence and makes an excellent bad guy.

The script was developed from a story idea by Larry Cohen (Phone Booth) and reworked into a screenplay by first timer Chris Morgan. Though flawed in its logic and plausibility, it is nonetheless clever in how it incorporates all the features and frustrations that cell phone users know all too well: Loss of signal strength, dying batteries, call waiting, and crossed lines all become inconveniences that take on life or death meaning as Ryan frantically tries to keep Jessica on the line.

Who among us would take the action Ryan did - breaking the law and risking his life - for the sake of a stranger? And yet that is what saviors do.

"Very rarely will anyone die for a righteous man, though for a good man someone might possibly dare to die, but God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us." Romans 5:7-8 (NIV)

Ephesians 2 reminds us that, in our original state of sin, we were alienated from God and strangers to the covenants of promise. Despite that, Christ offered himself as a sacrifice so that we might live.

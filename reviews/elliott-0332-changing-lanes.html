Changing Lanes

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 12, 2002
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 53 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Romans 12:17-181 Peter 3:91 Thessalonians 5:15

Translation: NIV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Peace; forgiveness; retaliation; Golden Rule

Author-verified transcription

Unbeknownst to them, the very different lives of Doyle Gipson and Gavin Banek are set on a collision course. Unfortunately, when that collision occurs on New York's FDR Drive, it is a particularly inopportune time for both of them. What follows in the story of Changing Lanes is an escalating battle between two desperately flawed men who, despite their increasingly stupid actions, are painted as inherently good people.

Doyle (Samuel L. Jackson, The Matrix) is a recovering alcoholic who was on his way to a court hearing in a last ditch attempt to keep his wife and kids from leaving town. Gavin (Ben Affleck, Pearl Harbor) is a young, brash, high-powered attorney who was also on his way to court. His reason: to file papers which will ensure that his firm will remain the legal, if not honorable, trustee for a multimillion dollar estate.

As a result of their fender bender, both fail in their appointed tasks. Doyle is late for his hearing and the unsympathetic judge sides with the wife. Gavin drops a crucially important file at the scene of the accident which he had so blithely left with a wave of a hand and a smarmy "Better luck next time" comment.

Of course Doyle picks up the file and uses it to strike back at Gavin, whom he blames for making him miss his court date. Gavin retaliates with a little computer chicanery to shut off Doyle's entire financial life and history. From there, things get ugly pretty fast as these two characters play a little game of one-upmanship to see who can ruin whose life the fastest.

Director Roger Michell (Notting Hill) is in the driver's seat for this ride and manages to avoid most of the potholes which are sprinkled throughout the script. The movie he ends up presenting to us seems more interested in stimulating our intellect with the moral or ethical issues it raises than in pulling us into the emotional drama of the story.

Changing Lanes is benefited from a fine, accomplished cast. Samuel L. Jackson, as a man who has hit bottom and is struggling to regain some order to his life, keeps his rage and sense of injustice just barely concealed. When it erupts, it is totally believable. Ben Affleck is also well suited for his role as a pampered, self-satisfied attorney who buries his awareness of the less than honest practices of his firm under a practiced smile and a smug attitude.

The supporting cast shines, most especially Sidney Pollack (Eyes Wide Shut) as Gavin's father-in-law and partner in the law firm to William Hurt (A.I.: Artificial Intelligence) as Doyle's AA sponsor and sounding board.

What is most striking about Changing Lanes is how easily these characters could have avoided the conflict at any time. By being so immersed in themselves and their problems, and by resorting to retaliation rather than forgiveness, their troubles keep escalating to the point where they nearly destroy themselves.

Some have criticized the Hollywood "pat" ending as being a weakness. I disagree as the ending helps to give a positive context to the message it wishes to deliver. That message is a familiar one. It is the oldest "rule" in the book. the golden rule. Do not repay anyone evil for evil. Be careful to do what is right in the eyes of everybody. If it is possible, as far as it depends on you, live at peace with everyone. Romans 12:17-18 (NIV) Had Doyle and Gavin simply took a few seconds to remember to "do unto others what you would have them do unto you" all unpleasantness could have been avoided.

The benefits of behaving cordially and peaceably with all men as we live in this world together are countless. Once on that highway, there will never be a need or reason to change lanes.

Head in the Clouds

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
September 17, 2004
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Romans 12:1Matthew 5:14-16Galatians 6:10

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Service; sacrifice; witness; selflessness; helping others

Author-verified transcription

A romantic melodrama is played out against the backdrop of wartime Europe in Head in the Clouds.

Guy (Stuart Townsend) is an industrious and serious Irish lad studying at Cambridge University and minding his own business when suddenly Gilda Besse (Charlize Theron) barges into his dorm room to escape the detection of school authorities. Guy is a perfect gentleman, allowing her to stay and protecting the reputation and continued enrollment of the guy she had snuck in to see. He apparently was not similarly concerned about her reputation. He already knew that it was beyond salvage.

An American heiress, Gilda was notorious on campus for her sexual permissiveness and free spirited ways. After their platonic night together, followed by a not so platonic romp the next time they meet, Gilda leaves Guy to his studies while she travels the globe.

When she finally settles in Paris, Guy has graduated and is now teaching in England. She summons him to come to her side and they begin their passionate affair in earnest. By now, Gilda is an artist of sorts and is living with Mia (Penelope Cruz), a Spanish born stripper and nude model who is studying to be a nurse. Guy moves in and for a brief time the three are inseparable.

But this sexually steamy arrangement won’t last for long. The politically minded Guy is being drawn towards the civil war in Spain and Mia feels compelled to return to her homeland and offer her nursing services. The carefree and decidedly apolitical Gilda doesn’t understand their passion for this war and considers their departure a personal form of treason.

Six years later, Guy returns to Paris as an operative for British intelligence. By now the Nazis have marched into France and the resourceful Gilda has protected her lifestyle by taking up with a high ranking German officer.

This is really the kind of story that calls for glycerin drops be placed in the actresses’ eyes for the weepy close-ups. Remarkably, writer/director John Duigan doesn’t go for the over-the-top, hankie-wringing melodramatic treatment that the story itself demands. But the film doesn’t really work without it.

There’s no romantic chemistry at play between the actors and no strong sense of a character arc with the central character of Gilda. While she is a much different person at the end of the film than when we first meet her, we never see her go through the transformation.

Granted, the film can be pretty to look at with its period costuming and various hair styles. But with a two hour running time, we need a bit more than that to hold our interest.

Gilda’s initial outlook on life is supremely selfish. Though she is aware of events taking place in the world she doesn’t see how they affect her and so ignores them in favor of more immediate gratification.

We are taught to reach outside of ourselves and help others if by no other means than by extending to the hope of the gospel of Christ. The superficial pleasures of this life are meaningless compared to the good we can perform in the service of God.

I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. Romans 12:1 (KJV)

To be a living sacrifice simply means to live one’s life according to God’s standards - to be his witness in this world and a light that will shine for all men. To do that means that we must be willing to extend ourselves beyond the insulation of our own personal shell.

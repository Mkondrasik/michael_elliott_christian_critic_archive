Charlie and the Chocolate Factory

Author-verified transcription

Filmmakers who choose to remake familiar films do so with the knowledge that they run the risk of their work being unfavorably compared to the original. And when the original is a beloved classic, the risk is heightened to the point where success is improbable at best.

Such is the case with Hollywood's latest remake. Roald Dahl's novel, Charlie and the Chocolate Factory, has already received a movie treatment which has delighted families for decades. Tim Burton's new version may win its fans but it will fall well short of winning the hearts of those who fondly remember the whimsical 1971 film with Gene Wilder.

In that film, Wilder played Willy Wonka as a whacky eccentric but one with a heart and an intelligence that was readily apparent. In the newer version, one that is reportedly closer to the tone of the original novel, Johnny Depp adds an unsettling creepiness to Wonka that will surely inspire comparisons to Michael Jackson.

The story starts out the same in both. Willy Wonka, who had closed the gates of his amazing chocolate factory many years earlier in order to protect his corporate secrets, decides to hold a contest where five random children will be permitted a behind-the-scenes tour of the magical place.

The children who find the special "golden ticket" are: obese Augustus Gloop, gum chewing Violet Beauregard, spoiled Veruca Salt, media addicted Mike Teevee, and good-hearted Charlie Bucket (Freddie Highmore).

They, along with one adult chaperone for each, explore the wonders of Wonka's factory, complete with chocolate waterfall, glass elevator, and the handiwork of the Oompa-Loompas (Deep Roy). At the end of the tour, Wonka promises that one child will win a prize beyond imagination.

The visuals are interesting although surprisingly not as inventive as today's technology would allow. The limited use of CGI (when it appears) feels out of place and it is debatable whether the decision to digitally replicate actor Deep Roy to 'play' all of the Oompa Loompas was the wisest.

Nevertheless, there are elements of the remake that are the equal to its predecessor. The film begins with great promise, easily matching the style, tone and humor of the original. But when Depp appears and introduces us to his character's odd backstory, the film begins to derail and never fully recovers.

What is missing from the remake is the effervescent joyfulness of Wilder and the individual moral lessons we're given as each of the invited guests succumb to the temptations of Wonka's world. Instead we are treated to Depp's odd characterization explained by a dysfunctional family relationship, clever musical numbers that are instantly forgettable, and a blanket 'family is important' message that, while nice to hear, seems to be a touch anti-climatic.

While I prefer the original film, I do appreciate some things about the remake - especially the importance placed upon the family. God designed the family to provide support, encouragement, guidance and comfort for the individual.

God setteth the solitary in families: he bringeth out those which are bound with chains: but the rebellious dwell in a dry land. Psalms 68:6 (KJV)

One reason Wonka turned out so weird in this film is because he grew up without having the benefit of the reproof and correction from people who loved and cared for him and his development.

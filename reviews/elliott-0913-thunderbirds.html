Author-verified transcription

Aficionados of early children's television may remember watching Thunderbirds, a British TV series featuring marionettes that was popular in the 60s. Filmmakers have decided to cut the strings of the puppets and put live actors in their place for an updated version of this Spy Kids precursor.

Alan Tracy (Brady Corbet) is the youngest member of a family of international rescuers known as Thunderbirds. While his father and brothers spend their days behind the controls of their awesome rescue machines saving people from one disaster after another, Alan is stuck in school and can only dream of one day becoming a "Thunderbird."

Their home is Tracy Island, a secluded and secret tropical paradise where the rescuers keep their gadgets, vehicles and laboratory equipment. Alan, his school friend Ferment (Soren Fulton) and his gal pal TinTin (Vanessa Anne Hudgens) are left behind yet again as the Thunderbirds respond to another emergency - this one in outer space.

Little do they know that the emergency is a trap set by their long time nemesis The Hood (Ben Kingsley). With the Thunderbirds trapped in space, there is no one to stop The Hood from carrying out his evil plan to ruin the do-gooder image and reputation of the Tracys. With the lives of his family and the future of the Thunderbirds at risk, Alan must find the courage and the resourcefulness to save the day.

From what I understand, the appeal of the original series was in the imaginative machines employed by the Tracy's to achieve their hair-raising and last minute rescues. Young boys will be glad to know that the film keeps in that same spirit by presenting powerful vehicles and devices filled with switches, throttles, dials, and levers all of which certainly do something. (What is not important - it is better not to get too bogged down in detail when there's a world that needs saving).

Thunderbirds is a fantasy adventure that will tickle the imagination of young boys. Young girls may not get quite as much out of the film although TinTin and the pleasantly pink Lady Penelope (Sophia Myles) represent "girl power" with considerable aplomb. For the most part, director Jonathan Frakes keeps things straightforwardly simple and age appropriate.

There is an occasional lapse or two where an adult innuendo is allowed to pass through but these are limited and relatively innocuous.

The actors playing the Tracy siblings don't get much time or opportunity to present characterizations that would distinguish them from their puppets counterparts. Sophia Myles and Ron Cook as the unflappable Lady P and her British manservant Parker provide most of the humor. What Academy Award winner Sir Ben Kingsley is doing in this film is anybody's guess but he avoids embarrassing himself and actually makes for a decent, imposing villain within the framework of this children's film.

Throughout the film, Alan is shown to be impatient, impetuous and unwilling to take the steps that would allow him to build the knowledge and experience needed to operate the complex Thunderbird crafts. That the film didn't explore this theme is a disappointment because it would be an important lesson to communicate. Our young people are growing up in a world that promotes instant gratification. This is an unhealthy view of life. Few things come to us without our first making the effort to obtain them.

But this I say, He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; and he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully. 2 Corinthians 9:6 (KJV)

This verse of scripture is generally used in regard to financial wealth but the analogy holds true to all of life. As we apply ourselves towards any goal and diligently work to achieve it, we will, in due season, receive the fruit of our labor. Granted, it may have not have been as exciting an adventure but I would have preferred to see Alan stay in school and learn the subjects he needed to take before he was given the opportunity to take command of any of the Thunderbird machines. That's a lesson our young people need to hear.

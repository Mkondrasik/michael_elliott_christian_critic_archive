Author-verified transcription

As the end credits state, "The Tao of Steve" is "based on a story by Duncan North." It is "based on an idea by Duncan North." In fact, it is "based on Duncan North." The sister screen writing team of Jenniphr and Greer Goodman took the unusual philosophy of their friend Mr. North and made it into the centerpiece of a surprisingly likable romantic comedy.

"The Tao of Steve" is indeed a philosophic treatise. However, the enlightenment it brings is not designed to elevate one's consciousness to a higher plane. Rather it exists as a means for ordinary guys to "bag chicks." And lots of them. This philosophy is put into quite successful application by the film's protagonist, Dex (Donal Logue, "The Patriot"), an overweight, underachieving, pot-smoking slacker who is, it would appear, truly irresistible to women.

He credits the attraction to his belief in the Tao (pronounced "dow") of Steve; which promotes the adoption of qualities exemplified in the archetypal male, Steve McQueen (or Steve Austin, or Steve McGarrett). This "Steve" quality is not contained in the name but rather the attitude: one of mysterious aloofness. The "Steves" mentioned never seem interested in getting the girl, and yet they always manage to end up with her.

Dex proselytizes this unusual approach of appealing indifference to the various males who approach him for advice, getting into particular depth with one of his roommates (Kimo Wills, "Lush") who, truth be known, is the opposite of a "Steve." He is a "Stu."

The Tao of Steve, as is true with most philosophies, may appear to be effective for a period of time. But not every situation will fit the mold cast by the philosopher. In the case of Dex, the challenge comes in the guise of an attractive drummer/opera set designer named Syd (co-writer Greer Goodman, in an excellent debut performance.) So intriguing an individual is Syd that Dex has trouble maintaining his Steve-like nonchalance. His belief structure is threatened and the one girl he might really care for may be beyond his reach.

Mr. Logue, who put on considerable weight in order to play this role, makes for a perfect Dex. Spending his day playing pool or Frisbee golf with his roommates, while spouting his "Steve-isms," he combines an obvious intelligence with a natural and childlike charm. He even works part time as a kindergarten aide because he gets along with young children so well. Part of the Tao of Steve is to do something excellent in front of the woman, thus establishing one's "sex-worthiness." Dex's excellence is his immature playfulness.

The script by the Goodman sisters is simplistic in structure but is also sprinkled with a rare wit and an intellectualism which is clearly self-aware. The film's conversation topics range from Kierkegaard and Don Giovanni to Josie and the Pussycats and The Bugaloos.

In one such banter between Dex and Syd, Dex theorizes on the deeper meaning of Don Giovanni's life, which he surmises was based on the art of seduction. Syd stops Dex cold by pronouncing that Don Giovanni "slept with thousands of women because he was afraid that he would never be loved by one."

Scriptures warn us of the dangers of heeding too closely the various theories and logical analyses presented by man:

"Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ." Colossians 2:8 (KJV)

The logic we employ to gain understanding of the world around us must be Word-based and not that which is concocted or devised by man. In the original language, the word "spoil" in the above scripture means to "seduce" or "lead away." There is only one "philosophy" which will keep us on track with the thinking of God. It is the Word of God which reflects His heart and mind and which God commands us to study and believe.

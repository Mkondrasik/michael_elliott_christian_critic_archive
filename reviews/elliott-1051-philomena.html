Philomena

Author-verified transcription

Philomena tells the true story of a woman who, after keeping a secret for fifty years, is compelled to not only tell her story but also to follow that story to finally close a chapter in her life. It is a riveting story of loss, forgiveness and the resilience of the human spirit.

In the fifties, the Catholic Church did not always demonstrate a Christian attitude or heart towards its followers who had strayed from the righteous path. When Philomena (Judi Dench), was a young Irish Catholic girl, she succumbed to temptation and found herself pregnant. Disowned by her father, it was left to the nuns to handle the rest of her “upbringing.” This amounted to using her as slave labor, overseeing her childbirth with a lack of mercy and medical attention, and then selling her child to a wealthy couple.

Fifty years later, her story came to the attention of Martin Sixsmith (Steve Coogan), an ex-journalist and disgraced political correspondent. Looking to resurrect his career by means of a human interest story, this somewhat jaded wordsmith agrees to help Philomena track down the son that was taken from her so many years ago.

Judi Dench delivers one of the finest performances of her already distinguished career. Her restraint in portraying a woman in conflict with herself and her church is almost otherworldly. Steve Coogan, who also co-wrote the film, provides solid support as a man doing his own soul-searching and perhaps learning a bit about himself from the example that Philomena sets.

Thanks to the strong performances of the leads and the realization that the events unfolding were, in part, true, the film never lagged or failed to arrest our attention. Philomena is a well-crafted, well-acted film that is both insightful and meaningful.

Wisdom tells us that we cannot always dictate what happens to us in life. All we can control is our response to it. In Philomena, we see the truism of that statement. Both Philomena and Sixsmith have been wronged. Sixsmith was publicly shamed and then fired for words he never said. His response was to become bitter, angry and rail against the injustices done to him. Philomena’s treatment was far worse. What she suffered at the hands of the nuns was horrific and inexcusable. And yet Philomena found the ability to forgive those who had wronged her and robbed her of a life with her son. It can be unfathomable to some to understand Philomena’s ability to forgive such a heinous act but, of all characters, hers is the most Christian response.

“Put on therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, bowels of mercies, kindness, humbleness of mind, meekness, long-suffering; Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye.” Colossians 3:12-13 (KJV)

Forgiveness is a double-edged sword and can cut both ways. It affects both the one to whom forgiveness is offered as well as the one who is able to forgive. It is a message not lost on Sixsmith who, at one point, becomes frustrated with Philomena’s calm demeanor. “Why aren’t you angry at them? I’m angry!” Philomena’s simple yet poignant response was, “It must be exhausting.”

Letting go of bitterness, resentment, and hatred can be hard. Philomena admits as much. But it is exhausting to hold onto those emotions. They poison our soul. They do not heal or comfort. By forgiving those who have wronged us, as we are taught and encouraged to do by God, we learn the important lessons that God would want us to know. That it is through forgiveness that we find love, joy, and the peace that passes all understanding.

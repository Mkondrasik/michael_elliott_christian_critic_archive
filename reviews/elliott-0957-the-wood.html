Author-verified transcription

When a man about to get married gets cold feet, he's going to need and want his friends around him. That's the premise and plot device for the coming-of-age story of The Wood.

It is three hours before his wedding and Roland (Taye Diggs, How Stella Got Her Groove Back) is nowhere to be found. His best friends Mike (Omar Epps, The Mod Squad) and Slim (Richard T. Jones, Event Horizon) finally find him, sloppy drunk at an old girlfriend's house. They've only got a few hours to sober him up and get him presentable so he can either get married or tell his fiancee that it's off.

This leads to a series of flashbacks which show us how these three men became such close friends. In 1986, young Mike (Sean Nelson, American Buffalo) moves to the 'Wood (Inglewood, CA), a middle-class African-American neighborhood. There he meets young Slim (Duane Finley in his film debut) and young Roland (Trent Cameron, The Kid Who Loved Christmas). They help Mike get acquainted with his new surroundings. This mostly consists of knowing which girls are "fly" and which guys to stay away from.

We track their friendship and adventures through the years, from junior high to their junior prom. The only difference seems to be that in junior high, they bet on who could "grab the booty," and in high school the bet was on who could "get the booty" first.

The film, written and directed by Rick Famuyiwa (Blacktop Lingo), while consisting predominately of African Americans, is universal in nature, spanning ethnic and cultural barriers. It's a story of friendship, of bonding, of relationships, of growing up and growing apart.

As in American Pie, the coming-of-age aspect of the story centers around sex, as if that is the only earmark of maturity. But The Wood has precious little to add to the subject. The boys talk about it more than they do it. And when it finally happens, despite their bravado, they are just as scared and nervous as the girls. The language and scenes of sexual discovery depicted are what merits this film's "R" rating.

This is a shame, for the relationship between the adult males is the more satisfying story, deserving to be seen by young teen aged males. We like and associate with these three intelligent, well-groomed black men who have formed a close familial bond which they've maintained over the years. As they remember the past, we see that they understand that the future is bringing changes which will alter that long friendship.

The ensemble and unselfish work of Omar Epps, Taye Diggs and Richard T. Jones is very enjoyable to watch. Mr. Jones especially is quite amusing as the sharp tongued and quick-witted Slim. As an audience, we get the impression that the actors truly enjoyed the experience of working together. Their camaraderie carried over to communicate the tight bond of friendship felt by the characters.

The female characters are not as clearly drawn nor are the relationships between the men and their women, with the one exception being young Mike's object of desire, Alicia (played by Malinda Williams, High School High). This results in our understanding fully the commitment the men have towards each other and their friendship, but not viscerally feeling the sense of change looming before them that has given Roland his case of prenuptial jitters.

Marriage does bring about change. God understands this and expects us to understand it as well.

An unmarried man is concerned about the Lord's affairs--how he can please the Lord. But a married man is concerned about the affairs of this world--how he can please his wife and his interests are divided. 1 Corinthians 7:32b-34a [NIV]

God considers the marriage relationship to be a serious commitment. One that requires a man to change the way he thinks and acts by considering another person's emotions and interests prior to making decisions. Had this truth been more strongly illustrated, the impact of the film would have been far greater.

Author-verified transcription

Some people are too smart for their own good. At least there are some people who like to think they are. Such is the case with Richard Haywood and Justin Pendleton, a pair of students who become unlikely friends and coconspirators in Murder by Numbers.

Theirs was not a crime of passion but of boredom... a boredom perhaps only known by a rich and privileged few. Smug and feeling vastly superior to the rest of mankind, Richard (Ryan Gosling, Remember the Titans) and Justin (Michael Pitt, Finding Forrester) set out to prove it by committing an unsolvable crime.

They thought of most everything, from the random selection of the victim to the planting of misleading evidence. What they didn't expect was the tenacious involvement of Cassie Mayweather (Sandra Bullock, Miss Congeniality), a bulldog of a homicide investigator. She, along with her new partner Sam (Ben Chaplin, The Truth About Cats and Dogs), suspect the two almost from the very beginning. All that is needed is to convince the captain to keep the case open long enough to find the proof.

But Cassie has a problem. Instead of trying to "get inside" the minds of the perpetrators, which is what a criminal investigator is supposed to do, she keeps seeing things from the victim's perspective.

That is largely the problem with the film as well. Director Barbet Schroeder (Desperate Measures) and screenwriter Tony Gayton (The Salton Sea) spend far too much time hinting at the story of Cassie's past instead of bringing us more deeply into the world and minds of the students.

Largely inspired by the infamous Leopold and Loeb case of the early 1920s, Murder by Numbers barely scratches the psychological depths that this story demands. It is a movie version of a paint-by-numbers picture. We can tell what it is supposed to be, but can't really call it a work of art.

Sandra Bullock, who also serves as executive producer, takes a step away from her girl next door persona. Here she plays a flawed character with deep psychological wounds who struggles with her past as it threatens to invade her present. While we can intellectually appreciate her character's arc, it isn't all that interesting to watch.

Far more interesting are the characters of Ryan Gosling, as the cool kid student, and Michael Pitt, as the brainiac nerd. Demonstrating the arrogance of youth, the script unfortunately does not allow them to explore the inner nature of their characters nor of their strange relationship. Thus, we are left without a true understanding as to why these characters felt compelled to act the way they did. This flaw weakens the film and makes the final chapter rather meaningless.

I suppose that there are as many reasons to commit a crime as there are crimes to commit. I'm also sure that criminals have tried every justification imaginable in order to rationalize their actions. But to kill for no reason other than to prove some kind of intellectual superiority smacks of stupidity. Scriptures tell us where thinking too highly of ourselves will lead:

Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall. Proverbs 16:18 (KJV)

The fall of Richard and Justin began the minute they elevated themselves above the rest of the world. Their unrestrained egos led them down a path which should have remained untraveled.

Meekness and humility are qualities that often go unappreciated in this celebrity-conscious world. In God's eyes, they are qualities to be highly praised and deeply coveted.

Before destruction the heart of man is haughty, and before honour is humility. Proverbs 18:12 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

Simple Things is an aptly named film about a young widowed doctor who becomes the latest cinematic fish-out-of-water when he leaves his normal big city life for the unfamiliar slower pace of a rural North Carolina backwater town.

With his fearful young son (Aidan Mitchell) in tow, Dr. Evan Gibbs (Cameron Bancroft) agrees to fill a need at a small, understaffed and meagerly supplied clinic in order to better his chances for a prestigious appointment to a top Chicago hospital.

Things do not go as expected. The clinic is a broken down shack; the handyman is the town drunk; his patients don't trust outsiders (especially doctors) enough to schedule appointments; and medical care is not a high priority on anyone's list.

"You're on mountain time, now," is the explanation Gibbs receives when his frustration mounts. His clipped, impersonal professionalism and marketing strategies backfire on him as they only serve to build a bigger wedge between him and the townspeople.

The themes contained in Simple Things have certainly been covered before in films with bigger budgets and more famous casts. But as its title suggests, the film isn't out to wow us with its originality or impress us with its credentials. Instead, it aims to win us over with its simplistic reminder of what is truly important in life.

The simple things of life are often given short shrift as we focus on careers and financial success. But without taking the time to nurture friends, family, and peace of heart, the extraneous trappings of life have little meaning.

Cameron Bancroft is a likable lead as the doctor whose must learn to reconnect with people. Bellamy Young is a warm presence as the affable and caring mayor. Edie McClurg as the town's nurse and Mickey Jones as the drunken handyman have some nice scenes.

A standout performance comes from Joshua Leonard who portrays a redneck, trailer-trash bum who is transformed by a simple thing like parenthood.

The script, by Andrew C. Erin (who also directed) and Peter Perland, is overly simplistic and requires us to suspend our disbelief of the plot in order to appreciate the tone and tenor of the film which is sweet and endearing.

There's an old saying that goes something like this: "People won't care that you know unless they first know that you care." It is a lesson that Dr. Gibbs must learn. All his medical skills and training are worthless to him if he first can't get the small town residents to trust him enough to seek his advice and counsel.

Trust is not something that is automatically delivered or comes with a degree or position. It is individually earned through one's association with one's fellow man. How we treat people; how we view people; how we interact with others all lead to the building of trust.

It is an important thing to remember as we share the Word of God with others. Are we delivering our version of "the sermon on the mount" with a haughtiness born from a feeling of superiority or "holier-than-thou-ness?" Or are we taking the time to get to know our audience and delivering a message that will help meet their specific needs? As we talk to people about God and what He can do for them, we should be spending most of our time doing the listening.

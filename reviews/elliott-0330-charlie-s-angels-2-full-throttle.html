Charlie's Angels 2: Full Throttle

Author-verified transcription

Imagine a cake made entirely of frosting. It might look pretty but a little piece of it will go a long way. Too much can make one sick. Charlie's Angels 2: Full Throttle is all frosting.

Not having learned from the similarly flawed original film, Director McG and his bevy of bodacious beauties still don't seem to recognize that in the movie business, story is king. The alleged story at the center of this particular movie is pretty much superfluous to what they're really presenting. Any film which chooses to open in a Mongolian bar featuring a mechanical yak for absolutely no reason is not too interested in staying credible.

In Full Throttle, the trio of angels: Dylan (Drew Barrymore), Natalie (Cameron Diaz), and Alex (Lucy Liu) are trying to recover a pair of rings which, for some inane reason, contains the names and locations of everybody placed within the Federal Witness Protection Program. The rings were stolen and it seems every dastardly villain in the world wants them - from the Irish Mafia to Mongolian barflies to a deadly fallen angel named Madison (Demi Moore).

That's pretty much the story. Everything else that occurs does so not because it adds to this story but because it gives McG the opportunity to try to razzle dazzle us with some meaningless stunts or CGI work. While the stunts and special effects are inventively outlandish, they don't work because we're too busy scoffing at the absurdity of what we're being asked to accept. The opening sequence alone will start eyes rolling so far back in the heads of some audience members it may prevent them from watching the rest of the film.

The original Charlie's Angels TV show helped to give ABC the unofficial nickname of being "the jiggle network" due to its insistence of accentuating the physical assets of its stars. The films adopt the same attitude but with less class or decorum. Where the 70's TV show featured mild titillation, these films and their stars are going for more of a sleazy approach. It's not an improvement.

There's little attempt at characterization, continuity, or any of the many other basic elements taught in Filmmaking 101. When not flying through the air, dodging bullets, or performing other feats of Matrix-like proportions, the stars fill the action gaps by giggling like schoolgirls and behaving as if they're at a soft-core porn pajama party.

If there was any hope for an insertion of energy by the addition of comedian Bernie Mac as Bosley, it's best to lose it now. The man is himself lost and paddling upstream in a vast sea of estrogen.

The same fate is suffered by almost every male character with the exception of Justin Theroux as a tattooed Irish mobster and Crispen Glover who scores points as The Thin Man mainly by playing up his own quirky reputation.

As far as seeing something of a spiritual nature in the film, we need only look at Demi Moore's character of Madison - a highly skilled and trained ex-angel who decided she was too good for the boss.

Pride [goeth] before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall. Proverbs 16:18 (KJV)

Pride has been the downfall of many potentially great men (and women). The best way to guard against it is to continually put on the mind of Christ.

I can of mine own self do nothing: as I hear, I judge: and my judgment is just; because I seek not mine own will, but the will of the Father which hath sent me. John 5:30 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

"Out of sight - out of mind." So the cliché goes. But there are exceptions to that rule... Times when even a chance meeting will make such an impact on a person that the memory of it will linger long after the other person is gone.

Such is the premise of Out of Sight. The chance meeting occurs in the trunk of a car. In the trunk are Jack Foley (George Clooney, The Peacemaker), who is a professional bank robber and escaped convict, and Karen Sisco (Jennifer Lopez, Anaconda), a Federal marshal who happened to be on the scene when Jack escaped.

After they climb out of the trunk and go their separate ways, they can't seem to stop thinking about the other. Even though to pursue any relationship would be insanity given their respective career choices, they are inexplicably drawn to one another.

Mr. Clooney gives another casually pleasant performance using his good looks and appealing personality to his full advantage. Miss Lopez, while not believable as a Federal marshal, is certainly believable as a woman who could easily capture a man's interest and invade his thoughts.

I especially enjoyed the work of some of the secondary characters in the movie. Ving Rhames (Con Air) as Buddy, Foley's partner in crime, gives a low-key but polished performance. Don Cheadle (Bulworth) is amusingly menacing as Snoopy, a con who is after five million dollars of uncut diamonds hidden in the home of white collar convict Richard Ripley (an unrecognizable Albert Brooks, Mother). Steve Zahn (The Object of My Affection) plays a pot smoking, sunglass-wearing airhead, bringing a light humorous touch to what is essentially a crime story with a romantic twist.

Not surprisingly, Out of Sight is based on an Elmore Leonard novel which has been adapted for the screen by Scott Frank, who also wrote the script for Mr. Leonard's Get Shorty. Mr. Frank's treatment keeps the focus upon Mr. Leonard's characters by not adhering to a chronological time line. Instead the story jumps around... It starts with Foley robbing a bank, then goes back a few years to when he was in prison... then jumps forward to a different scene.

I must admit that I did not enjoy the film as much as I appreciated the individual components that comprised the film. This is due to some directorial choices by Steven Soderbergh (sex, lies, and videotape) which I personally did not care for. His use of the action freeze frame technique and funky music track which peppered the film reminded me of the opening credits of the old Starsky and Hutch TV series. Personally, I found these techniques unnecessarily distracting.

Another element which may have affected my overall enjoyment of Out of Sight was its lack of moralistic integrity. We are being asked by the filmmakers to root for a protagonist that was clearly operating under a self-imposed standard of right vs. wrong. Rape is bad, theft is allowable. He wouldn't think of being disloyal to a friend, but doesn't think twice about killing another human being.

There is only one standard that should be used to discern right from wrong. A standard which was issued from a source outside of ourselves. A standard that we may use to effectively steer our way along life's rocky path. The standard of God's Word:

Thou shalt not steal. Exodus 20:15 [KJV]

Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth. Ephesians 4:28 [KJV]

Had Foley adopted that standard as his own, he would have been a protagonist worthy of our remembrance. As it is, the cliché holds true: once the movie is over, Out Of Sight will be out of mind.

Author-verified transcription

“Oh what a tangled web we weave when first we practice to deceive.” A young TV associate producer learns the hard truth of that phrase when she begins to lie her way into her boyfriend’s past.

Stacy (Brittany Murphy) has known what she wants out of life from an early age. Now in her 20s, she appears headed on the path of her choosing. She’s got a producer’s job with the syndicated Kippie Kann tabloid talk show, and a fantastic boyfriend named Derek (Ron Livingston) who seems to be head over heels in love with her.

It is when her co-workers (Holly Hunter and Kevin Sussman) place doubt in the back of her mind regarding Derek’s faithfulness, that she succumbs to temptation and begins to pry into his past relationships. Using his Palm Pilot as a source of information, she decides to look up his ex-girlfriends and, using the talk show as an excuse, interrogates them as to their past and present relationship with her boyfriend.

Her curiosity backfires when she learns more than she really wanted to know and becomes involved in an elaborate scheme that is sure to cause emotional pain for more than one person.

Little Black Book is an awkward little story with characters whose personality faults far outweigh their more positive qualities. The central figure is Stacy but she makes so many bad choices during the course of the film that it is difficult to root for her. Director Nick Hurran and first time screenwriter Melissa Carter try to force us to connect with their lead character by using the most mundane script device there is: the voice-over narration. It doesn’t help.

Brittany Murphy is cute and quirky and offers a bit of superficial appeal but she is not supported with a script that might better showcase her abilities. Holly Hunter always does solid work and while her turn as a cynical TV producer is no exception, it is not an exceptional role. The rest of the cast, other than Julianne Nicholson who is able to build some genuine empathy for one of the ex-girlfriends, is simply average.

The filmmakers might have gotten more comedic mileage out of the Kippie Kann show, a female version of Jerry Springer’s televised circus. Sadly, they seem stuck on the idea that alliteration is the highest form of humor. Therefore they kontinually kum up with kooky kinds of kaptions to see how freekwently they kan konk us over the knoggin with the K sound and the komedy ends there. Kathy Bates, after unwisely signing on for this project, wisely keeps a low profile as Kippie and barely registers on screen.

The film does demonstrate the insidious nature of doubt. It was by entertaining doubt in her mind that Stacy was led to take the ill-advised action that proved ruinous to her relationship with Derek. We are exhorted by God to eliminate doubt from our thinking. When we believe wholeheartedly and live our lives with fully persuaded minds, the things we desire and are working towards attaining shall be given to us. Seeds of doubt do nothing but bear the fruit of unbelief.

“But when he asks, he must believe and not doubt, because he who doubts is like a wave of the sea, blown and tossed by the wind.” James 1:6 (KJV)

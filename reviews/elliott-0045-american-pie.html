American Pie

Author-verified transcription

If apple pie can be used as a descriptive for all that is good about America (as in “as American as apple pie,”) the movie American Pie, can be used as a descriptive for much of what is bad. Its obsession with sex mirrors our country’s own.

The movie follows the exploits of a foursome of haplessly virginal friends reaching the end of their high school lives. Esteeming it a failure to be leaving high school without having had sex, they make a pact with one another that come prom night they will be men. As if one had anything to do with the other.

The structure of the movie allows first time director Paul Weitz and first time screenwriter Adam Herz to examine multiple ways a young man in the throes of hormonal confusion can embarrass himself.

Kevin (Thomas Ian Nicholas, Rookie of the Year) spends most of his time trying to convince steady girlfriend Vicky (Tara Reid, Cruel Intentions) that they’re ready to do “it”, without having to say the dreaded “L” word.

Oz (Chris Klein, Election), having had no success with his simian-like jock “approach”, decides to go the sensitive route by joining the choir and befriending a “goody two-shoes” type (Mena Suvari, Atomic Train) who may not yet know of his “bull in a china shop” reputation.

Finch (Eddie Kaye Thomas, Rage: Carrie 2) goes the opposite route by getting friend Jessica (Natasha Lyonne, Slums of Beverly Hills) to spread a rumor that makes him one of the most sought-after dates for the prom.

Jim (Jason Biggs, in his feature film debut) is the most pathetically sympathetic character in the bunch. His parents are forever interrupting his solo acts of physical release. He tries to duplicate the experience of a woman by having carnal knowledge of a baked desert. When a beautiful exchange student (Shannon Elizabeth, Seamless) invites herself to his house for a study session, his response is to secretly broadcast the encounter over the Internet. This results in a major embarrassment for him and an opportunity for the filmmakers to slip in some Porky’s style nudity.

American Pie is really just recycled teen trash. It’s the same film seen before in Fast Times at Ridgemont High without the drugs, or American Graffiti without the stellar cast. While its script tries to tag on a “moral” which seems to teach that sex under pressure is no fun, it is obvious that the filmmakers are of the widely-held belief that sex sells. In fact, it is apparent that this movie is banking on that belief.

There are three parental figures featured in the movie: Stifler’s mom, who is an object of teen-aged sexual fantasy; Jim’s mom, who would rather close her eyes to her son’s growing sexual awareness; and Jim’s dad, (played by Eugene Levy, Almost Heroes) who while sympathetic to his son’s libido, is laughably ineffectual in his attempts to help his son control it.

Control is a concept that goes over the heads of most of these young people. Surprising as it may be to the characters in this film, sex is not the be-all and end-all of existence. It is not even a defining characteristic of a human being. Animals copulate... Most do so indiscriminately.

What is unique to man is our ability to love. To control our thinking and our actions so as to stay faithful to the one we love.

Whoring, fornication, and adultery are terms that God uses throughout His Word to describe idolatry, which is seeking after other “gods.” Sexual terminology (especially terms that speak of unfaithful or promiscuous sex) are used to illustrate the most heinous of actions (in God’s view).

Thus were they defiled with their own works, and went a whoring with their own inventions. Psalms 106:39

Sexual obsession is nothing new to our generation. But our generation must learn to guard itself on many fronts. Movies, TV, Internet, periodicals, music, advertising, and all other mediums use sex to sell their product. As a culture, we are what we think. Corporate America wants us to think sex. God wants our thoughts to be set somewhat higher.

Take the Lead

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 7, 2004
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 48 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Romans 12:102 Corinthians 5:17Proverbs 29:18

Sermon / spiritual topic: Self-worth; Vision; Respect

Author-verified transcription

Cultures clash as a ballroom dance teacher decides to teach what he knows and loves to a group of underachieving NYC high school kids in Take the Lead. It is a story loosely based on the life of dance professional Pierre Dulane.

Antonio Banderas stars as Dulane who, after witnessing an act of senseless vandalism, magnanimously decides to volunteer to teach ballroom dance to the malcontents of the local high school. It is a gesture that is initially ridiculed by both the faculty and the students.

The principal (Alfre Woodard) only accepts his offer because she believes it might serve as an effective punishment for the kids in detention.

Dulane perseveres, eventually hooking the kids with the allure of the dance and the hope of a life that might shine a bit brighter than the dismal existence they have heretofore known.

It is a gargantuan task he sets for himself. Not that the kids don't like to dance. They are able to "throw down" to a hip hop beat at the drop of a mix. But play a little Percy Faith and ask them to dance with grace, precision and form? Dulane might as well ask them to split the atom - the concept is that foreign to them.

As time wore on, their resistance wore down. Aided by the prospect of a competition with a substantial prize to the winner, the students begin to learn. What they may not realize is that Dulane is teaching them much more than dance steps.

Acclaimed music video director Liz Friedlander makes her feature film debut with this film that tries to unite the energy of hip hop with the discipline of competition ballroom. Her inexperience in helming a full length feature shows as sections of the movie remain underdeveloped. The pace drags at times but she manages to maintain the narrative flow just enough to keep the audience involved as she presents the multiple story lines.

Banderas sufficiently conveys the grace and charm of a cultured man - enough to set him apart from the sub-cultured individuals whom he intends to teach. His dance moves, aided (or obscured) by some frenetic editing, give his character the authority and credibility to make the film work.

There are a large number of supporting characters, with an equally large (and largely undeveloped) number of back stories, which populate the film. None of these stories are particularly engaging or original. They borrow from such sources as Romeo and Juliet, Jules & Jim, Music of the Heart, Lean on Me, among others.

Standouts include Yaya DeCosta (America's Top Model) as a young girl looking to break free of a life of poverty and despair; Rob Brown (Coach Carter) as a lost soul looking for a way out of a life of crime; and Jenna Dewan (Joey) as the pivotal point of an unsatisfying love triangle.

All of the students are changed by the dance steps Dulane teaches them but it would be a mistake to think that the dance steps brought about the change. Dulane, in a speech he gives to a concerned parents' committee, explains that what he is really teaching is respect -- respect for one's self and respect for one's partner.

What Dulane gave these kids went far beyond the fundamentals of dance... he simply used dance to teach fundamentals of life. He gave them a vision and a goal; he asked them to look beyond the cultural limitations that kept them unmotivated; he gave them the freedom to hope for a different life as well as the means by which to build it.

Spiritually, God has given us the same things. Sure, we can remain bound to the limitations of our physical realities. But why not gain a new vision? How about building our spiritual abilities and awareness in order to attain a new and far superior way of life?

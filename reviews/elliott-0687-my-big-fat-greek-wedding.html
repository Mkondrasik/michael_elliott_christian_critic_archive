Author-verified transcription

My Big Fat Greek Wedding has become the little movie that could. Like the fabled engine that kept on chugging "I think I can. I think I can," this film opened small, but somehow managed to make it into the list of top ten highest grossing films of that week. It then miraculously has continued to stay there week after week as positive word of mouth and glowing critical reviews keep drawing audiences to the few theaters where it is being shown.

What is it that has audiences buzzing? It is just a simple story about an average girl who falls in love with a guy. That the girl is Greek and comes from a Greek household which prides itself on its ethnic heritage and tradition is what sparks the humor.

Nia Vardalos (Meet Prince Charming), who not only wrote the screenplay but also the one-woman show upon which it is based, stars as Toula Portokalos. When Ian Miller (John Corbett, Serendipity) walks into her father's restaurant, where she works as a seating hostess, Greek and non-Greek make a connection. The rest of the film depicts their growing romance and her family's reactions to it.

Toula comes from a culture which teaches that a Greek girl has three purposes in life: To marry a Greek man; have Greek babies; and feed everybody until the day she dies. At the head of the family is her father Gus (hilariously portrayed by Michael Constantine, Room 222), whose quirky characteristics include an odd belief that a squirt of Windex can heal all ailments. He also has the uncanny ability to trace every single word to its Greek roots... even a word like "kimono."

Toula's mother Maria (Lainie Kazan, The Crew) has comfortably found her place within the traditional Greek family hierarchy. As she says, "The man is the head of the house... but the woman is the neck and can make the head turn any way she wants."

Add the traditional collection of extended family members: aunts, uncles, an endless stream of cousins (all named Nick or Nicki) and then insert into this Greek sea of eccentricity a drop of white-bread Anglo Saxon blood and sit back to watch the culture clash begin.

The jokes may not always be of the gutbusting variety, although the film does provide ample opportunity to break loose with a guffaw or two. More likely, the film will gently paste a smile across one's lips as its sweetness and warmth envelops the audience.

While My Big Fat Greek Wedding pokes obvious fun at Greeks and some of their idiosyncrasies, it never once turns ugly or mean-spirited. At the root of all the jokes and witty observations is a recognizable love and fondness for the characters in the film. Because of this, it is not necessary to be Greek to connect with the story. Familial love is something that is universally understood.

Traditions can be comforting to us as well as edifying. There is always something reassuring about the air of familiarity. They can also be a trap, preventing us from reaching beyond what is familiar to us. Certainly the first century church was confronted with this reality.

And he said unto them, Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition. Mark 7:9 (KJV)

This is not to mean that traditions are evil or never to be honored. We should, however, always remember the purpose of a tradition. Doing something just because we've always done it that way is never a good justification for continuing to observe a tradition. We shouldn't allow a rite to ever keep us from doing what's right.

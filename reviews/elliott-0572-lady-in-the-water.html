Author-verified transcription

Sometimes, it would appear, there’s nowhere to go but down. It’s just sad to see it in someone once hailed as a master storyteller. M. Night Shyamalan, who hit one out of the park with The Sixth Sense, whiffs badly with the ill-conceived, poorly executed Lady in the Water. It is described by Shyamalan as “a bedtime story” and in that description there is a shred of truth for it will put people to sleep.

Cleveland Heep (Paul Giamatti) is a sad little man who serves as a maintenance superintendent for a run down apartment complex in Philadelphia and believes there is something living in its pool. That something turns out to be a “narf,” a nymph-like creature from the “blue world” (i.e. water).

Bryce Dallas Howard plays the translucent character that has come to our world in order to deliver a message that will alter the direction of the universe and all who live in it. Standing in her way is a vicious “scrunt” which can best be described as a Chia-Pet with teeth.

According to ancient lore, she must get help from the humans for whom her message is intended. Each of them has a specific role to play and unique powers that will enable them to succeed. The problem is that their identities and powers are yet unknown – to them as well as the narf. Heep must uncover who is who and what they are to do before the scrunt destroys them all and returns to his mulch pile.

If all of this sounds a bit childish, perhaps it is because Shyamalan conceived the tale as a bedtime story for his children. Perhaps they were enthralled. Don’t expect the same.

It is a clumsily constructed screenplay that often elicits laughs that may or may not be intentional. It is difficult to know for sure how much Shyamalan is taking seriously here or how much he’s simply riding the huge marketing coattails generated from The Sixth Sense and Signs while laughing at the gullibility of modern day audiences.

Perhaps expecting the critical drubbing that is sure to come his way, Shyamalan decided to be proactive. Incorporated into his story is a sour-faced film critic (Bob Balaban) whose arrogance knows no limits and his ignorance knows no bounds. His condescending attitude leads to an unhappy end that must be providing the screenwriter/director with a certain vicarious glee. He should enjoy it while he can because the critical barbs coming his way are sure to be sharp and plentiful.

Most of the actors in the film are easily forgotten, their screen time is that limited. Paul Giamatti isn’t as fortunate. His talent is no match for a director who is either unwilling or unable to protect him from looking foolish. It is the first bad screen performance I’ve seen from this Oscar winner and it isn’t his fault.

Bryce Dallas Howard fares a bit better but has the easier job of playing a mysterious, ethereal creature of few words. She did that consistently and well but left it to her costar to carry the picture that, at times, must have felt like the proverbial anvil around his neck.

On the plus side, there is much in the story from which to draw spiritual or biblical parallels. One striking element is that everyone at the apartment complex seems to be searching for their “purpose” in life. They all seem to know that they have one but no one has ever told them what it is. They all have access to a power with no knowledge of what it might be. We do not share their plight. We have a purpose as well as the power to realize it. The God who gave us both also gives us the knowledge and the guidance we need to fully know who we are and what we can/should do.

Collateral

Author-verified transcription

When a professional assassin is contracted for five "hits," all of which must take place in the same night, he chooses to make an unusual transportation arrangement - he hires a cab to take him from victim to victim. The premise of Collateral is admittedly a bit forced, but director Michael Mann and his cast manage to make the film more entertaining than it has a right to be.

Tom Cruise seems to be enjoying himself as he steps outside of the heroic roles he usually plays. As Vincent, he's a charismatic sociopath who happens to kill for a living. It is this fact that Max (Jamie Foxx), an LA cabdriver, "accidentally" discovers when Vincent's first victim falls out of a fourth floor window and smashes onto the roof of his taxi.

Max is forced at gunpoint to accompany Vincent on his appointed rounds and during their time together, they inexplicably forge a bond of sorts. Vincent even helps Max assert himself with his boss and a new potential girlfriend (Jada Pinkett Smith).

As Max and Vincent are driving around LA taking care of Vincent's business, the LAPD and the Feds are trying to figure out what is happening. Mark Ruffalo plays Detective Fanning who seems to be the only one who believes that Max may be an innocent victim.

Director Michael Mann builds the suspense slowly, allowing the characters and situations to percolate a bit. Once the story begins to boil, Mann continues to apply the heat and the final 45 minutes are as exciting and thrilling as the genre allows. True, Mann and screenwriter Stuart Beattie (Pirates of the Caribbean) could have taken the film in a completely different direction but in settling for a predictable and formulaic ending, they have assured themselves of a popular box office hit.

Though Cruise is the one who gets top billing, this is really Jaime Foxx's picture. It is his character around whom the story revolves and with whom the audience will most closely associate. An intelligent dreamer, he keeps insisting to all who ask that driving a cab is merely a temporary gig that pays the bills as he tries to launch his fantasy limo service. Even after twelve years, he still believes his own rationalization.

Cruise displays an icy charm and the confident demeanor of a seasoned professional. He shows us enough to let us know that there's a whole lot going on under Vincent's polished facade. It's an interesting performance that could have been better served by a script that spent more time exploring the nuances and development of its characters.

Surprisingly, there is a fair amount of humor sprinkled coming mainly from Vincent's calm philosophical observations and Max's uncomfortable reactions to them. The humor ends three-quarters through the film as the movie ends with a traditional cop action chase/rescue.

At one point, Vincent tries to comfort Max's guilty conscience regarding the deaths of Vincent's victims. "You never had a choice," he reminds Max. That is, of course, a lie. We always have a choice. Life is nothing but a series of choices. It is just that, for most of the film, Max manages to avoid making them.

What Max learns through the course of the film is that inaction is also a choice - and a dull one at that. He comes to realize that his life has stalled behind the wheel of a cab because he has allowed himself to dream of a better life instead of taking the necessary action to attain it.

We should never allow our fear to freeze us into immobility. Making choices and acting through those choices is an essential part of life. When it comes to our own lives, being a passive, uninvolved spectator is the worst possible choice that we could make.

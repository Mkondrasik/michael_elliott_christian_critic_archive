Author-verified transcription

I provide the following in the spirit of full disclosure: I'm in the wrong theater. I intended to watch an advance screening of the sci-fi kids' film Zathura (which I will now have to see this weekend) but got my invitations mixed up. The movie began and I discovered that I was part of an audience for the latest adaptation of Jane Austin's Pride and Prejudice.

I silently groaned... OK, so maybe it wasn't so silent. But can you blame me? How many adaptations of Jane Austin's works must we watch in the space of a decade? Ten films in ten years seems to me a tad excessive.

But then I settled down to actually watch the darn thing and soon forgot all complaints and became lost in the handsome production qualities and stellar acting that brought Austin's characters to life. I again realized why her stories have been adapted so often. It's because they work. And they work brilliantly.

Pride and Prejudice tells a rather simple tale. The Bennet family is of modest means and in the 18th century the way out is to marry well. With five daughters, the Bennets are looking for suitable suitors to pull the family into the upper crust. Eldest daughter Jane (Rosamund Pike) has the best opportunity when her beauty catches the eye of Mr. Bingley (Simon Woods), a dapper young man of wealth.

His traveling companion is none other than Mr. Darcy (Matthew MacFadyen), whose aloof manner insults and irritates the headstrong Elizabeth Bennet (Keira Knightley). We know that a love match is in the making long before they do.

The story (adapted by Deborah Moggach) progresses through the Bennet family's romances, trials, and tribulations but it focuses primarily on Elizabeth and her love/hate relationship with Darcy. And therein we find what makes the film work. Knightley and MacFadyen both provide solid and heartfelt performances that more than match anything previous actors have brought to oft performed roles.

They, along with the supporting cast led by Brenda Blethyn, Donald Sutherland, Rosamund Pike, and Simon Woods give the film an ensemble feel which is so crucial to its success.

Director Joe Wright didn't play safe with this classic. He approached the work as if it were a new script by a first time screenwriter and delivered a story as fresh as anything written this year despite the fact that it was, in actuality, penned almost 2 centuries ago. His hand is subtle and yet noticeable. There's an extended tracking shot set during a dance that gives more information about multiple characters than any scripted dialogue could ever provide. It is a masterful and thoroughly enjoyable piece of filmmaking.

The lesson of the film can be found in the title. Austin commented on the social realities of her day - how pride and preconceptions could blind people to the point that they miss the valuable qualities of an individual that were there for all to see. It is a lesson that we would do well to learn. Pre-judging is a practice that is ill-advised.

Be honest in your judgment and do not decide at a glance (superficially and by appearances); but judge fairly and righteously. John 7:24 (Amplified)

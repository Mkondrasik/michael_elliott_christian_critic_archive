Accepted

Sermon / spiritual topic: Teachers

Author-verified transcription

If you ever wondered what Ferris Bueller might have been like in college... look no further. Accepted is a movie that shows us that an intelligent, quick thinking high school student with no respect for rules can beat all authority and most logic to succeed when common sense tells us that he should fail.

Bartleby Gaines (Justin Long) is the student in question and he's a likable fellow. He spent his high school life looking for ways to get around the system but failed to prepare for a means to get into a college. As soon as a prospective school takes a look at his transcript, they reject him.

Tired of the rejection and under pressure from his parents, Bartleby pursues the only course he can think of - he makes up a college and writes himself an acceptance letter. South Harmon Institute of Technology (its acronym is a gag continually revisited by the screenwriters) is the figment of Bartleby's imagination and is so convincing that over 300 other high school losers sign up and are automatically accepted to the imaginary school.

Thus Bartleby becomes a shaper of young minds. And what better way to shape them than to let the students become the teachers?

It's a silly concept driven by sex and bathroom related humor and could be easily written off as just another teen comedy. But there's something about Long's performance that kicks Accepted up the acceptability ladder a rung or two. Affable and self-effacing with a natural ease, Long adds a charming element to the excrement jokes and sexual banter.

As his best friend Sherman, Jonah Hill lends a heavily sarcastic air to the film. Smart but grossly unpopular, Sherman may be the only one of Bartleby's friends to get accepted into college but that's a long way from gaining the social acceptance he desires.

Blake Lively is an attractive addition as the object of Bartleby's romantic intentions. Adam Herschman is one of the school's students, a burnt out ex-druggie turned culinary expert. Comedian Lewis Black plays Dean Lewis, Bartleby's pick for an adult to pretend to be in charge of the school. An ex-teacher turned surly shoe salesman, his humor is laced with profanity and bellicose commentary.

Bartleby begins his school as just another way to dodge the consequences of his actions. As he gets in deeper and deeper to avoid exposure, he stumbles onto something he considers profoundly important. His "students" were rejected by every other learning institution because they were different than the norm. They didn't fit in with the image of traditional academia. But at his "school," the curriculum became whatever the students wanted to learn. And whoever wanted to learn something had to take the lead in teaching it.

There's obviously a fatal flaw in the structure of Bartleby's logic. There's only so much a person can learn without someone helping him to understand it. A teacher will help his students comprehend the material and place it in its proper context until full understanding is achieved. A self-taught man will always be limited by the perspective of his teacher.

And Philip ran thither to him, and heard him read the prophet Esaias, and said, Understandest thou what thou readest? And he said, How can I, except some man should guide me? And he desired Philip that he would come up and sit with him. Acts 8:30-31 (KJV)

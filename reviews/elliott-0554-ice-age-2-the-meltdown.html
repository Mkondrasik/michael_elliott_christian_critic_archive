Author-verified transcription

As we all know, sequels can be hit or miss and most of them fall into the latter category. Ice Age 2: The Meltdown suffers from the most common malady facing sequels – there’s simply no good reason to revisit these characters.

In the first film, three very different animals (a mammoth, a sloth, and a saber-toothed tiger), with very different motivations, join forces to help reunite a human child to his family. In the process they bond together to form their own semi-dysfunctional and somewhat comical family.

In the sequel, they are still together but the dramatic tension between them has been dulled, diluting the humor as well. What is now facing them is the need to lead the animals of their peaceful valley to safety as the melting of the Ice Age threatens to flood everything in sight.

With that as the foundation of the story, the subplots take shape. Manny, the mammoth (Ray Romano), fears that he is the last of his kind… that is until he meets Ellie, a female mammoth (Queen Latifah). Unfortunately, due to some major imagination stretching on the part of the screenwriters, she believes herself to be a possum.

Diego, the tiger (Denis Leary) is the character that suffers the most from sequelitis. His teeth, figuratively speaking, have been pulled from him and there’s little left for him to do but wrestle with a hackneyed fear of water. Sid, the sloth (John Leguizamo) gets most of the good lines but can’t carry the picture by himself and isn’t given much to do but react to what’s going on around him.

Not surprisingly, the star of the film continues to be a largely non-verbal squirrel named Scrat whose Herculean efforts to acquire an acorn results in a few side splitting moments. But it is a character and plot device better suited for a short subject rather than a 90 minute film.

The CGI animation has somewhat improved since the original film but all animators in the business must now be measured against the gold standard – Pixar Films. And the animators of Ice Age 2 have yet to capture the heart and soul of an animated figure in the same endearing way that Pixar has shown is possible.

Of the new characters introduced in the sequel, Crash (Seann William Scott) and Eddie (Josh Peck), Ellie’s possum brothers, are given the most screen time as a pair of wise-cracking, extreme sports goofballs.

Fear seems to be the central focal point of each character. Manny fears extinction; Diego fears water (not a good thing with the ice melting and all); and Sid, when he remembers to fear, fears not belonging to a family or herd. All of them fear the future and what the end of the Ice Age might bring.

We need not share their fears when the signs of our times turn ominous. We can trust in the promises of God and rely on his faithfulness. He has painted us a glorious picture of what our future will hold and we may rest in the hope of its fulfillment regardless of what may be happening around us.

God is our refuge and strength, very much found to be a help in distresses. On account of this we will not fear when the earth changes and when mountains are slipping into the heart of the seas. Psalms 46:1-2 (LIV)

Author-verified transcription

As ugly a reality as war is, there are countless records of bravery, valor, and sacrifice that come from those who serve at times of war. The Great Raid is a movie inspired by one of those records.

Set in the Philippines of 1945, it is based upon the ambitious rescue of 500 Allied POWs being held by the Japanese. As the voiceover narration tells us, as the war was nearing an end, the Japanese thought they had to erase any evidence of war crimes by brutally eliminating those being held in prisoner of war camps.

As the balance of war tipped in favor of the Americans and their Allies, a handful of untested soldiers were given a non-essential but morally important duty. They were to travel behind enemy lines and across open terrain to liberate the POWs being held at Camp Cabanatuan before the enemy carried out their genocidal plans.

The officer in charge of the battalion was Lt Col. Henry Mucci (Benjamin Bratt) who delegated responsibility of the raid to Captain Prince (James Franco). Inside the camp, Major Gibson (Joseph Fiennes), sapped by a bout of malaria, does what he can to keep order in the ranks while keeping hope alive. He is assisted by Margaret Utinsky (Connie Nielsen), the widow of his ex-commanding officer, who has helped to lead the Philippine underground resistance to smuggle food and drugs into the camp.

This is an old fashioned war tale that feels like it easily could have been made some forty years ago without raising an eyebrow. It doesn't try to provide us with insight to the enemy's point of view. It simply paints them as evil and barbaric in their treatment of their captives.

Benjamin Bratt captures the military bearing and manner of a career combat officer perfectly. His is a curt, no-nonsense performance. James Franco's Prince is an educated, intelligent man who just happens to find himself in the middle of a war. As so often happens with history, he was the right man in the right place assigned to do an impossible job. So he does it.

Joseph Fiennes, as the ailing ranking officer inside the POW camp, shows a quiet strength. Keeping emotions in check; weighing the consequences of every action before taking it; practicing restraint for the good of every man under his command - he demonstrates superior leadership qualities.

The film follows these three men (and Margaret Utinsky) as their lives converge and stories merge into the climatic ending.

What I found particularly compelling about the film was the illustration it gave of the importance of having a leader within the group dynamic. Each of the main characters serves in a leadership role and offers us a look at the mindset, attitude and behavior that leaders must adopt to be successful.

They practice focused thinking. They set unselfish goals that will benefit all. They give clear and direct communication. They provide direction, encouragement, support and discipline to all who follow them.

Whether in a military organization, a corporate setting, or a spiritually minded church, the need for men and women to actively serve as leaders is vitally important.

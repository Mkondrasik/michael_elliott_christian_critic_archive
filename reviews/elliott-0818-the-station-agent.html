Author-verified transcription

The Station Agent is a small film with a big heart starring a small man with a big talent. Its unhurried pace is perfect for this simple story that wins us over with its warmth and gentle humor. By the end of the film, we genuinely care about these characters and are gratified to see them in a better place emotionally than when they began.

Finn (Peter Dinklage) is an introverted dwarf with a passion for trains who has long since built up an emotional barrier protecting him from the rude stares and thoughtless comments from those he meets. When his only friend dies and bequeaths to him an abandoned train depot in rural New Jersey, Finn decides to move in. Happy for an isolated existence, he imagines long, lazy days of train watching and walks along the "right-of-way" (which is train talk for railroad tracks.)

What he didn't expect was the presence of a talkative hot dog vendor named Joe who is overjoyed with the arrival of a cool new "friend." He also becomes acquainted with Olivia, a local painter who has not yet fully come to terms with the death of her son which occurred two years earlier. Although he resists their intrusion into his life at first, Finn is ultimately drawn into their midst and is reintroduced to the joys of fellowship.

Peter Dinklage, who also appeared in a show-stealing scene in this year's Elf, is a major talent. His ability to non verbally communicate internal thoughts and emotions must be innate. I don't think it can be taught. It is a brilliant performance that elevates this film to an emotional plane that touches us all.

Bobby Cannavale, as the pushy food cart employee, turns his character into more than a mere catalyst. True, his character is responsible for pushing the action forward because both Finn and Olivia would be content for the rest of the world to leave them alone. But Cannavale makes Joe a real and complete person by emphasizing his need for human contact. That is the underlying motivation that drives his extroverted personality.

Patricia Clarkson, also seen in this year's Pieces of April, slowly reveals her character to us. At first appearing to be an accident-prone, scattered brain artist, she deliberately reveals the layers of pretense her character has put on to let us see the depth of emotional pain that she is still experiencing.

The script and direction by first timer Thomas McCarthy are worthy of awards. It is a rare achievement to tell such a simple story that carries with it such emotional weight. I look forward to Mr. McCarthy's next project.

At the heart of the film is the always present but largely unspoken fact of Finn's diminutive size. Finn does not mention it unless asked a direct question about it. And many of the questions, he chooses not to answer. Through his performance, our heart goes out to him. We understand him. Our physical appearance is only a small fraction of who we are. Whether we are idealizing attractive people or approaching with curiosity those who look different than we, we expose ourselves as being shallow and insensitive.

It is no wonder that Finn has chosen a life of isolation. Being an object of curiosity wouldn't wear well on me either. What finally encourages him to trust Joe and Olivia enough to accept their offer of friendship is his recognition that they are hurting as well. They have their own problems and needs. Each of these characters have a hole in their hearts that the others are able to fill.

Ointment and perfume rejoice the heart: so doth the sweetness of a man's friend by hearty counsel. Proverbs 27:9 (KJV)

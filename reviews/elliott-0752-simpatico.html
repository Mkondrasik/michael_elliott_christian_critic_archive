Author-verified transcription

My son came home from school one day with an "F" on an English test. As a concerned parent, I asked him if the material was too difficult for him. His surprising (and completely unacceptable) reply was, "No, the teacher always throws out our lowest test grade so the whole class decided not to study for this one. Don't worry, it won't count."

After watching Simpatico, I wonder if the usually reliable actors involved in the film wouldn't mind at all if Hollywood and the theater going public shared that teacher's philosophy when it came to movies. To use sandlot sports terminology, let this one be a "do over."

Adapted from a Sam Shepard play, Simpatico deals with the after effects of a horse racing scam perpetrated by three teenaged friends. Twenty five years later, the past is dredged up, forcing them to deal with who and where they are and what they have done.

Jeff Bridges (Arlington Road) is Carter, now a wealthy horse breeder living in Kentucky with his wife Rose (Sharon Stone, The Muse) who rarely gets out of bed and when she does, it's apparently only to get another drink. Nick Nolte (Affliction) plays Vinnie, a psychologically tortured private eye wannabe who sets in motion one last attempt to make things right. He concocts a story to bring Carter back to his ramshackle existence in California, only to abandon him there while he heads back to Kentucky, trying to peddle his shoebox full of secret truths.

The three stars are joined by Albert Finney (Breakfast of Champions) as a race track official whose career and reputation are ruined by the scam, and Catherine Keener (Being John Malkovich) as a grocery store cashier who becomes involved in the complicated lives of Vinnie and Carter, completing quite an impressive cast list for a project ultimately unable to effectively capitalize upon the talents which were collected.

Playing the trio of friends as teenagers are Shawn Hatosy (Anywhere But Here), Liam Waite (The Day October Died), and Kimberly Williams (Just a Little Harmless Sex). The younger versions bear little resemblance (in appearance, characteristics, or the foreshadowing of either) to their adult counterparts which greatly lessens the potential impact of the flashback sequences.

The acting, by and large, is acceptable although not outstanding. The disappointing weakness of the film is found in the poorly drawn characters rather than in the actors who play them. Logic must have been a scarce commodity in the crafting of the screenplay because only a smattering of it appears on the screen.

Writer/director Matthew Warchus, making his feature film debut, is known primarily for his theater work so perhaps it made sense to entrust him with bringing Sam Shepard's play to cinematic life. However, what might work on stage falls flat on film. Taking a more stylized approach might have helped to serve the material better. At its core, Simpatico's mystery is not that mysterious.

The film can serve as an illustration of the devastating effects that guilt can have upon the human psyche. The main characters lead fragile lives because their past remains always beneath the surface, ready to swallow them up and devour them with regret and hurtful memories.

No doubt all of us have managed to do things in our past for which we are not particularly proud. Thankfully, in this age of God's grace and mercy, His forgiveness is readily available. And when God forgives, He doesn't store the memory of our sins.

I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake, and will not remember thy sins. Isaiah 43:25 (KJV)

As we humbly seek His forgiveness and change our thoughts and actions so as not to repeat our wrongful behavior, God does forgive... and forget. We owe it to ourselves to do likewise. Harboring the pain and guilt of past mistakes is detrimental and debilitating. Life is too short for the guilt of what we've done in the past to stop us from the good we can do in the future.

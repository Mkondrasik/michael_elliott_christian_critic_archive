Author-verified transcription

Rush Hour is a bit of a misnomer. It takes longer than an hour and it doesn’t give much of a rush.

Its premise is nothing we haven’t seen before: A couple of ethnically and ideologically different cops team up to battle the bad guys. The bad guys in this case are Chinese hoodlums who kidnap the young daughter of the Consul for an exorbitant ransom. China’s top cop, Detective Inspector Lee (Jackie Chan, Mr. Nice Guy) flies all the way to America to help the FBI with the investigation.

Since the FBI doesn’t want a foreign cop’s involvement, they arrange for LAPD’s Detective James Carter (Chris Tucker, Jackie Brown) to keep him busy and out of harm’s way. A task easier said than done. It isn’t long before these two pair up to work independently of the FBI to solve the case on their own.

Jackie Chan’s English is about as intelligible as Chris Tucker’s Chinese (but only marginally worse than Mr. Tucker’s English). Subsequently, their scenes (the ones with dialogue) have us straining forward in our seats as if our doing so will help them with their enunciation.

Mr. Tucker pushes his urban black persona way over the top. He loses any credibility for playing a realistic character within the first ten minutes of the film. While he certainly calls attention to himself, not all of it is good. His loud, rapid-fire delivery falls on the same droning note and quickly becomes tedious.

Director Brett Ratner (Money Talks) keeps the action moving with lots of explosions, lots of flailing fists and legs, and of course, Jackie Chan’s trademark moves and stunts.

The stunts are enjoyable to watch. Mr. Chan’s popularity is largely due to the way he successfully mixes light-hearted comedy into his martial arts fight sequences. Rush Hour continues in that vein. His fight scene in a convention hall filled with priceless and fragile Chinese artifacts that he is intent on leaving intact is very funny.

We’ve seen many movies where the heroes, in order to prevail, have to work in opposition to the ineffective, bumbling, or corrupt bureaucratic organizations in charge. They ignore regulations, disregard direct orders, and violate proper procedures in order to go their own way. Of course, being the heroes, they are always found to be correct in their disobedience.

Rush Hour is constructed using that same logic, but because the FBI, who is in charge of this particular investigation, is not mishandling the case, the blatant disobedience of Detectives Lee and Carter and their unwillingness to follow authority sends a spiritually erroneous (albeit subliminal) message.

There is nothing heroic or noble in disobedience or in expressing disdain or disrespect for authority. Working outside of a governing framework is actually less productive and provides less protection than working within that framework.

God gave to His church certain ministries for the purpose of establishing a spiritual framework whereby people may be nourished and grow.

And he gave some apostles; and some prophets; and some evangelists; and some pastors and teachers; For the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ: Ephesians 4:11-12 [KJV]

Working within the framework God has designed will bring the results that God promises: the perfecting of the saints and the edification of the body of Christ. Jesus Christ himself understood and operated within frameworks:

And he [Jesus Christ] went down with them [his parents], and came to Nazareth, and was subject unto them: Luke 2:51 [KJV]

Certainly if anyone could have tried standing alone and apart from a functioning framework, it was Jesus Christ, but he clearly submitted himself to the framework designed by God and explained to us in Ephesians 6:1 [KJV]

Children, obey your parents in the Lord for this is right.

Rush Hour offers some entertaining fight sequences but very little else. There’s no danger of a traffic jam here.

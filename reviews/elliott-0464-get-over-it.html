Author-verified transcription

Get Over It is a typical teen comedy which Miramax released without giving an advanced screening to critics. Normally this would be a sure sign that the movie going public will have no problem obeying the command of the title. Surprisingly, Get Over It has enough pleasant verve and high-spirited energy that we might find the film lingering in our memories a bit longer than the other recent examples of its genre.

Ben Foster (Freaks and Geeks) plays Berke Lawrence, an average guy who fell madly in love with his neighbor Allison when he was seven years old, only to see her family leave town. Now that they are in high school, Allison (Melissa Sagemiller in her film debut) suddenly returns and much to his delight, they resume their romance. After a while, she's ready to call it quits. He isn't... even after she starts going out with the talented "Striker" Scrumfeld (Shane West, Whatever It Takes), an English-accented member of the boy band, "The Swingtown Lads."

Hopelessly in love, Berke is determined to win Allison back, even if it means auditioning for the school play, a musical rendition of Shakespeare's "A Midsummer Night's Dream," to be directed by the flamboyantly pretentious high school drama teacher, Dr. Desmond Forrest Oates (Martin Short, Father of the Bride).

Berke lands a role with the help of his best friend's "baby sister," Kelly (Kirsten Dunst, Bring It On) who obviously wishes that he would feel for her what he feels for Allison. Thus the love quadrangle is set, Kelly loves Berke; Berke loves Allison; Allison loves Striker; Striker is with Allison but loves himself. Not totally unlike the Shakespearean play they are performing.

Perhaps the similarity is because the rather flimsy script was written by R. Lee Fleming Jr. who also penned the teen comedy, She's All That which was a very loose rendition of the George Bernard Shaw tale "Pygmalion."

What sets the film apart from the rest of the post-pubescent pack is the playful tone set by director Tommy O'Haver (Billy's Hollywood Screen Kiss). We evidence this from the very start. The opening credits run while Berke, having been dumped by Allison, is walking forlornly home carrying a box of his returned belongings. In the background, the neighbors are joyfully singing and dancing to the Captain and Tennille song, "Love Will Keep Us Together." The combined effect was unexpected and quite amusing.

The actors comport themselves admirably. Ben Foster makes for an atypical romantic lead but his rather average looks build sympathy as the audience strongly identifies with the pain of rejection he portrays.

Both Kirsten Dunst and Melissa Sagemiller are examples of the kind of prototypical recipients of the teenage crushes I'm sure most males experienced in high school. Martin Short is extremely funny in a role that could have been easily overplayed. He walks a tightrope between character and caricature and his restraint makes for an enjoyable performance.

As is so often the case, where the film disappoints is in the heavy sexual content and crude humor which has apparently become a Hollywood requirement for teenage comedies. It is an unwelcome and unneeded addition to this story which had so much more to offer.

One scene comes to mind. Berke's parents (Swoozie Kurtz and Ed Begley Jr.) are depicted as liberal minded, free thinkers inclined to let their child experience all of life... both the good and bad. In a moment of frustration Berke shouts out to them, "You're my parents! Stop trusting me!"

Children, even teen aged ones, need a framework in which to grow. Parents have the responsibility to provide that framework, reproving and correcting whenever the child steps outside of the boundaries set for them.

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it. Proverbs 22:6 (KJV)

Teenagers, the smart ones, recognize this truth and are thankful for parents who provide it.

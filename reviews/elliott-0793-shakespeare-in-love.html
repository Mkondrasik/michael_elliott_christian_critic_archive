"Romeo and Ethel, the Pirate's Daughter." It doesn't quite have the same ring, does it? And yet, according to Shakespeare In Love, that was the working title of what many consider to be the greatest love story the world has ever known.

Set in London of 1593, Shakespeare In Love follows the trials and tribulations of young William Shakespeare (Joseph Fiennes, Stealing Beauty), a struggling playwright in desperate need of inspiration. He finds it in the guise of one Viola De Lesseps (Gwyneth Paltrow, Sliding Doors). As their relationship develops, so does the play he's been commissioned to write. From a comedy about pirates and "a bit with a dog," the play takes on the shape of a romantic tragedy... a love that is forbidden. Like the love between a woman of means and a common day player in a cash-poor theatrical company.

Words are the real stars of this movie... wonderful, poetic, inspiring words. It just helps to have exceedingly talented people speaking them. Joseph Fiennes is brilliant as the young Will Shakespeare. Mr. Fiennes captures the burning passion of a poet's soul and displays it for all to see, whether he is locked in an embrace with his lady love or hunched over a parchment with ink-stained fingers, scratching out the words that cause hearts to soar and knees to grow weak.

Miss Paltrow again turns in a luminescent performance. It is easy to picture her as the inspirational prototype of a Juliet, Viola, Ophelia, Rosaline, Portia, or any of Shakespeare's heroines. Here she plays a woman who out of a love for the theater, dresses like a man so she may audition for Shakespeare's new play during a time when women were not allowed on the stage. Through her eyes, we see and can appreciate the full magnificence of Shakespeare's work.

The entire cast is deserving of praise: Ben Afflick (Armageddon), as Ned, the leading actor of the troupe of players, brings an assured presence to the screen. Geoffrey Rush (Les Miserables), as Henslowe, the owner of The Rose Theatre, is comically engaging. Judy Dench (Mrs. Brown) as Queen Elizabeth I is regally commanding.

Director John Madden (Mrs. Brown) applies a master's touch in bringing all elements of the movie together so well that his work goes by almost unnoticeably. (That is a high compliment.)

But it is the words that give this film its real strength and power. Written by Marc Norman (Cutthroat Island) and Tom Stoppard (Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead), the screenplay contains wit, intelligence, and heart-swelling poetry to lift the most down-trodden soul.

Words do have power. The power to hurt and the power to heal. Words may incite riots, topple nations, ruin lives and reputations. They may also be used to build empires, inspire acts of courage and bravery, place within a man's soul the aspiration to be a nobler creature.

It is why God instructs us to consider our words before we speak. Everything we say has an impact. A carelessly uttered word can do an irreparable damage:

Death and life are in the power of the tongue: and they that love it shall eat the fruit thereof. Proverbs 18:21 [KJV]

The fiery darts that are fired from our spiritual adversary oftentimes come in the form of words that fly like arrows to our hearts with an aim to harm or destroy. Likewise, God gave us His Word to repel those fiery darts:

Above all, taking the shield of faith, wherewith ye shall be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked. And take the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God: Ephesians 6:16-17 [KJV]

Shakespeare chose his words carefully. It is the reason that he is still taught in high schools and colleges of our time. The impact of his words are felt to this day. Students who have yet to appreciate a Shakespearean play or sonnet need only see this film. His words, beautifully delivered, will reach not only their ears - but also their hearts.

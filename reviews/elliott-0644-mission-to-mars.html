Author-verified transcription

At its action-oriented heart, the story of Mission To Mars more resembles a Buck Rogers' adventure than any kind of plausible depiction of a future space endeavor. Even though NASA representatives worked in close consultation with the filmmakers before and during the production of Mission To Mars (which obviously resulted in the film's highly realistic set and costume designs), I can only imagine the incredulous looks given by the space experts when asked for advice on the story itself: “So, Commander, exactly what would you do if upon final approach to Mars, your space capsule is accidentally ruptured, loses pressure, and is sent spinning out of control?”

But as unbelievable as the adventure may be, the production values of the film are so high, it remains entertaining despite the hijinks and zero-gravity antics of these space cowboys.

Gary Sinise (Reindeer Games) stars as astronaut Jim McConnell, originally slated to be the mission commander and first man to set foot on Mars until he pulled himself from rotation to care for his sick and dying wife (Kim Delaney, NYPD Blue). His best friend, Luc Goddard (Don Cheadle, Out of Sight) becomes the newly appointed commander who leads his team to the face of Mars where a mysterious force kills all but Luc, supposedly leaving him on the barren planet to survive on his own.

McConnell, along with Woody Blake (Tim Robbins, Arlington Road), Terri Fisher (Connie Nielsen, The Devil's Advocate), and Phil Ohmyer (Jerry O'Connell, Scream 2) stage a rescue mission which actually suffers the fate described in the opening paragraph. After being marooned on Mars, the rescue team must salvage what they can from the damaged ships to build a ride home while investigating the force that attacked the first mission crew.

The actors take a back seat to the special effects team in this glossy sci-fi extravaganza. Director Brian DePalma (Snake Eyes) wisely opens the film on earth, at the farewell BBQ for the Mission One team on the eve of their liftoff. The long, uncut, traveling shot helps to introduce and establish the characters and their relationships with one another while, at the same time giving the actors one last opportunity to exercise their acting muscles which will afterwards stay dormant during the long space journey ahead of them.

But we really don't mind that much. After all, how long has it been since we've seen a crew repair a breach in their spacecraft with a can of soda and some chewing gum? That's not altogether an accurate description, but it communicates the general idea. Momentous, life-threatening problems are easily and humorously solved, mere seconds before certain and total annihilation. As audience members, we have two choices: We can either groan and roll our eyes at the increasingly impossible situations our heroes overcome; or we can just go along for the ride and enjoy the old fashioned Saturday matinee feel of the film.

Note: That being said, the film does contain scenes of death in outer space that may not be entirely appropriate for young and sensitive children.

Anyone who has seen the promotional trailer of the film knows that the mysterious force on Mars has something to do with the origin of life on our planet. Without giving away the end of the film, let me just say that according to the filmmakers, God was not involved in the process. God, of course, has a different viewpoint:

For thus saith the LORD that created the heavens; God himself that formed the earth and made it; he hath established it, he created it not in vain, he formed it to be inhabited: I am the LORD; and there is none else. I have made the earth, and created man upon it: I, even my hands, have stretched out the heavens, and all their host have I commanded. Isaiah 45:18 & 12 (KJV)

Anyone seeking for the origin of life need not travel to Mars to find it. The answers can be found in the pages of a book. A really good book.

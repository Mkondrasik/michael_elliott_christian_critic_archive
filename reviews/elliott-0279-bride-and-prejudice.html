Bride & Prejudice

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 11, 2005

2½ stars

1 hr: 50 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Like-mindedness; marriage; relationships; unity; shared beliefs

Author-verified transcription

Booray for Bollywood! Following the runaway success of Bend it Like Beckham, director Gurinder Chadha once again incorporates her taste of India to her storytelling in an attempt to appeal to a Western palate. East meets West as Bride & Prejudice twists Jane Austin's often copied classic in order to fit a modern Indian story.

The Bakshi family has been blessed with four daughters but arranging marriages for them may be difficult since the family doesn't have much wealth to form a proper dowry. So, when handsome Mr. Balraj from London visits their small town and becomes smitten with the eldest daughter Jaya, there is reason for jubilation.

Balraj is not alone. Accompanying him is his American friend William Darcy who takes a liking to Jaya's sister Lalita. But she sees him as just another arrogant American who looks upon India as being inferior. She is determined to marry for love, despite the frantic efforts of her mother to arrange a financially secure match for her.

This parental interference leads to the introduction of Mr. Kholi, an LA-based Indian who has ridiculously adopted the dress and manners of the American west coast while maintaining a boorish and annoying demeanor all his own. He sets his sights on Lalita while she foolishly falls for a mysterious drifter named Wickham whose dark past is a secret to all but Darcy.

Weaving throughout Austin's familiar storyline are the traditional elements of Bollywood films. Color, music, and lots of movement explode on the screen as the characters break into song and dance at the drop of a turban.

Unfortunately for those involved, Bride and Prejudice doesn't quite have the same crossover appeal that helped propel Bend It Like Beckham's box office success. Give Chadha an A for effort and an intriguing idea but only a C for actual execution. Perhaps the cultural divide proved too great a chasm to cross. After all, the Bollywood "flavor" is a bit strong and something of an acquired taste.

Indian superstar and stunning beauty Aishwarya Rai may be headlining the largely unknown cast but the most memorable performance is turned in by Nitin Ganatra as the ridiculously affected Mr. Kholi. It's a giddy portrayal that conveys infectious fun.

The film emphasizes how difficult it can be to form a loving relationship when people come from two different cultures. Not that it is impossible - but it does present additional obstacles to be overcome.

The key to a successful marriage (or any other relationship for that matter) is for both parties to share the same goals and beliefs. Being like-minded is essential if a couple hopes to travel the road of life together.

It is sometimes difficult for an individual to be singularly focused. It becomes doubly difficult when two separate people must be of one mind. And when those people come from different backgrounds; having been raised in different environments it becomes that much more challenging. But it is available - especially if the couple falls back upon the same source to set their goals and determine their course.

Fulfil ye my joy, that ye be likeminded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind... Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: Philippians 2:2, 5 (KJV)

To have the same mind as Christ means to think the same things he thought - and he always turned to God's Word and set his mind on the truths it contains.

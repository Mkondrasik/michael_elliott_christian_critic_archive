Author-verified transcription

Proverbs 22:15 says that foolishness is bound in the heart of a child. This has to be true... How else can one explain the Pokemon phenomenon?

Pokemon are animal-like creatures with various degrees of powers or abilities at different levels of strength. Under the direction of their human "trainers," they compete against other Pokemon in non-lethal struggles. Their name is an abbreviation of "pocket monsters," a reference to their earliest appearance in a Japanese video game.

In Pokemon the First Movie: Mewtwo Strikes Back, Ash Ketchum is a trainer, with a goal to become the greatest Pokemon master in the world. With his friends Misty and Brock and a pocketful of Pokemon, including his favorite, Pikachu, he responds to a challenge issued by an anonymous individual claiming to be the greatest.

Braving the elements to travel to the mysterious New Island, Ash learns that the challenger is none other than Mewtwo, a bioengineered clone of a rare and mighty Pokemon. Mewtwo has no intention of submitting to any human trainer. Instead, he has cloned his own band of "super Pokemon" for world domination.

It is up to Ash, Pikachu, Snort, Snideparka, Snuggles, and Draggy the Flying Lizard to save the world. (All right, I may have made up a few of those names but there are hundreds of these Pokemon characters and they all have odd-sounding names impossible for adults to remember.)

Artistically, there is little about the movie to recommend. The animation is sub-par, the dialogue is flat and in the case of most Pokemon, nonsensical. Their language appears to consist entirely of their name or derivatives of the same. "Pica... Pica... Chooo!" could mean "get up," "I'm hungry," or "who's that purple guy and why's he got a fire hose sticking out of his head?" depending upon the context.

The feature film is preceded by a short called "Picachu's Vacation" which supposedly gives insight on how Pokemon behave when by themselves, away from their human trainers. The moral seemed to be that Pokemon, while highly competitive, prefer cooperation to confrontation. A similar moral was tacked onto the end of the feature film. "Fighting is bad."

The problem with this is that neither moral is supported by the film which precedes it. The Pokemon are designed to engage in combat. The trainers (which do no training - they merely toss the Pokemon into the fray) gleefully rush to challenge or answer challenges from other trainers. At the end of the film, after the characters have realized the error of their ways, they fortuitously have their memories cleared, presumably so the Pokemon craze may continue unhampered by the insincere moral message of the film. In other words, the true message being delivered is that fighting is bad, but we're going to do it anyway.

The insane popularity of Pokemon should give spiritually minded parents pause. Anything this widely popular (with no apparent rationalization) must have some invisible attraction working for it. Examining the Pokemon stories, we see nothing new or original. It is basically modern day mythology, containing lies regarding power, psychic abilities, life and death.

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it. Proverbs 22:6 [KJV]

A true trainer teaches truth from error. Spiritually, we have an opponent which continually promotes error. Most of the time, it is presented in an innocuous and often appealing way. Our culture offers little resistance. As parents, it is our responsibility to stand in the gap for our children. If we allow the world or culture to raise our children and influence their minds, they will be taught the wrong standard.

Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child. By training our children, using the instruction of God's Word, we prepare them to make sound decisions in life. Until then, we must make the decisions for them. Especially the unpopular ones. Is Pokemon's message really the one we want our children to hear?

Cars

Author-verified transcription

Even the best ball players know that it is impossible to hit a home run every time at bat. It is a fact easy to forget given Pixar Animation's 1.000 batting average. Their latest film, Cars, continues their high level of technical brilliance but the story it tells is not necessarily one to be appreciated by all ages. It's no home run... but not all hits have to be.

Cars has a simple enough plot. Hot shot race car, Lightning McQueen (Owen Wilson) has the opportunity to see his single-minded dream come true - to be the first rookie ever to win the coveted Piston Cup. It has come down to a sudden death 200-lap race off between 3 cars: Himself; the King (Richard Petty); and Chick Hicks (Michael Keaton).

On the way cross country to the LA racetrack where the highly anticipated race is to take place, Lightning mistakenly leaves the high speed interstate and finds himself on the sleepy, windy, fabled road called Route 66 where he ends up in the nearly deserted town of Radiator Springs.

Held there against his will until he repairs the damages to the forgotten town that his recklessness caused, Lightning eventually learns that, by speeding through life, he has bypassed many of life's more appealing aspects: friends, love, and simple pleasures.

Pixar continues to dazzle with its technical mastery. Every frame of this computer animated film is lovingly crafted. And, as with their other films, they excel in matching their animated characters with the celebrities hired to give them voice: Owen Wilson as the headstrong, self-centered dreamer; Paul Newman as a grizzled veteran of the racetrack turned mayor; and Bonnie Hunt as a sleek Porsche who revs up Lightning's motor get the most screen time but not the most laughs.

Larry the Cable Guy as a broken down tow truck and Tony Shalhoub as an Italian tire store shopkeeper with a forklift companion clearly become the audience favorites.

Unlike other Pixar classics, Cars doesn't have a steady run of humor or action that will hold the short attention span of younger audiences. There's a wistful, nostalgic air that calls for a lazier pace throughout half of the film during which time the kids in the theater where I sat grew bored and disinterested. However, there's enough cleverness and wit to entertain their parents should they able to watch the screen between "shushes," bathroom breaks and whines.

Be sure to stay in your seats through the credits for a short but amusing homage to Pixar's other films and their "good luck charm" John Ratzenberger who has been cast in all of their films to date.

The film's lesson is one that adults can appreciate much more readily than children who still have the majority of their life ahead of them. We grownups quickly discover that life moves fast enough by itself. We shouldn't be in that much of a hurry to race through it. By taking our time we can not only reach the destination we desire but we can also enjoy the journey we'll be taking along the way. In the race of life, no one gets extra points for crossing the finish line ahead of everyone else.

By slowing down, Lightning discovers that blur that had been hurtling past his windows were actually opportunities that could enhance his life and the lives of those around him. Such opportunities await each of us. All we need do is take the time to notice them and act upon them.

This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it. Psalms 118:24 (KJV)

Cookie's Fortune

Sermon / spiritual topic: Suicide; endurance; life; community

Author-verified transcription

In an age of Technicolor brilliance and million-dollar computerized effects, there's something refreshing and surprisingly appealing about a movie that depends solely upon a simple story and richly drawn characters. In Cookie's Fortune, Robert Altman (The Gingerbread Man) provides us with a welcome change from the teenage driven drivel that Hollywood oftentimes seems compelled to produce. Here, we have no oil tanker explosions, no 15 foot monsters, no invading aliens, and only one dead body.

The body belongs to the elderly pipe-smoking Cookie (Patricia Neal, An Unremarkable Life) who decides one day to take her own life. Unfortunately, the first person to find her is her least favorite relative, Camille Orcutt, magnificently played by Glenn Close (Paradise Road). Camille can best be described as a cross between Blanche Dubois of A Streetcar Named Desire and Clara Edwards, pickle champion of Mayberry RFD.

Camille, refusing to be tainted by the scandal of a family member's suicide, arranges the scene to make it look like a burglary. Suspicion is cast upon Cookie's house mate, handy man, and longtime friend, Willis Richland (Charles Dutton, Black Dog). Of course, no one in town really believes Willis did it, but because the sheriff has to go through the proper motions, they "arrest" him, keeping him in an unlocked prison cell, where he plays scrabble with the deputies until someone can figure out what is going on. Meanwhile, Camille is directing the church's Easter pageant, a reworking of Oscar Wilde's Salome, with her sister Cora (Julianne Moore Psycho) in the title role. (Cora, it should be noted, appears to have a few colors missing from her mental Crayola box.) Together, Camille and Cora begin to move into the recently deceased Cookie's house, ignoring that pesky yellow crime scene tape which obviously isn't intended for them.

Cora's wayward daughter Emma (Liv Tyler, Armageddon) arrives back in town, much to the delight of Deputy Sheriff Jason Brown (Chris O'Donnell, The Chamber) who is obviously attracted to her. But he may have competition from Manny Hood (Lyle Lovett, The Opposite of Sex), a local fish merchant.

Back at the jail, Willis is the subject of an investigation being conducted by the prosecuting attorney, Otis Tucker (Courtney B. Vance, Ambushed) who is interrogating everybody in town. In response to Tucker's question "Why are you so sure Willis didn't kill the old lady?," Deputy Lester Boyle (Ned Beatty, Life) answers, 'Because.. I fished with him."

Cookie's Fortune is a throwback of sorts, depicting a gentler community, a slower pace, an easier time. Not that it's all Mayberry and Father Knows Best. The modern day world has certainly invaded Holly Springs. I don't ever remember Barney Fife having to handle a suicide. Betty never corralled some boy in the linen closet for some up close and personal action. Even Eddie Haskell wouldn't have let "The Beav" rot in prison for something he didn't do. There's two types of people who are reflected in Cookie's Fortune. On one hand, you have men like Willis, who is not above stealing a pint of Old Turkey if he's thirsty, but he will always return a full bottle in ts place the next morning. On the other hand, we see people like Camille - who is vain, self-centered, and morally skewed (albeit in a Southern genteel kind of manner) who would rather see an innocent man punished than endure the self-imposed shame of a family scandal.

There is certainly a charm to the Southern flavor of the film... A charm to the simplicity of a town who judges a man's character by the time spent with him rather than by his skin color, profession, or bank balance. But error is error, regardless of the charm with which it is presented. Suicide is wrong. There is no justification for ending the life that God has given us. Life is sometimes hard.. But it is always endurable. God gives us the strength to meet and overcome any obstacle.

Cookie's Fortune's title refers to a will she left behind. But what she failed to remember was that her real fortune was the life that she too easily ended.

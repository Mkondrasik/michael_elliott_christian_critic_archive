Author-verified transcription

Not all movies should necessarily be remade. Gloria is one such example.

In 1980, John Cassavettes wrote and directed an interesting little film starring Gena Rowlands as a moll who tries to protect a young lad from being killed by the mob. In 1999, director Sidney Lumet (Critical Care) tells the same story, using a screenplay adaptation by Steve Antin (Inside Monkey Zetterland) and a performance by Sharon Stone (Sphere). What does this 1999 version do that the 1980 version couldn’t? Not one single thing. There are no new insights, no technical upgrades that enhance the telling of the story, no cultural events that make the plot more timely. With the original film on video, there is absolutely no justification for making this newer version. Especially since Mr. Lumet’s version suffers when compared to the earlier one.

Gloria is a two-bit dame being released from prison for a crime she did not commit. She agreed to serve three years behind bars in order to cover for her gangster boyfriend, Kevin. And guess what? He never once came to visit her. Not even a postcard. So, what’s a girl to do? Of course! Go to their hideout, hold a gun on him and his bodyguards, have them strip and throw their clothes out the window.

In so doing, she also manages to take a young seven year old boy with her. We discover that his entire family was gunned down by one of Kevin’s henchmen because the boy’s father had a computer disk detailing the gangster’s operation. The boy is the sole survivor and now carries the disk. The mob wants the boy dead. Gloria is not about to let that happen.

Miss Stone affects a stereotypical “New Yawk” accent, dresses like a tramp, and emotes like a banshee, all the while keeping this highly organized but apparently incompetent crime syndicate at bay.

Jean-Luke Figueroa (Fifty Violins) is the sweet-faced but tough-as-nails boy that stirs her maternal instincts. Jeremy Northam (Mimic) is Kevin, the boyfriend gangster who is more than willing to set aside whatever feelings he might have for Gloria in order to kill her and the boy.

Cathy Moriarty (CopLand) is wasted as Diane, a call girl turned madam, to whom Gloria turns for help. And George C. Scott (Patton) makes a brief appearance on screen as Ruby, head of the syndicate. The Oscar-rejecting Mr. Scott can rest easy. There will be no awards thrust upon him for his performance.

But Mr. Scott’s performance is not the only thing about the film which is second class. From the costumes into which Ms. Stone squeezes her body to the dialect which she manages to squeeze out of her mouth, there’s a tackiness about her character. While it is a fully externalized performance, the inner character... the heart and soul if you will, just doesn’t ring true.

The plot spins around the information contained on a computer disk. Names, figures, events, crimes, all the details needed to bring ruin to the crime syndicate contained on a single piece of plastic. No wonder they want it back so badly.

In the Bible, Job laments at one point, “behold, my desire is... that mine adversary had written a book.” [Job 31:35] Job was making an honest statement here. He didn’t know why he was being beset with all his troubles. If he knew why, he would be on his way to figuring out what he needed to do to stop them. Evil requires secrecy to be successful. The devil would sooner burn us all than to make his ways known to us.

Our adversary may not have written a book, but our God had one written for us that explains what we need to know in order to be victorious over our adversary. Just like in the movie, our enemy will do whatever it takes to keep us from holding onto the contents of this book. And just like Gloria in the movie, we must be resilient, resourceful, and adamant not to let him win.

Author-verified transcription

If a picture is worth a thousand words, then a thousand pictures must certainly be worth enough to warrant a literary volume or two on the psychological quirks of those who took them. And, in the case of Sy Parrish of One Hour Photo, perhaps even of the ones who develop them.

Sy (Robin Williams, Insomnia) is an 11 year veteran of the photo processing lab which is a part of the friendly service of his neighborhood Sav-Mart. Sy, known to his customers as Sy the Photo Guy, takes his job very seriously. Of course, there's not much else in his life competing for his attention.

Single and friendless, his daily activities are both drab and dreary, reflective of a mundane, isolated existence. While his actual life is not much to behold, he does enjoy a rich fantasy life, fueled by the photos which he continually churns out in great and glossy abundance.

One family in particular has captured his fancy. The Yorkins, comprised of father Wil (Michael Vartan, It Had To Be You), mother Nina (Connie Nielsen, Gladiator), and son Jakob (Dylan Smith in his film debut), appear to be a typical, happy, suburban family. Sy has watched the Yorkin family grow over the years and now, in his fantasies, he has adopted them as his own.

Lately, Sy seems to be having trouble keeping fantasy and reality in their separate compartments of his mind. He desperately wants to be seen as their "Uncle Sy" but as time goes on, he learns that the reality of their lives might not be as picture perfect as the photos reflect.

Robin Williams nails the persona of Sy Parrish, a man who is more troubled than he is sinister. Williams fully embodies his character in what should be seen as the finest dramatic performance of his film career. Because Williams makes the isolation and resulting loneliness of his character so palpable, we are kept guessing as to what he might do when the strands of his fragile world begin to snap.

Connie Nielsen plays the relatively normal suburban housewife/shutterbug with the proper innocence and naiveté that most of us adopt when approaching the friendly vendors with whom we deal. Michael Vartan is not given much of an opportunity to offer more than a two dimensional characterization as the husband. Gary Cole (The Brady Bunch Movie) has a few nice scenes with Williams, playing the floor manager of the swept clean and tidily shelved Sav-Mart discount store.

Because of Williams' award-worthy performance, it may be easy to overlook the other elements which helped to make the film so effectively creepy. Predominant among them is the mood which was set by writer/director Mark Romanek (Static). Using the precision details of the process of photo development combined with the florescent-lit environs of a discount warehouse, Romanek communicates a dim and hopeless corporate world to mirror the stark and empty reality that is Sy's life. The only thing that has any color, any vibrancy, or any life are the photos that Sy painstakingly brings into focus. It is no wonder that he takes such care of them.

Life is, of course, much more than the images that can be captured by a photo. There is a fullness to life that can only be embraced by those who recognize the full four dimensional reality of it.

That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith; that ye, being rooted and grounded in love, May be able to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; Ephesians 3:17-18 (KJV)

We, who are born again, have the privilege to see life in a fullness that others can't. God designed life to include a spiritual reality. When we receive God's gift of holy spirit, we can not only see and appreciate life as God intended it to be, we also recognize, perhaps for the first time in our lives, that we are not (nor we will ever be) alone.

Living with that assurance is something that is beyond price. Just ask Sy.

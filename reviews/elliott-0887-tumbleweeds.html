Tumbleweeds

1 hr: 42 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Marriage; Commitment; Submission

Author-verified transcription

Writer Angela Shelton selected Tumbleweeds as the title of her autobiographical screenplay because of an incident which occurred when she was thirteen years old. Her oft-married mother was wont to chase tumbleweeds as she moved from city to city in search of her next relationship. One day, she was actually able to grab one and give it a big hug not knowing it would be a prickly experience. Young Angela equated that image with her mother's experience with men. She seemed always to be chasing after them only to be hurt when she caught them.

Tumbleweeds is an intimately personal film about two extraordinary people and the strong bond which exists between them. Remarkably similar in structure to the recently opened Anywhere But Here (with Susan Sarandon and Natalie Portman), Tumbleweeds uses a relatively unknown cast, a smaller budget, and a simplified story line and still manages to be just as effective as its slick Hollywood counterpart.

Janet McTeer (Velvet Goldmine) is Mary Jo Walker, a free-spirited woman who is overly dependent upon having a man in her life. As the movie opens, Mary Jo is walking out of yet another abusive relationship, dragging her young, foul-mouthed daughter behind her. Heading for parts unknown, she is bound and determined to fall headlong into another doomed relationship unless wiser heads prevail.

The wiser head in this case belongs to her daughter, Ava (Kimberly J. Brown, Christmas in Toon Town). At thirteen, Ava is very much aware of her mother's weaknesses and is not timid about expressing her disapproval of her man-chasing ways.

Tumbleweeds doesn't tell a specific story as much as it allows us to take a look inside their relationship. For all her faults and failings, it is clear that Mary Jo loves her daughter deeply. And despite the frustration and exasperation she feels towards her mother, it is also clear that Ava loves her mother just as deeply. Whether they be fighting or trading secret glances, the bond which has been forged between these two is remarkably strong.

The casting of the two leads has helped tremendously. British actress Janet McTeer fully embraces the character of Mary Jo and believably adopts the vocal patterns of the rural south in order to do so. Her performance is convincing, multi-layered and heartfelt. These same qualities are matched point for point by her costar, Kimberly J. Brown. As Ava, Ms. Brown is able to display a maturity far beyond her tender years without losing that wide-eyed enthusiasm of youth. It is quite a feat for one so young.

Director Gavin O'Connor (Comfortably Numb) pulls double duty by casting himself as a participant in one of Mary Jo's ill-conceived "romances." While it is clear to everyone in the audience that this particular relationship is another in a long line of mistakes, Mr. O'Connor avoids making his character just another loser with a temper. Jay O. Sanders (Music of the Heart) is also on the scene as one of Mary Jo's coworkers and a representative of the kind of "normal" guys with whom Mary Jo should be involving herself.

It is Mary Jo's poor selection of spouses that cause most of her troubles. Her apparent lack of self-worth or self-esteem continually pushes her into relationships with the opposite sex before knowing whether or not the man in question is one to whom she can commit for a lifetime. Her actions are wrong, leading to behavior that gives marriage a bad name. God, as the designer of the marriage relationship, defines the true spiritual roles of those involved:

Wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands, as unto the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church: and he is the saviour of the body. Ephesians 5:22-23 (KJV)

A marriage is not something to be entered into as easily as a pair of old shoes. Nor should it be as easily discarded. The decision to freely submit oneself to another in loving obedience is one which must be made with great consideration and wise counsel. To do otherwise would be an experience likened to kissing a cactus or hugging a tumbleweed. It's possible, just not recommended.

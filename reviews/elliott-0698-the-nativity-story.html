Author-verified transcription

A lot of Christian-themed films of late have been more well-intentioned than well-done so it becomes a rare pleasure when a movie like The Nativity Story comes along. Backed by a New Line Cinema, and headed by an accomplished director (Catherine Hardwicke - Thirteen, The Lords of Dogtown), The Nativity Story tells its well-known tale with the high production values we expect from our secular entertainment.

Though familiar to most, the story of Jesus' birth is often overshadowed by other aspects of his life. Case in point - this is the first feature film from a major studio to solely focus on the events surrounding the birth of Jesus. And yet, what could be more compelling? Two ordinary young people called by God to an extraordinary purpose. Through their believing, humility and faithfulness, they quite literally changed the world by giving it its Savior.

Mary (Keisha Castle-Hughes) is a young woman of Nazareth who has been betrothed to Joseph (Oscar Isaac), described as "a good man." Judean law requires a year of betrothal before the marriage may be consummated. It is during this time that the archangel Gabriel (Alexander Siddig) appears before Mary to deliver the news of God's request - she has been chosen to carry and give birth to the Messiah. Stunned, she nonetheless submits herself to God's will.

As her condition becomes noticeable, Joseph also has a decision to make. Under the law he would be well within his rights to divorce her. But after Gabriel visits him in a dream, he consents to accept the child as his and remain married to her.

When King Herod (Ciaran Hinds) calls for a census, Joseph and his pregnant wife must travel from Nazareth to Bethlehem (the place of Joseph's ancestors). Their journey is long (but not as long as the one taken by the Persian Magi who having continually studied the stars, recognizes God's celestial birth announcement written in the constellations.) Meanwhile, Herod, fearful of the prophecies that speak of a Judean king, issues an edict condemning to death every male child under 2 years old.

The screenplay by Mike Rich (Finding Forrester, Radio) pads the story a bit but doesn't stray too far away from the biblical records. The film does fall into the traditional Hallmark renderings of a celestial spotlight shining on the manger (wouldn't Herod and his army who are searching Bethlehem in search of this child have noticed such a thing?) and having the magi present at the same time as the shepherds (their arrival is historically recorded as being much later). But such depictions are only minor irritants and should not distract us from enjoying the greatest story being told.

I did appreciate how the film tried to show how the Magi did not follow a single star but rather knew how to read the constellations and knew what each star and planet spiritually represented. After all, the birth of God's son was preordained and God recorded his story in the heavens themselves.

The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Psalms 19:1 (KJV)

And God said, Let there be lights in the firmament of the heaven to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years. Genesis 1:14 (KJV)

This astronomical knowledge of the Magi must have been given to them by the prophet Daniel who, during the Babylonian captivity, rose to a position of prominence over the king's astrologers and magicians and would have had ample opportunity to instruct them as to God's names and meanings of the stars.

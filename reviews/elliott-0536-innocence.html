Author-verified transcription

It has been said that love is blind. I'm not so sure about the truthfulness of that statement, but I do know that love is ageless. In fact, proof of this is currently on display in an unusual love story by Australian filmmaker Paul Cox (A Woman's Tale).

It has been over 40 years since Andreas (Charles "Bud" Tingwell, The Dish) and Claire (Julia Blake, Traveling North) enjoyed a passionate and romantic relationship which was abruptly ended by the unexplained disapproval of Andreas's father. Now, in the twilight of their years, Andreas learns that Claire is living nearby and so decides to write to her. Upon meeting, they are rather surprised to discover that their love and passion for each other has not been dimmed by time.

A lot of water has passed under their respective bridges. Andreas has been a widower for 30 years and has a grown daughter as well as a doting housekeeper who keep a close eye on him. Claire is in a comfortable but loveless marriage with a good man who is completely unaware that he isn't meeting her emotional needs. The feelings that stir within her upon meeting Andreas again have been lying dormant for 20 years.

Andreas and Claire thus begin an odd affair. Odd in that, declaring that they are too old for lies, they do not hide their relationship from anyone. Claire's husband (Terry Norris, Lucky Break) moves from incredulity to jealous rage to finally, desperation as he tries to make amends for 20 years of unintentional neglect.

It's an old formula but because the participants of this love triangle are also old, there's a unique freshness to the story. This is due to the fact that the problems and concerns of the characters involved are simply not explored on film very often. Writer/director Paul Cox is to be commended for not shying away from a story which so strongly features the elderly in a romantic light... a light in which we rarely see them cast.

Cox treats the subject matter with a great deal of respect and sensitivity. He generously sprinkles the film with many flashback scenes of a young Claire and Andreas when they were obviously very much in love and very taken with one another. He perhaps indulges a bit on the excessive side with old couple/young couple juxtapositioning but he does get his point across.

All of the actors involved give believable and poignant performances. Terry Norris, in particular, is superb in his portrayal of the gray-haired cuckolded husband who travels through a range of emotions as his awareness of his wife's infidelities (and the reasons for them) become clearer to him.

As well-portrayed and affable as these characters are, we still find them in the midst of an uncomfortable situation that is entirely of their own making. Claire, long discontented with the state of her marriage, chose to remain silent for many years, compromising her own happiness instead of confronting her husband and working towards an acceptable resolution. Claire's husband didn't follow the exhortation of 1 Corinthians 7:3. By failing to render "due benevolence," a King James euphemism for conjugal duty, he inadvertently led his wife to consider taking the actions she did.

Marriage is a dual proposition. Both parties must work together to make a marriage successful. A key element that must be included in any long-term relationship is to address issues of concern immediately. Surmountable problems, if left unattended, can soon seem insurmountable; resentments will be allowed to build; and division becomes likely if not inevitable. Scripture reminds us not to let unresolved matters remain unresolved.

Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: Ephesians 4:26 (KJV)

Handling matters of strife or contention as soon as they arise keeps a relationship honest, pure, and healthy. Marriage partners should be like-minded. This quality doesn't automatically occur. Open and honest communication is vital. Something that the characters in this film should have learned many decades ago.

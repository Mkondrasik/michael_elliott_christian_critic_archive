Author-verified transcription

David Spade’s newest release, Lost & Found, is not so much a movie about a dog as it is merely a dog of a movie.

Mr. Spade (TV’s Just Shoot Me) plays Dylan Ramsey, a man with money problems, girl problems, personality problems… He’s just got problems, OK? When a beautiful French cellist named Lila Dubois (Sophie Marceau, A Midsummer Night’s Dream) moves next door, he must think of a way to spend time with her so she can find out there’s more to him than the sarcastic jerk everyone knows him to be.

Well, what better way to steal a girl’s heart than to first steal her dog and then pretend to help her look for it? This is the basic premise of the movie, and yes, it is as wafer thin as all that.

Layered on top of this ridiculous excuse for a story are a few subplots: Dylan’s restaurant needs a loan from the bank to stay open. His business partner (Mitchell Whitfield, My Cousin Vinny) buys a ring for his wife, then foolishly asks Dylan to hold it for him. Of course, the dog eats it. An overweight waiter at the restaurant (Artie Lange, Dirty Work) decides, frighteningly enough, to make himself over into the mirror image of Dylan. Lila’s ex-fiancee (Patrick Bruel, Sabrina) arrives from Paris in an attempt to win her back. Yada, yada, yada… ::yawn::

Mr. Spade’s acerbic personality, which he has successfully used in his TV series, does not lend itself to a romantic comedy leading role. Truth be told, he had better chemistry with the dog than with the girl.

French stars Sophie Marceau and Patrick Bruel add a bit of European class to these proceedings, but it isn’t long before they are asked to wallow in the same American cesspool which some call contemporary humor.

There’s a lot here to embarrass those who were associated with the film. Director Jeff Pollack (Booty Call) has protected none of his cast from the danger of looking bad. TV veterans Estelle Harris (Seinfeld), Rose Marie (The Dick Van Dyke Show), and Marla Gibbs (The Jeffersons) are among a pitiful group of sexagenarians (accent heavy on that first syllable!) who thrust their strip poker game onto any unsuspecting man they meet.

Mr. Bruel shows us that the French derriere looks just like the American version. Miss Marceau tells a crass and hackneyed joke about female cellists. Artie Lange is indescribably shameful as the waiter Wally. Whether it’s smearing doogy-doo on his face or parading around in a pair of Dylan’s underwear, his antics are largely unfunny and grossly offensive.

Even Mr. Spade, whose vehicle this is, does not escape this movie unscathed. His climatic rendition of Neil Diamond’s “Brother Love’s Traveling Salvation Show” is surprisingly bad. Lip-synching to the oldies have been a trend of current movies for some time now… From Matthew Broderick’s marvelous “Twist and Shout” in Ferris Bueller’s Day Off to Susan Sarandon and clan singing “Ain’t No Mountain High Enough” in Stepmom. Here, it’s obvious that the filmmakers are simply following that trend, oblivious to the fact that it neither serves their film nor is particularly tailored to the talents of their star.

Spiritually, this movie stays in the lost column. Mr. Spade’s character may put on the facade of having an altruistic nature, but all of his “giving” stems from selfish motivations. He looks for and returns the dog because he wants to get closer to the girl. He sings the Neil Diamond song because he wants to get his bank loan approved. This is not the mindset of a godly man.

While it is a spiritual truth that “as you give, you receive,” we are never to give for the sole reason of wanting or expecting something in return. Our giving should come from a pureness of heart and should be designed to bless, edify or comfort those to whom we give. Until we “lose” the selfish “what’s in it for me?” mindset and learn instead how to genuinely give, simply for the joy of it, we will never understand or receive love in its purest manifestation. And that is definitely something worth “finding.” You just won’t find it in this film.

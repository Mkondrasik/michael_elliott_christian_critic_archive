The Score

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 13, 2001
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
2 hr: 3 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Proverbs 25:19Psalms 62:10Jeremiah 17:5-8

Sermon / spiritual topic: Trust; Integrity; Love of Money

Author-verified transcription

The biggest score of The Score may very well be the casting coup which brings together what could arguably be considered the three greatest actors of their respective generations. Marlon Brando, Robert DeNiro, and Edward Norton add their considerable talents to what is essentially a run-of-the-mill crime drama. But not even their exceptional collaboration can totally disguise the unexceptional nature of this project.

DeNiro (15 Minutes) plays Nick Wells, a jazz club owner cum safecracker. After his last, nearly unsuccessful heist, Nick is giving the idea of retirement some serious consideration. It is an idea which is met with guarded optimism from his longtime girlfriend Diane (Angela Bassett, Supernova).

Her hesitant skepticism is understandable. She's heard this song before. And once again, no sooner does Nick store away his safecracking tools, than he is approached by Max Baron (Marlon Brando, The Godfather) who makes him an offer he can't refuse. There's a priceless scepter that is being stored in the Montreal Customs House where Jack (Edward Norton, Fight Club), a young, brash and highly inventive thief has conned his way into occupying a trusted position.

With Jack's help from the inside and Max's assistance in fencing the rare, stolen object, the job appears to be a "cake walk" for a man of Nick's talents... a cake walk ending with a multimillion dollar payoff. But things are rarely as easy as they first appear and the complexities start to build rather quickly.

Director Frank Oz (Bowfinger) builds scene upon scene of exposition and set up. During this elongated opening, there is little tension at work between the characters and, frankly speaking, this makes for some pretty mediocre drama. Eventually, Oz's methodical approach will lead to a very successful final thirty minutes but for the first two-thirds of the film, The Score's "score" would not likely be high enough to win in any game.

Of course, it must be tough to be a screen legend, always having to live in the shadow of one's past accomplishments. Brando's work in this film is paled by the screen icons he has already committed to celluloid. There's just nothing about Max Baron that is particularly memorable. Likewise, DeNiro's portrayal of Nick Wells is lacking the intensity that is so evident in his other screen incarnations. That is not to say that either actor performs poorly. It is just that their characters are not all that compelling.

Edward Norton has more of an opportunity to sink his thespian teeth into his role, playing a con man who pretends to be a mentally impaired custodian in order to ingratiate himself to the guards. It is a risky portrayal and, in the hands of a less accomplished performer, one that might have easily become condescending or offensive. But Norton is both consistent and convincing, without being disrespectful.

Angela Bassett is completely underutilized as Nick's love interest. There was an angle to be played here that could have developed into showing more of the inner conflict raging within Nick: the hope of a normal life with the woman he loves vs. the temptation of a final, easy score. But that conflict was never fully explored.

Instead, Oz relies on a familiar movie truism: there is no honor among thieves. Trust, or rather the lack of trust, is the major issue on display in this film and the single factor which threatens to bring them down.

Confidence in an unfaithful man in time of trouble is like a broken tooth, and a foot out of joint. Proverbs 25:19 (KJV)

Misplaced trust can prove to be disastrous for the one who needs to rely upon that trust. And since love of money remains the root of all evil, it would be inadvisable to ever place ourselves in a position where we must trust a person whose love of money is his sole motivation.

He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver; nor he that loveth abundance with increase: this is also vanity. Ecclesiastes 5:10 (KJV)

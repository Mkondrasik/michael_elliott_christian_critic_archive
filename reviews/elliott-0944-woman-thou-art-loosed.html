Woman, Thou Art Loosed

Sermon / spiritual topic: Forgiveness

Author-verified transcription

That which we most desire to receive is often difficult to give to others. In Woman, Thou Art Loosed, a film based upon AME evangelical preacher T.D. Jakes' self-help book, a young woman on death row struggles with the concept of forgiveness.

Michelle Jordan (Kimberly Elise) did not have an ideal childhood. She was sexually assaulted as a young girl by her mother's boyfriend (Clifton Powell) and called a liar when she told her mother (Loretta Devine) about it. It is an event that unfortunately helped to shape the unhappy life to follow. Growing older, she becomes addicted to drugs and enters into one abusive relationship after another.

After serving time for drug possession, she is determined to go straight and change the course of her life but her past continues to haunt her. Part of her parole requires her to attend Bishop T.D. Jakes's three day revival meeting. During that time she has the opportunity to confront her mother, her drug dealer, the manager of the strip club where she worked and a childhood friend who seems keen on rekindling a "normal" relationship.

Even as Bishop Jakes preaches from the pulpit and implores people to turn their lives over to Christ ("There's room at the cross," he bellows) we're never quite sure whether the message of God's deliverance is able to penetrate Michelle's hardened shell.

Woman Thou Art Loosed is a rung or two above the average evangelical film produced by Christian organizations because of the quality of talent appearing before the camera. Kimberly Elise, last seen in The Manchurian Candidate, is quite effective playing the emotionally damaged Michelle. She keeps a guarded, "tough girl" front which she occasionally lets down to show the vulnerability hidden underneath. By doing so, she is able to generate the empathy needed for the audience to care about what happens to Michelle.

There is ample talent to support her efforts. Loretta Devine (I Am Sam) co-stars as her mother who stays in denial of the truth rather than risk losing the man who has shown her affection. Clifton Powell gives some textured shading to a totally unsympathetic character. Michael Boatman (Spin City) lends an air of decency and morality as Todd, Michelle's childhood friend who somehow escaped the perverse elements which shaped her life.

The only element which doesn't work is the blatant self-promotion of Bishop T.D. Jakes and his ministry. The film and its message would have been served better had he chosen to take a lower profile. His appearances and the moments taken to explain who he is and why he is important draw us away from Michelle's situation.

The central issue is one of forgiveness. Michelle knows that she has done things in her life that are contrary to the ways of God. For that she requires forgiveness. At the same time, she has had much done to her by others whom she cannot forgive.

God's promise of salvation extends to all men. No one is truly worthy of his forgiveness. It is an act of grace that he freely offers to us. Seeing as he is so willing to forgive us of our sins and trespasses - the weaknesses of our flesh - and the times we have willfully disobeyed him, we must find the strength and meekness to forgive those who have done us wrong. Not because they deserve it - but because we deserve to be freed or "loosed" from the bitterness and bile that resentment and hatred builds.

Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you. Ephesians 4:31-32 (KJV)

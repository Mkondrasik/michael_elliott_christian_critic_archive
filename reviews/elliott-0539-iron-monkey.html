Robin Hood robbed the rich and gave to the poor in merry olde England. Zorro did the same for not so merry California. In Chekiang, a hero also rose to do battle against a corrupt government on behalf of the poor who were too weak to take action themselves. This hero was a kung fu master known only as Iron Monkey.

Mild mannered Dr. Yang (Yu Rong Guang, Shanghai Affairs) dispenses herbs and medicinal advice by day but when night falls, he dons a mask and becomes the protector of the weak and oppressed. With his female sidekick, Miss Orchid (Jean Wang, Once Upon a Time in China IV), he is putting a dent into the coffers of the corrupt Governor Cheng (James Wong, Stooges in Hong Kong). Not even the turncoat Shaolin monks brought in to capture the Iron Monkey are a match for his skill.

When renowned fighter Wong Kei-Ying (Donnie Yen, Highlander: Endgame) comes to town with his young son Wong Fei-Hung, played by actress Tsang Sze-Man in her film debut, the governor seizes on what he considers to be his best opportunity to end the Iron Monkey scourge. Throwing Wong Fei-Hung into prison, he gives Wong Kei-Ying exactly a week to capture the Iron Monkey. Things, needless to say, do not go as planned.

Director Yuen Woo-ping choreographed the fight sequences. His work is familiar to American audiences from his action choreography seen in The Matrix and Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon. Iron Monkey, which was filmed 1993 but is just gaining its American release now, does not have the intricate plot of The Matrix nor the romantic subplot of CTHD, but in terms of kung-fu action it is every part their equal.

Fans of the genre will recognize Iron Monkey as being a prequel of sorts to the Once Upon A Time In China series which follows the exploits of the adult kung-fu folk hero Wong Fei-Hung. Here, he's just a child but even at this tender age, he displays amazing skill and resourcefulness.

The action sequences are thrilling to watch, most notably some impressive wire work done by Yu Rong Guang and Donnie Yen atop a series of poles in the midst of a blazing inferno. I also enjoyed hearing the various names of stances and moves used by the kung-fu experts: "Shadow Kick," "Buddha's Palm," "Fisherman's Paddle," "Flying Sleeves," "Grappling Hands," "Ugly Virgin," among many others.

The repertoire of kicks, punches, throws and stances which these fighters demonstrate is impressive. Each move has its designed purpose and when used appropriately, achieves its desired effect. Similarly, as Christians, we have a vast repertoire of spiritual resources available to us.

First and foremost, we are blessed with the written Word of God - a standard for truth which 2 Peter 1:3 tells us contains all things "pertaining unto life and godliness." In addition, God has given us His gift of holy spirit with its corresponding nine manifestations which are listed in 1 Corinthians 12:7-11. Also, according to Ephesians 4:11, God has given certain gift ministries to men to be used for "the perfecting of the saints."

God exhorts us, in 1 Corinthians 12:31, to "covet earnestly the best gifts." This is not to say that one manifestation is better than another, or the gift ministry of a prophet is better than a gift ministry of a pastor. All of these resources have their unique value.

What we are to "covet earnestly" is the best gift to meet whatever specific need we currently face. This may change as our needs change day to day. By building our awareness of what God has made available to us and how to use those resources, much like a kung fu master develops his proficiency in a wide array of fighting techniques, we equip ourselves with the "whole armour of God" so we may spiritually handle obstacle or opportunity that comes along.

Author-verified transcription

The Patriot arrives in theaters just in time for the Fourth of July. But make no mistake... This is no vague or symbolic flag-waving, feel-good kind of film. It is a bloody reminder of the horrors and sacrifices of war and the internal conflicts raging within the principled men who fight them for causes in which they deeply believe.

Mel Gibson (Chicken Run) stars as Benjamin Martin, a widowed plantation owner who lives a comfortable life with his seven children in South Carolina during the year 1776. Having fought in the French and Indian War, where he distinguished himself with a ferociousness and brutality which still haunts him today, he is determined to live the remainder of his life in whatever peace he can summon in his heart.

As war with England becomes more and more of a certainty, his principles and belief in the cause of American freedom are at odds with his sense of responsibility towards his children and his fear that his own nature which so horrifically revealed itself during the last war will rise to consume him again.

Even after his eldest son, Gabriel (Heath Ledger, 10 Things I Hate About You), joins the Continental Army against his desires, Benjamin refuses to involve himself in the conflict. It is not until the British are literally on his doorstep, threatening his family with bodily harm and loss of life (and carrying out that threat) that Benjamin realizes that protecting the lives and property of his family will require his taking up arms again. It is not long before he finds himself in command of a ragged civilian militia.

Historically, there may be flaws galore in the telling of the story. Reportedly, Mr. Gibson's character was based on a compilation of many men, including Francis Marion (AKA The Swamp Fox). However, The Patriot has been given a pure Hollywood treatment and thus, historical accuracy may have been the first casualty of this battle.

Director Roland Emmerich (Independence Day) and screenwriter Robert Rodat (Saving Private Ryan) downplayed the political factors which motivated the colonists and emphasized instead the personal losses they suffered at the hands of the British army, particularly at the hands of one unbelievably diabolical Colonel Tavington (Jason Isaacs, The End of the Affair) who was decidedly un-British about the way he so casually butchered colonial men, women, and children. It is this depiction that is immediately visceral and communicable, but not particularly historical.

Still, the battle scenes are effectively albeit vividly staged, the actors well cast, and the high production values are handsomely evident throughout the film. Mr. Gibson is impressive as the reluctant hero, demonstrating a fighting man's spirit under the restraint of family obligations and the specter of the past. Mr. Ledger is likewise impressive as the eldest son. It is the differences between these two characters that provides the movie with its most interesting moral debate.

If one does not act upon one's principles, is one being true to them? In a very powerful scene set in a church where Gabriel was trying to enlist men to fight in the militia, a young woman stood to confront the silence of the men there. She had heard them speak passionately about America and its right to be a free and sovereign nation. She boldly asked the question, "Will you stop at only words? Or will you act upon the beliefs you have so strongly spoken?"

God exhorts us along the same lines when He said via James:

"In the same way, faith (believing) by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead." James 2:17 (NIV)

Believing is a verb which connotes action. In today's world, we are not called to arm ourselves with muskets and tomahawks. We are, however, called to arm ourselves with the sword of the Spirit, which is the Word of God. We, who believe the Word of God to be truth, have an obligation and responsibility to hold it forth. It takes no less courage or conviction to do battle in the spiritual fight by speaking and living the Word of truth in the midst of an unbelieving and oftentimes hostile world than did the physical fight for freedom depicted in The Patriot.

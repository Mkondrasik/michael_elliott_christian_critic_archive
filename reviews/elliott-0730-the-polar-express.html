Author-verified transcription

The Polar Express, a new animated film based on the 29-page award-winning picture book by Chris Van Allsburg, is a solid lock to become the newest movie to reach the coveted must-see hoiday experience. Both the story and the telling of it have a timeless feel that will be sure to delight generations of families in the many years to come.

Director Robert Zemeckis reunites with his Cast Away star and screenwriter (Tom Hanks and William Broyles) to tell the magical story of a little boy who wants to believe in Santa Claus but is beginning to think that the stories he's heard about Christmas and Santa might only be lies told to gullible children.

One Christmas Eve, while trying to stay awake to hear the sleigh bells he is half convinced will never come, he is startled by the arrival of a huge steam-powered train which comes to a stop right outside of his front door. Informed that its destination is the North Pole, the boy makes the decision to climb aboard and thus begins the most exciting adventure of his young life.

Zemeckis and Broyles have fleshed out Van Allsburg's sliver of a story, giving it more dimensions, new characters, a number of moralistic lessons, and a stronger narrative line. But they have thankfully retained the simple and poignant message of the book and have tailored the animation in such a way to match Van Allsburg's distinctive original illustrations.

Using a process called "performance capture", the filmmakers applied sensors to the faces and bodies of the actors who then played out each scene. Their movements and expressions were captured and fed into a computer and reworked by technicians to reach the final animated effect. The end result looks like a Norman Rockwell or Thomas Kinkade oil painting come to life

In addition to Hanks, who plays multiple roles including the train's conductor, the heroic boy and Santa, Peter Scolari, Nona Gaye and Eddie Deezen all play children who are also on the train. Scolari is a shy boy from a poor family who has never known Christmas. Gaye is a young girl who must learn to follow her instincts to lead others. Deezen is a whiny know-it-all (who knows everything except humility.)

There are plenty of action/adventure sequences including an extended roller coaster of a train ride over mountains and across an ice ocean. The musical score by Alan Silvestri includes a number of original songs by Glen Ballard and Alan Silvestri including the raucous dining car dance number "Hot Chocolate" sung by Hanks and the inspirational ballad "Believe" sung by Josh Groban.

Zemeckis and company don't hit a false note throughout the film to capture the magic of childhood and make-believe. The awed sense of wonder and discovery is communicated in every frame. Like James Barrie of Finding Neverland, The Polar Express is fascinated with the process of growing up and whether it is possible to keep one's childlike faith throughout one's entire life.

To be able to believe in something without seeing solid proof first gets harder and harder as we grow older and "wiser." But it is doable and extremely worthwhile.

Jesus said to him, Because you have seen Me, Thomas, you have believed. Blessed are the ones not seeing, and believing. John 20:29 (KVJ)

We are doing ourselves no favors if we limit what we believe to just what we can see. As the conductor of the Polar Express points out, "The most real things in the world are the things you can't see."

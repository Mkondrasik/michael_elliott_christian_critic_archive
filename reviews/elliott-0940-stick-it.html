Stick It

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 28, 2004
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 45 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Rebellion

Author-verified transcription

A "rebel without applause..." That is the label that is given to a hard-headed gymnast by her hardnosed coach in Stick It. Told through the eyes of a talented athlete who once walked away from the sport only to be reluctantly dragged back into it; Stick It is a sassy but rather superficial look at the world of competitive gymnastics.

Haley Graham (Missy Peregrym) was on the Olympic fast track as a nationally ranked gymnast until one day she inexplicably walked off the mat in the middle of the World Championships, costing her teammates their chance to medal. Though she was determined to leave the sport completely, a run in with the law delivers to her an unusual choice: go to jail or go back to the gym and train. She chooses the latter. Whether or not she picked the lesser of two evils remains to be seen.

She enters the gymnastics camp of Burt Vickerman (Jeff Bridges), a coach best known for sending his athletes to the hospital more often than the awards stand. Though they quickly butt heads as she challenges his authority, they eventually learn to respect one another's talents. She begins to get back into competitive shape but must then find a way to redeem herself in the eyes of those she disappointed back in the day.

Missy Peregrym adopts the quick witted and sharp tongued personality of a rebel who has been to the top of the mountain and didn't like the view. She has the athleticism to maintain the illusion of being a world class champion. (In actuality, French gold medalist Isabelle Severino served as her gymnastics double).

The film would probably have been an instantly forgettable teen comedy were it not for the casting of Jeff Bridges as Haley's coach. His performance adds credibility to a rather incredulous story line and creates an enjoyable dynamic between the two central characters.

Of the other athletes, Vanessa Lengies is the only standout as Joanne, a young girl who has so devoted her life to the sport's disciplines that she has forgone all possible distractions. She takes an instant dislike to Haley and her disrespectful attitude to gymnastics until she discovers that her nemesis actually knows some real boys. It's a whole new world about which she is naively clueless.

Writer/director Jessica Bendinger (Bring It On) has a good ear for dialogue and a great sense of humor that serves her well. Many of the laughs are subtle, found in throwaway lines and offhand comments. But the film itself isn't all that deep or meaningful. It is frivolous entertainment that doesn't provide much insight into its subject matter.

Most sports films will emphasize the importance of discipline, perseverance and teamwork. Stick It, as its double entendre title may suggest, is not like most sports films. Haley, fed up with the unfairness she sees in the arbitrariness of judging, decides to do something about it. Her solution is one that is both simple and surprisingly effective.

The lessons the film is preaching are these: Rule 1: Life is not fair. Rule 2: Nothing we do can change Rule 1. However, we can change how we react to the daily injustices that occur around us. Rebellion does not have to be a violent or physical conflict. Sometimes it is enough to hold onto one's integrity and refuse to go along with the flow even though it might be easier or safer to do so.

The Bible is filled with examples of rebels who resisted the injustices of the world - not through a show of force but by simply refusing to cower in the face of adversity. The world was changed (and can be changed again) by people who have the courage of their conviction and the boldness to act... even when that action is one of passivity.

Author-verified transcription

In the black and white sitcom world of June and Ward Cleaver, life was simpler than today. Rules were understood and followed without question. Holding hands was the height of romantic commitment. Toilets... well, they were nonexistent.

Pleasantville is one such sitcom that was "rerunning" along smoothly in its sterilized state as the anchor program of TV Land, a retro cable station. One day, a mysterious repairman (Don Knotts, The Andy Griffith Show) shows up at the door of David and his sister, who are transported into the black and white world of Pleasantville, where they adopt the bubble gum personas of Bud and Mary Sue Parker.

Their entry into the "repressed" society of 1950's television does not go unnoticed. As they impress their 20th century "values" and worldly wisdom onto the naiveté of the gray or pasty white residents of the Mayberry-like town, color begins to creep into the scene. It is a change that is not universally welcomed.

This is a concept that could have been written off as a one-joke movie about the anachronisms of 1990 people in a 1950 world. Sort of a Blast From The Past in reverse. But the script by Gary Ross (Dave), who also directed the film, is far too intelligent and multi-layered to be so dismissed.

The black and white world of Pleasantville may appear pristine and pure, but when examined more closely, it is found to be repressed and close-minded. As Bud and Mary Sue spur people to act independently or differently than they have in the past, those people move from a bland or gray existence into a technicolored explosion of variety and change.

Along the way, Mr. Ross takes aim at such social issues as race relations, book burning (shades of Nazism), and censorship, as we see those who remain in black and white adamantly resist the spread of the "vulgar" expression of color.

Undeniably, Pleasantville's introduction into color manifests itself in a sexual way. It is, in a way, understandable as 1950's television was heavily censored in that area. Parents should be advised that there is an overt sexual theme running throughout the film that make it inappropriate for children. But it would be a mistake for adults to totally discount the film solely due to its blatant sexuality.

As Jennifer/Mary Sue, Reese Witherspoon (Fear) plays a character who actually moves from being a tramp to one whose "color" comes from a growing desire for intellectual pursuits. As David/Bud, Tobey Maguire (Deconstructing Harry) gains his color when he loses his fear of confrontation and stands up for something in which he believes.

William H. Macy (Air Force One) plays a man who doesn't colorize until he can publicly admit that he loves his wife. By the way, Joan Allen (The Ice Storm) plays his wife, Betty, in a performance so perfected that you might think, at times, you were watching an actual 1950's sitcom.

Where the movie misses the mark is in the exclusive painting of the two extremes. It leads us to think that the only choice of life we have is between a stark black and white existence or a garish multicolored existence. God tells us that the secret and beauty of life is found in moderation.

And every man that striveth for the mastery is temperate in all things. 1 Corinthians 9:25a (KJV)

There's a great middle ground between having no sex and having indiscriminate sex. That middle ground is what the movie ignores. In Pleasantville, sex goes from being a complete unknown to being the favorite pastime of high school students. This is certainly not the message that we parents wish to present to our children.

But, if you'd like a spiritual analogy to use... try to look at the film this way: Like the good people of Pleasantville, we too are born to a black and white existence. As natural men, we have no spiritual connection to God... until we get born again of His spirit. Once that happens, using the movie's example, all the colors of the rainbow (the multifarious resources of God) open up to us. We just need to learn to incorporate His color into our black and white world.

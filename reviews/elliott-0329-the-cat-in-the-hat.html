The Cat in the Hat

Author-verified transcription

Mike Myers dons a fur suit and prosthetic makeup to play one of the most beloved characters in children's literature, The Cat in the Hat. Somehow, I don't think this is what Theodore Geisel (aka Dr. Seuss) envisioned when he first dreamed up his colorful and moral-teaching mischief maker.

In the Seuss-like town of Annville lives Sally (Dakota Fanning) and Conrad (Spencer Breslin) with their perfectly coifed real estate salesman of a mom (Kelly Preston). With mom hosting an important party for her fastidiously clean boss (Sean Hayes), she goes to work leaving her children with one crystal clear instruction: "Don't go in the living room."

With their baby-sitter (Amy Hill) asleep, rain beginning to fall, and no fun on the horizon, who should appear but the magical Cat in the Hat with more fun under his topper than any barrel of monkeys could ever produce? With their pet fish as the only voice of reason (one that is continually being ignored), Sally and Conrad follow the Cat along a series of adventures which quickly escalate to have disastrous effects. What will Sally and Conrad say to mom when she finds out that her house has been destroyed by all the "fun" they were having?

Except that this isn't much fun. So much has been added to the story, presumably to stretch it out to fill its 80 minute running time, that Dr. Seuss's simple and imaginative tale kind of gets lost in the shuffle. Such additions include a sleazy neighbor (Alec Baldwin) trying to score with the sexy mom; a lengthy outdoors excursion which includes a stop at a child's birthday party and an underground disco, and a Dali-esque themepark ride which is such a shameless advertisement that it is admittedly funny.

The production design copies Dr. Seuss's recognizable illustrative style, starting with cartoon renderings of the studios logo.

Of course, key to the success or failure of the film is the titular character. The problem is that this cat has fleas. Mike Myers, while a gifted comic, does have a crude side which he often exploits. While he's toned down considerably it's not nearly enough for a film that should be targeting preschoolers as their primary audience. The Cat in the Hat is liberally sprinkled with innuendo and crude humor which is inappropriate for the material.

Whereas Jim Carrey managed to capture the essence of Geisel's Grinch (though that too was a flawed adaptation), Myers delivers to us an odd characterization that has little to do with the book. He adopts a New York accent and borrows physical gestures from Bert Lahr's Cowardly Lion. It is far too calculated a performance and it is missing the primary feature of the Cat's personality... A sense of fun.

Still, the movie, like the book, does have a message that parents can share with their children. That having fun can be OK, as long as it is kept within certain boundaries. When we stray outside those boundaries, even though it might seem like fun at first, bad things can happen. Rules are made, not to be broken, but to be upheld. They are for our benefit.

Ye therefore, beloved, seeing ye know these things before, beware lest ye also, being led away with the error of the wicked, fall from your own stedfastness. 2 Peter 3:17 (KJV)

Dr. Dolittle 2

Michael Elliott

Author-verified transcription

He's back and he's talking smack.. animal style. Eddie Murphy (Shrek) stars in Dr. Dolittle 2, the sequel to his 1998 hit based upon the Hugh Lofting stories about the man who has a way with words... as well as a way with whinnies, bleats, barks, croaks, chirps, and growls.

In the first film, Dolittle was a milquetoast vet who was having trouble accepting the fact that he could actually talk to the animals. In the sequel, Dolittle is a confident animal expert celebrity. He still can talk to the animals, but now he's having problems communicating with his wife Lisa (Kristen Wilson, Dungeons & Dragons) and troubled teen aged daughter Charisse (Raven-Symone, The Cosby Show).

Invited by a raccoon Mafioso-type, Dolittle visits his boss, "the beaver," who makes him an offer he can't refuse. He is asked to help them save their forest from being razed by an evil developer (Jeffrey Jones, Ferris Bueller's Day Off). The plan is to get the forest protected under the endangered species act. The species in trouble is Eva, a female Pacific Western bear. In order to get classified as endangered Dolittle must find her a mate so the species has a chance for continuation. Thus the future of the forest and its denizens rests entirely upon the broad shoulders of Archie, a circus bear who has never lived in the wild and is a bit of a dork when he gets around the ladies... bears, that is. Dolittle has three weeks to reintroduce the singing, dancing Archie to his animal instincts.

As with the original film, Dr. Dolittle 2 relies, in part, on crude bathroom humor scaled down slightly to receive the PG rating. Still, there are scenes that are in questionable taste, including one that provides a different answer to the rhetorical question, "Does a bear poop in the woods?"

The animal voices and cinematic tricks which make them appear to be speaking are, as they were in the first film, the source of most of the humor. Steve Zahn (Saving Silverman) lends his voice to the spoiled and whiny Archie; Norm MacDonald (The Norm Show) reprises his role as Lucky the Dog who spends much of the film "marking his territory;" Lisa Kudrow (Friends) makes for an alluring lady bear; and Michael Rapaport is quite funny as the thuggish Joey the Raccoon ("Hey! The beaver offers you a fish, you take the fish!")

The human actors perform well enough even though they do not get the same number of laughs their animal costars receive. Eddie Murphy appears to be relaxed and comfortable in the role of Dolittle. Kevin Pollak has some nice though brief moments as the incredulous lawyer in the employ of the developer.

Director Steve Carr (Next Friday), working from a script by Larry Levin who penned the first film, keeps the film's focus upon the animals and manages to present a humorous, if somewhat slight and trivial, comedy.

As we mentioned in our review of the first film, the attributing of speech to animals and therefore "humanizing" them is a rather silly practice. But the reverse, attributing animal characteristics to man or angel, is used throughout the Bible in order to bring emphasis to truths God wants us to remember. Thus we read:

Who satisfieth thy mouth with good things; so that thy youth is renewed like the eagle's. Psalms 103:5 (KJV)

The eagle will plunge itself into an earthbound dive in order to peel of his old feathers, revealing the new youth-like growth that exists underneath. Obviously, we do not take this passage to mean that we are to literally do the same. The truth can be found in the figurative application. Like the eagle shedding his old growth, we can shed our old-man nature, allowing the new man which is born again in Christ to have dominance in our lives and fill us with a renewed energy, strength and vigor.

There are many examples like this that can be found in God's word. The animals may not be able to talk to us but there is still much we can learn by observing the animal kingdom.

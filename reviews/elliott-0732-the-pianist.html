Author-verified transcription

Director Roman Polanski (Chinatown), himself a survivor of the Jewish ghetto in Nazi-occupied Warsaw, has in The Pianist, crafted a remarkably honest and intimate look at the Holocaust horror which today, even after countless books and movies have been made on the subject, remains almost unimaginable. Based upon the autobiography of Wladyslaw Szpilman, The Pianist traces one man's incredible survival amid the devastation and atrocities committed during WWII.

Szpilman (Adrian Brody, The Affair of the Necklace) was an accomplished pianist living in Warsaw with his affluent family. We first see him playing for Polish radio even as the German bombs are exploding outside the walls of his studio.

Little by little the Nazis take away the rights, then the dignity, finally the lives of Warsaw's Jewish population. After he is separated from his family, who were herded like cattle to one of the Nazi concentration camps, Szpilman hides himself in war-ravaged Warsaw. He first finds refuge within the Jewish sector where often survival was randomly determined by the whim or mood of the sadistic and brutal German guards.

Later, he opts to take his chances on the other side of the wall where it was "verboten" for Jews to dwell. Relying on the brave assistance of Polish citizens who provided him with some food and shelter (though both woefully meager), Szpilman survives one day at a time and is witness to the devastation which was brought to his country and countrymen.

His actions are not what we'd term heroic... at least not in any traditional sense. But at a time when all reason is gone, all man has left is his basic instinctual desire to survive... which Szpilman does, against all odds.

Adrian Brody is brilliant as Wladyslaw Szpilman, not only capturing the confident, talented artist we see at the beginning of the film but also the gaunt, frightened, guilt-ridden survivor that picks his way through the Warsaw rubble at the end. He never leaves the screen and your eyes will never leave him. A marvelous performance in which he shows considerable restraint and control.

This is Roman Polanski's success however. The Pianist is the movie masterpiece for which he will be remembered. There have been some critical complaints that the film is too emotionally detached; that we don't have a visceral connection with the characters or understand how they are emotionally responding to the events around them. I believe this, in itself, is a tremendous statement that Polanski is making.

The enormity of the events; the unexplainable hatred and evil which showed its face; the amount of loss and grief mixed with the guilt of surviving... It is all too much for human comprehension. We record, as does Szpilman, what is happening with a sense of disbelief. That disbelief later must grow to a kind of detached awareness for to emotionally connect with the sights, sounds, and smells of the horror would be too much for us to bear.

And yet, it is a story of survival. As such, as horrific as it is, we can be reminded of God's grace, mercy, and deliverance.

For the enemy hath persecuted my soul; he hath smitten my life down to the ground; he hath made me to dwell in darkness, as those that have been long dead. Therefore is my spirit overwhelmed within me; my heart within me is desolate. I remember the days of old; I meditate on all thy works; I muse on the work of thy hands. I stretch forth my hands unto thee: my soul thirsteth after thee, as a thirsty land. Selah. Psalms 143:3-6 (KJV)

The psalmist (David) is pouring his heart out to God in the midst of persecution and enemy attacks, but he is also driving his mind to the promises of God and the works and wonders God has brought to pass in the past. God does not promise that we won't see hard times or suffer injustices at the hand of our fellow man. What he does promise us is that His Word will comfort, guide and strengthen us as we work our way through such times. He also promises that all injuries and injustices will be addressed to our complete and total satisfaction.

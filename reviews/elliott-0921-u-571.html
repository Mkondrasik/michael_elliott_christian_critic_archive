Author-verified transcription

Das Boot can rest easy. Its claim to the "Best Submarine Movie Ever Made" title remains unchallenged. The newest contender, Universal's U-571, did not usurp the crown. Not that all the ingredients weren't present for a possible upset. Good story, fine acting, great special effects... But U-571 adds up to less than the sum of its individual parts, leaving disappointment in its wake.

The premise is a clever one, inspired by a compilation of actual events. During WWII, Hitler's U-boats played havoc in the Atlantic, interrupting the flow of supplies between America and Europe. Much of Germany's success was attributable to the Enigma code which allowed them to transmit indecipherable messages without fear of compromise.

It became crucial that the Allied Forces be able to decode these unreadable messages. According to the movie, their opportunity came when a German U-boat is crippled in the high seas. The Americans immediately launch a secret mission to take control of the vessel and obtain the Enigma machine. They just can't let the Germans know that the Enigma code is no longer a secret. Of course, the Germans had already launched a rescue mission of their own. The race to U-571 is on and the movie has just started.

Matthew McConaughey (EdTV) stars as Lt. Andrew Tyler, the executive officer whose leadership under pressure remains untested. Having just been turned down for command of his own vessel (scuttled by his acting captain no less), Lt. Tyler must deal with his own damaged pride and confidence while still performing the duty required of him as a naval officer.

He gains the opportunity to prove himself when he, along with 8 of his young sailors, successfully board and take control of the German U-boat.

Before they can accomplish their mission and return to their own vessel, the German rescue ship appears and blows the American boat out of the water, leaving the 9 seaman trapped on the crippled German sub. The only way home for our heroes is to fix the sub, destroy the German ship, and sail to safe anchorage through hostile waters without further incident.

Mr. McConaughey does fine work as the unseasoned officer. He is able to maintain a military bearing, yet show enough doubt to indicate why his skipper thought him unready for command. His maturing as a leader of men is the subplot (no pun intended) of the story.

The rest of the cast, while competent, fill stereotypical roles: the grizzled veteran who teaches the young officer how to command (Harvey Keitel, Holy Smoke); the young sailor who questions the wisdom of his captain (Erik Pallandino, Can't Hardly Wait); the desk-jockey intelligence officer who must find the courage to act in actual combat (Jake Weber, Pushing Tin.)

The stereotypes are there in abundance as are the clichés: (How does one visually depict a submarine that goes too deep? Squeeze an egg, of course.) Perhaps that is why the film disappoints. All this is so familiar that we are not engrossed in the story, merely curious as to how it is being presented.

Lt. Tyler learns the truth of the old adage, "He who hesitates is lost." The reason why soldiers train, study, practice, and theorize before the conflict is so that while in the midst of the conflict they may act without hesitation or fear. The disciplined habits developed at times of peace allow men to act instinctively when the pressure is on.

It is the same reason we study the Word of God continually. It is our defense in the spiritual battle against our adversary. Our success in that battle will lie in how well prepared we are when the attacks (be they in the form of trial or temptation) hit.

Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand. Ephesians 6:13 (KJV)

The armor must be put on before the battle starts in order for it to be effective.

A Cinderella Story

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 16, 2004

1 hr: 35 min

Sermon / spiritual topic: Self-esteem; identity; individuality

Author-verified transcription

No glass slipper. No magic pumpkin. No fairy godmother. The only thing A Cinderella Story has going for it is a princess - pop princess Hilary Duff to be specific. But in the end, she's not enough of a draw for this tale to live happily ever after.

Sam (Hilary Duff) is a young high school student oppressed by a self-centered stepmother (Jennifer Coolidge) and two cruel and unusually dense stepsisters (Madeline Zima, Andrea Avery). Not only does she have to wait on these ungrateful and manipulative witches at home, she also must pull multiple shifts at the diner which her late father owned and is now being run (into the ground) by his widow.

Sam's dream is to escape her miserable, slave-like existence by being accepted into Princeton. This dream is shared by her anonymous "text message buddy" who goes by the user name Nomad. Nomad turns out to be the high school equivalent of Prince Charming - Austin Ames (Chad Michael Murray) the star quarterback and student body president who surprisingly possesses the soul of a sensitive poet.

Sam is clued to his identity first and she fears rejection should he ever discover that she is the "diner girl" that his cool friends like to humiliate. So she doesn't tell him, forcing him to search high and wide for the girl he knows only as "PrincetonGirl."

It's actually a kind of cute, if unoriginal, idea to deconstruct the Cinderella tale and set it in a modern and realistic setting. It has worked in the past and could have worked here. But with uninspired direction from TV helmer Mark Rosman and a script from first-timer Leigh Dunlap, mediocrity abounds and this fairy tale, try as it might to make the leap into something more regal, can't help but to stay a frog.

Sixteen-year-old Duff has yet to show any range beyond what she has already displayed as Lizzie Maguire. She manages to smile, cry, and stare blankly on cue which is all that is really asked of her. It is a "safe" performance in a "safe" film. Parents with young children will be pleased. I'm not so sure about the rest of us.

Jennifer Coolidge, as the evil stepmother, has been given the juiciest lines to deliver but most of them can be seen in the film's widely marketed trailer. It is a caricaturized role to be sure and one which loses appeal as the minutes tick by.

Chad Michael Murray plays the kind of guy who young girls dream about when they play the board game Mystery Date - handsome, kind, sensitive... and a bit bland when you come right down to it. Dan Byrd, as Sam's best friend Carter, has the misfortune of being a dead ringer for Harry Potter. The subplot which has him lusting after the "mean girl" of the school (Julie Gonzalo) is a bit far fetched which means that it fits right in with the rest of this non-fairy fairy tale.

The major lesson the film tries to communicate is that we should have the courage to be ourselves - to show people who we really are instead of trying to become what we think others want us to be. Although the film doesn't amount to much, the lesson is worth learning.

Self-esteem problems can beset anyone. They are usually brought on when we make the mistake of comparing ourselves to someone else.

Instead, we should get into the habit of appreciating who we are and valuing the work God has wrought in us. If we would simply take the time to see ourselves as God sees us, we need never be insecure again.

Author-verified transcription

“Do what you always do.” That’s the recommendation made to aging NY cop Jack Mosley in 16 Blocks. We can imagine the studio heads saying the same thing to the film’s star who, to his credit, is willing to put the word “act” back into action hero.

Bruce Willis plays Mosley as a tired boozehound of a cop just trying to put one foot in front of the other until he reaches the retirement that seems to be just around the corner. He’s long lost interest in the job (or anything else for that matter) and finds his solace from whatever bottle is in front of him.

Into his lap falls a low level nuisance of an assignment. He is to transport Eddie Bunker (Mos Def), a talkative prisoner, to the courthouse 16 blocks away so he might testify before a grand jury. What he doesn’t know is that a battalion of cops, including Mosley’s partner of 20 years (David Morse), are lying in wait to stop Bunker from exposing them as murderous thugs.

It’s a simple premise of cat and mouse as Mosley must muster the courage and smarts to outwit and outgun the entire NYPD in order to let justice prevail. It’s a role Willis has played before but never before with such lack of self-aggrandizement. He’s climbed into the skin of a man who has clearly let himself go (both physically and psychologically).

Willis is not afraid to look bad and his fearlessness greatly improves the effectiveness of the film. Mosley is a wreck of a man who redeems himself during the course of the film.

Willis also allows his costars to shine. Mos Def has a far showier role, jabbering nonstop from beginning to end with his high pitched observations and complaints. His dialogue was not always understandable to my ear, but he did manage to communicate the sweet simple-mindedness of his character that may or may not be an act.

David Morse sinks his teeth into the role of the villain, a cop who has crossed one moral line too many and now finds himself defending and justifying a corrupt and untenable position.

Director Richard Donner deftly maneuvers 16 Blocks through its required paces; building both tension and momentum as the story unfolds. The ending is admittedly too reliant on a long clichéd bad guy mistake but it really doesn’t lessen the entertainment level of the movie to any great extent.

The admonition that Mosley received early on - “Do what you always do” - was not intended to be encouragement. Those giving it wanted him to follow the predictable pattern that his life has thus far indicated. They wanted him to “play ball” and look the other way.

Instead, Mosley is reminded that change - no matter how drastic - is always available. We are not bound to the conditioning of our past any more than we are limited to our present situation. Each of us have the ability to change; to leave the path we are on and redirect ourselves to a more worthy endeavor.

Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out, when the times of refreshing shall come from the presence of the Lord; Acts 3:19 (KJV)

The Bible gives us one of the greatest examples of change in history. Saul once persecuted the followers of Christ. He later became the greatest apostle Christianity has yet produced. He changed. He did not do what he always did. He saw the light and followed his desire to leave the darkness.

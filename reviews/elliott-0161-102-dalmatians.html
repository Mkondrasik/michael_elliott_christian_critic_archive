102 Dalmatians

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 22, 2000
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Animals; dominion; stewardship; humanity; God's created order

Author-verified transcription

There are two kinds of sequels that get made in today's Hollywood. There are those which add to the story line of the original, providing additional depth or insight into the lives of the characters which was not covered by the first film. These, admittedly, are rare.

The second kind of sequel is made to capitalize on the popularity and financial success of the original film, even though there is nothing new to say. Disney's 102 Dalmatians belongs to this latter group.

Cruella DeVil (Glenn Close, reprising her role), while being incarcerated for her misdeeds depicted in 101 Dalmatians, underwent radical behavior control therapy. Now, completely rehabilitated, she is released and under the watchful eye of her parole officer, Chloe Simon (Alice Evans, Le Dernier Secret) who just happens to be the owner of Dipstick, one of the puppies from the first film - now grown and with a canine "family" of his own... a litter which includes Oddball, a Dalmatian pup who has yet to sprout a single spot.

Cruella ("just call me Ella"), as a result of her treatment, now loves animals and becomes the benefactor of a home for stray dogs called Second Chance. It is run by the cute but kind of goofy Kevin Shepherd (Ioan Gruffudd, Shooters). Chloe is suspicious and her apprehension proves valid when Cruella snaps out of her nice personality and becomes the evil fur-loving villain we remember from before.

From that point on, the film pretty much develops its deja vu quality. Cruella still wants a Dalmatian spotted coat. This time, one with a hood which explains the need for the extra puppy in the title. She directs her loyal valet Alonso (Tim McInnerny, returning in his role) to steal enough dogs to allow the famous furrier Jean Pierre le Pelt (Gerard Depardieu, Man In The Iron Mask) to make it for her.

Leave it to Kevin and Chloe, along with Dipstick and the dog communication network of the UK, to save those pups from becoming tomorrow's bold fashion statement.

Glenn Close is as maniacally evil as she was in the first film. Her over-the-top performance is not too scary for the young ones but she makes it clear that her character is not a nice person. The fine French actor Gerard Depardieu has many embarrassing moments... notably his first entrance as he parades down a fashion walkway wearing a pair of furry shorts. But his role is so outrageously overplayed, the children will no doubt be tickled with this ridiculous man with the Brillo pad haircut and the funny accent.

However, the star of this picture is neither human nor of the four-pawed species. Waddlesworth (voice by Eric Idle, Dudley Do-right), a rather verbose Macaw who thinks he's a rotweiller, steals most of the scenes with his sarcastic wit and adamant belief that despite his wings and feathers, he's a dog through and through.

This is a nice G-rated family picture which may move a trifle slow for some of the young ones, but contains nothing overtly offensive. There is some cartoon violence, especially when Cruella gets her just desserts in a patisserie (pun most definitely intended), but it is nothing too suspenseful or frightening.

The only negative observation to be made is the same one which was spurred by the original. At one point, Kevin makes the uncontested statement, "Dogs are people too." The truth is, they are not. Animals are not afforded the same God-given rights as man. In fact, God clearly states the relationship man has to the animal kingdom.

And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that moveth upon the earth. Genesis 1:28 (RJ)

Animals were made by God for the benefit of man. They can provide sustenance for us. Their pelts can be used to clothe us. Their size and strength can sometimes be used to provide physical labor. Even as pets, they serve a purpose. But they simply are not "people too" - regardless of how cute, smart, inventive, or personable Disney films may make them appear.

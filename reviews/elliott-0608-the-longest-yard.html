Author-verified transcription

If a director is going to remake a successful and popular film, it would be best if he had a good reason for doing so. Adding a twist; using new and better technology; making it relevant for today’s audiences... all or any of these would certainly be reason enough to revisit a familiar tale. Unfortunately, with The Longest Yard, Adam Sandler and company have chosen none of the above. They are telling the same story as the 1974 original and they aren’t telling it as well.

Sandler plays Paul Crewe, a former NFL quarterback who left the game six years earlier after being accused of shaving points during a game. With a six-pack of beer and a major chip on his shoulder, he takes his girlfriend’s (Courtney Cox) car and leads police on a wild joyride that culminates with him receiving a 3 year prison sentence.

Inside the pen, Crewe is conned into playing ball once again. The warden (James Cromwell), whose semi-pro prison team keeps falling short of the championship, expects Crewe to field a team of inmates for the guards to practice against. With the help of Caretaker (Chris Rock) and Nate Scarborough (Burt Reynolds), Crewe assembles a team that has a real chance of winning against the guards. The warden, of course, has other ideas.

This remake is filled with problems. None are more obvious than its casting. Those who are puzzled by the film success of Adam Sandler will have even more reason to scratch their heads after this film. Does anyone see him as an NFL superstar? Even he seems to think it ridiculous as he makes no attempt to convince us otherwise.

Judging from his performance, Chris Rock apparently cannot contain his glee just to be attached to a Happy Madison production. If only he would have given us a reason to share in his enjoyment. From his first appearance, he is uncharacteristically flat and boring. Burt Reynolds doesn’t fare any better serving more as a box office curiosity than a supporting character.

The script by Sheldon Turner offers no tension, few laughs, and an obscene abundance of product placements. McDonald’s must have spent their entire year’s advertising budget to get as much exposure in this film as they did.

Director Peter Segal has teamed with Adam Sandler twice before (Anger Management and 50 First Dates) but this third time is no charm. He mishandles his star and his story to presents a lackluster sports comedy that is but a poor imitation of a far superior film.

Thankfully, the filmmakers haven’t changed the message of the film. By the movie’s end Crewe must decide between doing what is right or doing what is convenient for him. Both come with consequences. The former choice may cost him his freedom. The latter choice will cost him his soul. In one way or another, we will all be faced with a similar choice. When it comes, may we be led to a decision that meets with God’s approval.

And fear not them which kill the body: but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell. Matthew 10:28 (KJV)

In our lives we cannot control what others may do to us. We can always control how we react to what they do. Be it in the face of good or evil, we can be consistent in our manner by maintaining our integrity and acting with righteousness.

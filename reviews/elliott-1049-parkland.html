Author-verified transcription

Although it has been fifty years since the event, the images of November 23rd, 1963 can still strike a strong emotional chord for those old enough to remember. The Kennedy assassination serves as the pivotal moment in Parkland, a film by Peter Landesman, who, working from a non-fiction book by Vincent Bugliosi, views the event from the eyes of those on the periphery whose lives were forever changed by their role in the chaotic aftermath.

The most familiar name to be featured is Abraham Zapruder who, by happenstance, managed to capture what may arguably be the most studied 26-second film in history. As played by Paul Giamatti, Zapruder immediately recognized the import of what he recorded and how his life could never be the same as a result.

The most sympathetic character, with the possible exception of the widowed Jackie Kennedy (Kat Steffens), is Robert Oswald (James Badge Dale), brother of the assassin. Innocent and unaware of his sibling’s actions or intents he nonetheless bears the guilty by association label as he watches his family name become vilified by a grieving nation.

For irony, nothing can match the valiant efforts of the medical professionals of Parkland Memorial Hospital who were surprised to be called into service in a futile attempt to save the life of a president only to be called into service again a few days later to try to save the life of the man accused of killing him.

Landesman keeps a hand on the authenticity of the piece, not letting dramatic interests deter him from presenting a realistic portrayal of what these days must have been like for those who lived through them. The film may experience some pacing problems that could try the patience of some audience members but it is an intelligent treatment of an epochal moment in US history.

Parkland does give us an opportunity to reflect upon the importance of acting in the face of a crisis. When the fatally wounded president is wheeled into Parkland Memorial Hospital, a young, untested Dallas physician is momentarily frozen by the weight of the responsibility thrust upon him. It takes the sharp reminder of a veteran nurse to prod him into the action he had been trained to do. But once he begins, his training takes over.

It is why we practice to be good at something. When the pressure hits, we must rely on what we already know… what we have already practiced. This is why God commands us to study His Word and meditate on its truths as often as He does. The spiritual power of the spoken Word dispels the negative forces that exist in this world. We cannot wait until we are in the midst of a spiritual fight before we arm ourselves with the only defense that will work in that battle. By preparing ourselves ahead of time through study and prayer, we will be able to act when adversity hits us. And we can emerge victorious as a result.

“Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand.” Ephesians 6:13 (KJV)

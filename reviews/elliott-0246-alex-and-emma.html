Alex & Emma

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 20, 2003
Rating
2½ stars

Original Author Copy

Author-verified transcription

Writers' block. It happens to the best of us, although rarely with fatal consequences. In Alex & Emma, a new romantic comedy by Rob Reiner (When Harry Met Sally), a blocked novelist has 30 days to get himself "unstuck" or else his literary career will come to an end... along with his life.

Alex Sheldon's (Luke Wilson, Old School) first book was a success. It's the second one that is giving him trouble. Unfortunately, he borrowed money from some Cuban loan sharks who don't understand the fickleness of his artistic muse. Having gambled the money away, he now must pay them back in the only way left to him - he must present a finished novel to his publisher. It will be a task made more difficult because the thugs thoughtlessly smashed his laptop as a warning.

With no computer, Alex desperately reaches out to Emma (Kate Hudson, The Four Feathers), a forthright and seriously opinionated stenographer to whom he begins to dictate his story. As the story progresses, so does their relationship which inevitably finds its way woven into the novel being written.

As Alex dictates, the film switches to the story within the story as we see his narrative come to life on the screen. Wilson plays the central figure: a young writer who falls in love with a sexy and beautiful French woman of means (Sophie Marceau, Lost and Found) whose children he is tutoring. Hudson assumes the role of the French woman's au pair, who comically goes from being Swedish to German to Spanish to American because Alex can't seem to make up his mind about the character.

This is a light and often funny romantic comedy played nicely by the two leads. Wilson is goofily charming, although his many character flaws (he's a compulsive gambler, a recalcitrant liar, and a hypochondriac) come and go with little continuity. Hudson is once again adorable and while she'll never be mistaken for having the chameleon-like linguistic skills of a Meryl Streep, her exaggerated dialects are amusing and brief enough not to become tiresome.

The romantic sparks between the two never quite ignite but both characters are likable enough for us to be rooting for them to get together.

Interestingly, the movie is loosely based upon the tale of Fyodor Doestoevsky who wrote his short story The Gambler in 30 days in order to pay off his own gambling debts rather than be forced to sign over the rights to all his works. During that time, he reportedly fell in love with the stenographer to whom he dictated his story.

The screenplay, by Jeremy Leven (The Legend of Bagger Vance) is much tighter than Hudson's last foray into romantic comedy (How to Lose a Guy in 10 Days) and it includes plenty of amusing setups, funny lines, and a sweetly emotional payoff. Director Rob Reiner, who also appears as Alex's publicist, deftly moves between modern times and the 1920s which is when Alex's novel takes place.

The practice of using scribes to record one's words is as old as the scriptures themselves. Not only did many of the great men of the Bible utilize this practice or served in this capacity, but we can honestly say that the entire Bible was, in a spiritual sense, dictated.

Knowing this first, that no prophecy of the scripture is of any private interpretation. For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake [as they were] moved by the Holy Ghost. 2 Peter 1:20-21 (KJV)

There were many men who penned the words which make up the various books of the Bible. But there was only one author. And thankfully for us, He never suffered from writers' block.

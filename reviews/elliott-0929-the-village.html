The Village

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 30, 2004
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 48 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

1 John 4:18Deuteronomy 28:65-66Joshua 1:9

Sermon / spiritual topic: Fear

Author-verified transcription

Hilary Clinton once observed that it takes a village to raise a child. The only thing The Village is raising is the suspicion that writer/director M. Night Shyamalan has run out of tricks.

The residents of Covington, Pennsylvania have forged for themselves a peaceful, nearly idyllic 19th century community. Living off the land, raising their children in a loving and puritanical manner, there is really only one thing wrong with their happy valley. The woods surrounding them are filled with monsters.

A truce of sorts has been reached between the Pennsylvanians and the creatures known only by the Harry Potterish phrase: "Those Who We Do Not Speak Of." If the humans stay in their valley, the creatures stay in their woods. Anything red is also banned from the village as it tends to attract the creatures. Yellow is a safer and therefore preferable color.

This arrangement does place hard restrictions upon the villagers. The film opens after the death of a small child has taken place. Medicine to help cure his illness may have been available outside of the tiny village in the neighboring towns but no one dares cross the woods for fear of angering the fearsome beings who surround them.

A young man named Lucius (Joaquin Phoenix) appeals to the village elders to let him try to make the journey. His requests are repeatedly turned down. But when something happens that puts another villager in a life or death situation, it becomes clear that someone is going to have to try to leave the village for help.

Shyamalan has been called a modern day master of suspense. He has led us, the audience, to expect something unexpected. Waiting for the plot twist to be revealed has become part of the Shyamalan experience. In The Village, the twist is a major disappointment and the film would have been better served without it. More time spent on the villagers and how they feel about their plight and less time focused upon the mystery of the creatures would have made this a compelling and interesting film.

A very accomplished cast lends their talents to the project but there isn't much required of them. Joaquin Phoenix does a nice job as a romantically shy young man who seems to be the bravest of the villagers. Bryce Dallas Howard's (Ron Howard's daughter) work is the best thing about the film. She plays an attractive, blind villager to whom Lucius is drawn.

William Hurt, Sigourney Weaver, Brendan Gleeson, Cherry Jones, and Judy Greer all play villagers with varying degrees of success - more the fault of a weak script that doesn't provide them with enough opportunities to explore their characters. Adrien Brody plays a version of the village idiot in the film's biggest miscalculation. It is a characterization that is too broad and comical to sustain the mood Shyamalan wishes to evoke.

The mood of course is one of fear. The Village is largely about the power of fear and its controlling aspects. It is fear which keeps the villagers locked within their isolated community and it is fear which, in some respects, brought them there in the first place. Fear is a strong motivator but it is not a good one. Decisions made out of fear are generally not sound or profitable. Above everything else, the presence of fear indicates the absence of love.

There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love. 1 John 4:18 (KJV)

To live in a perpetual state of fear is to live without love. A community founded on fear is not a place we'd want to visit much less live.

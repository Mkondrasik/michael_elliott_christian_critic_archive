Author-verified transcription

It is somehow fitting for the great-grandson of H.G. Wells to have a hand in directing the latest adaptation of the novelist's classic story, The Time Machine. Ironically, although Simon Wells (The Prince of Egypt) has the advantage of a century's worth of perspective, his version of the tale is far less insightful than the work of his great-grandpa. It's as if he were proving one of the story's lessons: the future does not always bring progress.

Alexander Hartdegen (Guy Pearce, Memento), is a 19th century professor of applied mechanics and engineering, distracted and exhilarated by the promise of scientific discovery that seems to be around every new corner. After a tragic accident claims the life of his fiancee (Sienna Guillory, Superstition), his exhilaration turns to obsession as he feverishly works to conquer the field of time travel in order to gain her back.

When his experiment both succeeds and fails, he leaps forward in time to get an answer to the question that has him stymied... "Why can't we change the past?" The future doesn't seem to be in much of a position to help him seeing as the world in the year 802701 is in a much more primitive state than it was during his time of 1898.

Befriended by Mara, (Irish singer Samantha Mumba), one of the few remaining humans who still speaks the "stone" language of English, Alex is just settling into the future when he learns of the existence of some ape-like subterranean creatures who make a habit of terrorizing his peaceful cliff dwelling friends and dragging the older ones down to their underworld for reasons best left unsaid.

Wells and director Gore Verbanski, (The Mexican), who took the reins of this project near the end of the shoot, straddle between two genres and ultimately satisfy fans of neither. Starting out as a sweet, quirky romantic comedy, the film abruptly shifts gears to become a nightmarish action/horror adventure.

Guy Pearce handles himself in the two genres equally well. He's got some nice moments early on playing a bumbling, absent-minded professor. Later, he shows that he's believable as an athletic action hero. He gives two fine portrayals that really belong in two different films.

Orlando Jones (Evolution) makes the most of his small comic relief role playing a 8000th century holographic database with an attitude.

All in all, purists who enjoyed the original Time Machine story for its examination of class distinctions and societal ills will be disappointed by the lack of a social conscience in this film. However, as a mindless action adventure, it is admittedly entertaining, though not without flaws, and much of the production design (Oliver Scholl, Independence Day) is both inventive and attractive.

Though rated PG-13, parents should note that the subterranean creatures called Morlocks are hideous in appearance and that much of the violence in the film may frighten younger viewers.

One thing the film does depict well (even going so far as to quote the scripture below) is the truth that no matter what man does to ruin this planet for himself, God designed it to endure.

One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever. Ecclesiastes 1:4 (KJV)

Watching with Alex as he hurtles through time, we see the changes to this world that are due to man's influence. Each time, as our efforts end disastrously, God's creation restores itself without man's help and life again flourishes. It's a visual reminder of Genesis 1 when God took the chaos of this world and restored it to order.

The Time Machine also allows us a small peek into God's unique chronological perspective. It is written, "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day." The worldly changes that appear so monumental to us are insignificant when compared to that which remains constant. God's truths will continue to stand despite what any generation tries to do to their world.

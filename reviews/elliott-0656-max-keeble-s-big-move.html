Author-verified transcription

The ability to take action without risk of consequence... it a situation that opens up a world of possibilities for the title character in Max Keeble's Big Move. Of course, such a thing doesn't really exist but let's not get ahead of the story.

It's the first day of school and Max Keeble (Alex D. Linz, Bounce) has vowed to enter the 7th grade with a new "phat" attitude, or a "phattitude," as he calls it. Never mind that he's saddled with a nerdy father (Robert Carradine, Revenge of the Nerds) who dresses up in strange costumes for his advertising pitches. Never mind that his best friends are a band geek (Zena Grey, Snow Day) and a freak (Josh Peck, Snow Day) who wears a bathrobe wherever he goes. This year, Max knows that he is going to be cool.

Alas, he is victimized from the start. Singled out for humiliation by the school bullies, it looks like Max may be headed for another year of uncool status. Suddenly, everything changes with one simple announcement from his dad: They're moving to Chicago.

After his initial disappointment, Max realizes that this is his chance to get his revenge on all those who have treated him poorly: The ruthless, self-centered principal (Larry Miller, The Princess Diaries); the evil ice cream man (Jamie Kennedy, Three Kings); the bully who beat him up (Noel Fisher, Freddy Got Fingered); and the other bully who extorts money from him (Orlando Brown, Major Payne)... no one is safe from the wrath of Max, because he knows that with his relocation to Chicago, they won't be able to do anything to him. He's got carte blanche (which is French for "stupid idea").

Not really... but maybe that's what it should mean because after Max successfully launches his retaliation plan, he learns that he's not moving after all. Consequences are now in his future... big time. And they don't look like they will be pleasant.

The Disney team has mounted a handsome production with a clever idea at its core. Geared for the pre-teenage set, Max Keeble's Big Move delivers laughs to young and old alike. The children will like the cafeteria food fight and embarrassing the principal. Parents will appreciate the gentle humor and the moral lessons that the characters learn.

Much of the film's charm comes from a very personable young performer, Alex D. Linz, who plays Max. Like a younger version of Jonathan Taylor Thomas, Linz has a hip, cool persona that plays well on screen. He is ably supported by a comedian who doesn't always get the recognition he deserves. Larry Miller's deadpan turn as the dictatorial principal is a hoot and a half.

Yes, the comedy is broad and exaggerated. The story line is silly and implausible. That is all part of the fun. Director Tim Hill (Muppets From Space) keeps the mood light and the action flowing. And it helps that young Max learns a thing or two from this experience.

Everything we do... every action we take... has an impact somewhere. There is such a thing called cause and effect. Whether we are there to witness it or not, we remain responsible for our actions and the impact made by them. Not only responsible, we will be held accountable... if not in this world, then the next. Carte blanche simply does not exist.

For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things done in his body, according to that he hath done, whether it be good or bad. 2 Corinthians 5:10 (KJV)

Taking aggressive action when he thought there was going to be no consequences was easy, even enjoyable for Max. Accepting the waiting consequences was much more difficult. But as the wise school janitor says, "Any kid can make a mess. It takes a man to clean it up." Coming to that understanding that may be the biggest "move" Max has ever made.

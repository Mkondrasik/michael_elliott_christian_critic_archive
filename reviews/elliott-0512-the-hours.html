Author-verified transcription

Some people seem to view time as an enemy. As if it is something to be endured until it can be endured no longer. It is not a particularly happy way to live but then, the characters in The Hours are anything but happy people.

The Hours traces the parallel stories of three women from three different generations by examining a single day of their lives. In so doing, the film provides its actors with the juiciest character flaws, emotional disorders, and dysfunctional relationships this side of a long running soap opera.

First we see Virginia Woolf (Nicole Kidman, Moulin Rouge) quietly going mad in the 1920's while writing Mrs. Dalloway, a novel in which the protagonist reflects upon her life as she plans a party.

Two decades later, we are introduced to Laura Brown (Julianne Moore, Far From Heaven), a housewife and mother suffering from major bouts of depression which is not helped by the fact that she has just begun reading Mrs. Dalloway. Her husband (John C. Reilly, Chicago) is blissfully unaware of her deep unhappiness.

The modern day character is Clarissa Vaughn (Meryl Streep, Adaptation) who is playfully called Mrs. Dalloway by Richard (Ed Harris, Pollock), her manipulative ex-lover who is dying from AIDS. Though Clarissa is deep into a ten year lesbian relationship, it is her past male lover who demands her attention and constant care.

As depressing as this story is, and as objectionable as its underlying message, there is no denying that it is comprised of some exceptional performances. The trio of actresses do stellar work. Nicole Kidman's performance extends far beyond the novelty of wearing a prosthetic nose. Perhaps by distorting her familiar features, she has been freed to explore a depth of character which we have not seen from her in previous roles. Whatever the reason, this is by far her best work.

Meryl Streep and Julianne Moore are similarly inspired to give complete and textured performances. But individual performances alone, no matter how brilliantly executed, do not always make for a satisfying experience.

The lives of these women are intertwined, forming a single narrative line even though their stories are separated by decades. By this, director Stephen Daldry (Billy Elliot) and screenwriter David Hare (Plenty), who has adapted Michael Cunningham's novel for the screen, seem to be expressing what they might consider to be the universal plight of women: Living a life that is fashioned or dictated by others.

The problem is that the actions taken by the characters (and apparently applauded by the filmmakers) are singularly selfish ones. Motivated by desires which exclude the consideration of others, it is difficult to understand their choices much less agree with them.

Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory; but in lowliness of mind let each esteem other better than themselves. Look not every man on his own things, but every man also on the things of others. Philippians 2:3-4 (KJV)

The most important aspect of love is one of giving. ("God so loved that He gave His only begotten son.") These women apparently grew tired of giving. They were so focused on what they thought were their own needs that they failed to appreciate the needs of others. One character makes the comment that the life she was living was "death" and so she opted instead for "life." She was wrong. What she chose was not to love.

Mental illness, depression, suicide, the ravages of a devastating disease, child abandonment, sexual confusion... the examination of such topics will almost certainly be rewarded by award groups (the film has already been a big winner at the Golden Globes), but that doesn't make them any easier to watch.

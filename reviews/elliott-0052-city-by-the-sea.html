City by the Sea

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
September 6, 2002
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Ezekiel 18:20Deuteronomy 24:162 Chronicles 25:4

Translation: NRSV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Free will; generational responsibility; parenting; choices

Author-verified transcription

If the new film, City by the Sea, is accurate, time has not been kind to Long Beach, NY. In the fifties it was a lovely place to visit or live, but it has since deteriorated to become a graffiti scarred home to crack addicts and the human vermin who supply them. Once full of hope and promise, it now exists as an empty shell of what might have been. In this respect, it has a lot in common with the characters within the film.

Vincent LaMarca (Robert De Niro, Showtime) is one such empty shell. His father, a convicted child killer, was executed when Vincent was just eight years old. He learned to deal with the stares and whispers at an early age by simply disengaging from people. He eventually grew to become a respected member of NYPD’s Homicide Division.

It’s just that his interpersonal skills need a lot of work. He is estranged from his bitter ex-wife (Patti LuPone) and troubled son (James Franco, Spider-Man). His current girlfriend (Frances McDormand, Fargo) knows next to nothing about him. He won’t even visit his partner (George Dzundza, Instinct) at home because seeing a happy family is too painful for him.

A murder investigation brings him back to his Long Beach roots where he learns that the primary suspect is his drug-dependent son. This forces him to reflect upon the various choices the male members of the LaMarca family have made, especially his.

Robert De Niro takes a low-key, naturalistic approach to his role of Vincent. Except for an emotionally overblown climax which doesn’t quite ring true, he gives a balanced introspective performance. He does his best work when paired in scenes with Frances McDormand (Fargo) who is quite remarkable in her small but important role.

James Franco, as Joey LaMarca, gives a standout portrayal of a young man who knows he’s taken a wrong path in life but has no clue how to find his way back. It is a role that could have easily been overplayed but Franco manages to keep a lid on the desperation and emotional pain his character is experiencing.

This film is inspired by Mark of a Murderer, a 1977 Esquire Magazine article by Pulitzer Prize winner Mike McAlary. In it, McAlary told the true story of Vincent LaMarca, whose family tree did indeed include a branch where kidnapper/killer begat a cop who in turn begat a killer. Using that framework, the film then veers into fictional territory, taking great liberties with historical facts. Many of the changes made by the filmmakers only serve to trivialize the larger sociological questions which might have otherwise been raised or emphasized.

There was a question that was a strong element of the article and was also touched upon in the film. Namely, is there such a thing as a “killing gene”? Is it possible to pass a predisposition for violence down from one generation to another? The scriptures provide us with the answer:

“The person who sins shall die. A child shall not suffer for the iniquity of a parent, nor a parent suffer for the iniquity of a child; the righteousness of the righteous shall be his own, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be his own.” Ezekiel 18:20 (NRSV)

We each have the free will ability to choose to do good or to do evil. The choices made by our forefathers are not genetically passed down to us. There is no such thing as a “killing gene.”

It should be said, however, that a father provides more than genetic material. He also provides things that can actually have an impact on a child’s free will behavior. Things such as instruction, reproof, guidance, example, and love. Such things are impossible to be transmitted at conception. It takes involving one’s self in the lives of one’s children. One consequence of the choices made by the LaMarca men was to remove them from participating in the raising of their children. This had as much to do with the kind of men their boys became as any molecular substance that may have been contained in their DNA.

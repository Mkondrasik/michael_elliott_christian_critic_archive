Author-verified transcription

I must have rocks in my head. After watching the live action feature, THE FLINTSTONES IN VIVA ROCK VEGAS, I actually walked away with a mild appreciation for it. True, it was nothing more than lightweight silliness... But it was so unabashedly committed to its own silliness that I was begrudgingly won over.

The odds against it receiving a positive rating from me were stacked high. After all, it is a "prequel" to the universally panned 1994 film which, in turn, was based on the Hanna Barbera cartoon series of the 1960s. It shares the same director, Brian Levant, while lacking the high profile stars of the original. Nevertheless, thanks to the likable portrayals of America's favorite Neanderthals and the unapologetic approach of Mr. Levant, this Flintstones' film rises from the slag heap which buried its predecessor.

Fred Flintstone (Mark Addy, The Full Monty) and his pal, Barney Rubble (Stephen Baldwin, Friends & Lovers) could be called "blue-collar bachelors," if not for the fact that collars had not yet been invented.

One day, at a drive-in restaurant, they catch the eye of a pair of bronto-burger beauties, Betty (Jane Krakowski, Ally McBeal) and Wilma (Kristen Johnston, 3rd Rock From the Sun.) Before Fred can stammer, "Hummana, hummana, hummana...," a date has been arranged and the rest, as they say, is prehistory.

Of course, Wilma's family is aghast, especially her mother (Joan Collins, Dynasty.) After all, the Slaghooples are upper-crust and want nothing to do with these common, quarry-working cavemen. They would much prefer to see Wilma marry Chip Rockefeller (Thomas Gibson, Eyes Wide Shut), the good-looking and charming casino owner who desires her as a means to get his hands on the Slaghoople fortune.

Watching the proceedings with a large helping of sarcastic glee is the space alien, Gazoo (Alan Cumming, Titus) who has been banished to Earth to observe this species' mating rituals. With the pair of "dum-dums" he's selected, his research may not be completed until after the ice age.

There is a contrast in playing styles among the leads. Mr. Addy plays Fred more like an early version of Ralph Kramden (from The Honeymooners) who served as the original inspiration of the character. Mr. Baldwin plays Barney far more broadly, basing his characterization from the cartoon source. His "heh, heh" laugh is amazingly accurate, as is Ms. Krakowski's Betty-like giggle. Listening to these two communicated sans dialogue is one of the film's bright spots.

Alan Cumming appears to be having a ball in two different roles: Not only does he play the digitally enhanced diminutive green Gazoo, he struts around the screen as the prancing rock star Mick Jagged, even singing a rendition of the title song, Viva Rock Vegas. Purists need not panic.. Ann Margaret's version is also heard during the course of the film.

Brian Levant and team have designed a cartoon world in which to house this silliness, complete with dinosaur bridges, octopi masseuses, bird-powered remote controls, and other prehistoric versions of 20th century conveniences. Such cleverness was a staple of the TV show and Mr. Levant borrows heavily from it for this production. The films includes more sexual innuendo than appropriate but such indulgences are brief and understated.

At the center of all this foolishness is a simple love story. Can a common, hardworking man with limited financial resources win the heart of an attractive woman whose family is rolling in clams? The answer is a resounding "why not?" Money has never been (and never will be) the key to happiness. And it has never been known to bring peace or contentment. On the contrary, it has been known to bring unhappiness, discontent, and many sleepless nights.

The sleep of a labouring man is sweet, whether he eat little or much: but the abundance of the rich will not suffer him to sleep. Ecclesiastes 5:12 (KJV)

The Winslow Boy

Author-verified transcription

“It’s easy to do justice... It’s hard to do right.” These insightful words are spoken by Sir Robert Morton in David Mamet’s brilliant rendition of the Terence Rattigan play, The Winslow Boy.

Both the film and the play are based upon an actual incident which gripped an entire country during the early 1900’s. In the film, a young 13 year old cadet named Ronnie Winslow (Guy Edwards, A Pride of Lions), attending the Osbourne Naval College of England, was expelled from that institution after the college accused him of stealing another student’s five shilling postal note, forging his signature and cashing it.

Upon hearing the news and his son’s claim of innocence, the father of the lad (Nigel Hawthorne, Tarzan) decides to fight against the injustice and for the honor of the boy and the family name. It is a fight that is not without sacrifice. The college happens to be under the protective umbrella of the Admiralty which in turn is part of the King’s domain. As such, regular procedures for legal action could not be followed as the King, being infallible, was immune to all legal claims of wrongdoing.

After exhausting every avenue and much of the family wealth, the father turns to the noted barrister, Sir Robert Morton (Jeremy Northam, An Ideal Husband) to take up the case. As the proceedings drag on, it takes more and more of a toll upon all concerned. Catherine Winslow (Rebecca Pidgeon, The Spanish Prisoner) postpones her engagement, perhaps indefinitely, to stalwart John Watherstone (Aden Gillett, The Borrowers) in order to pursue family vindication. Eldest son Dickie (Matthew Pidgeon in his film debut) is asked to voluntarily leave Oxford University so more funds may be funneled to the defense of young Ronnie.

Eventually, all parties must ask themselves (and each other) “Is it worth it?” At what point is the sacrifice too great to continue the fight being waged?

Nigel Hawthorne is every inch the proper English patriarch as the head of the Winslow clan. He delivers Mr. Mamet’s lines with the understated eloquence they deserve.

Jeremy Northam cuts an impressive figure as the best barrister that 1910 England has to offer. Strong, quick-minded, passionately impersonal, he has a commanding presence whose every minute of screen time demands our attention.

Rebecca Pidgeon (who is married to David Mamet) portrays Catherine as a willful and resourceful young woman able to operate in a Victorian world even while actively trying to modernize that world. Her being a suffragette is a playwright’s addition, enabling him to show her penchant for “lost causes” and adding a little spice to the implied romantic stirrings between Catherine and the conservatively conventional Sir Robert.

David Mamet (The Spanish Prisoner), as a screenwriter, is well known for his skill in writing dialogue. Here, uncharacteristically using G-rated language, he captures a time and place where virtue, honor, and propriety had meaning. I must admit it is unusual to find a G-rated film that is not a) animated; b) a remake of a children’s book; or c) filled with cute talking animals. The Winslow Boy, containing no profanity, nudity, sexual innuendo, profane humor, or perversity of any kind, is indeed for general audiences, but more than that... it will insult nobody’s intelligence. Bravo, Mr. Mamet.

The story itself is reminiscent of a parable related by Jesus Christ and recorded in Luke 18. A woman was wronged and her transgressor was going unpunished. She besought the intervention of an unjust judge but could not get him to pay heed to her pleas until, he conceded, she would wear him down by her continual coming. It was only her persistence which moved him to take action. The point of Jesus’ parable was to teach us that we must not faint in our efforts to receive that which we seek.

The rallying cry of the Winslow case was “Let right be done!” As Sir Robert pointed out, justice is relatively easy. It is simply the adherence to the law of the land. Doing what is right oftentimes requires much more, and is often only achieved at a great cost, as the characters in this film discover.

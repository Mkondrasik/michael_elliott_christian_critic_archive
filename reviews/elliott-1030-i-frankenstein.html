Author-verified transcription

In I, Frankenstein, an unlikely hero joins the eternal battle between good and evil. Frankenstein’s monster, doomed by his creator to wander the earth in a solitary existence, finds himself to be the key to both mankind’s survival and its total annihilation.

It has been 200 years since the monster (Aaron Eckhart) buried the grieving doctor who had foolishly pursued his creation into the frozen tundra following the murder of his wife. Since then the world has changed greatly. The one thing that remains unchanged is the unending struggle that is invisible to most humans – the one between demons and angels.

The demons, led by Prince Naberius (Bill Nighy), have concocted a plan to build a demon army using the soulless corpses they have been collecting for centuries. All they need is a way to re-animate their lifeless flesh. That is why Frankenstein’s monster and the secret to his scientifically created life are important to them. The angels, who have taken on the form of gargoyles to watch over and protect the human race, are not about to let that happen.

While the stage within this framework could have been easily set for an exploration of the definition of life and soul; the nature of evil; or the spiritual reality of the unseen world, the filmmakers had no such aspirations. Instead we are given a high gloss, comic book treatment of the action-oriented fantasy, which comes as little surprise as it was inspired by the graphic novel by Kevin Grevioux. There may not be a lot of depth or substance but style and special effects are in abundance.

Thankfully, the filmmakers opted for a PG-13 treatment, substituting pyrotechnics for gore. Whenever the monster, nicknamed “Adam” by Lenore (Miranda Otto), head of the Order of the Gargoyles, dispatches the dozens of demons he fights, they “descend” into the pits of hell in a colorful display of fire and lights. Conversely, when a gargoyle loses a battle, they “ascend” to the heavens awash in a brilliant white light.

One of the crucial plot lines or premises within the story is the belief that demons can only possess bodies that have no soul. The implication is that our soul is what protects us from the unseen spiritual evil that surrounds us. Such is not the case. Our soul is simply our life, our breath. It has no inherent spiritual power. All living creatures have a soul. It begins when we take our first breath and leaves us when we breathe our last.

“That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit.” John 3:6 (KJV)

There is a difference between soul and spirit. While our soul is finite and corruptible, our spirit is neither. As we learn the nuances of these differences and continue to exercise our spiritual nature and abilities as God intends for us, we grow stronger and can more ably defend ourselves and guard ourselves from the devilish influences that continue to tempt and attack us to this day.

“The seventy-two returned with joy and said, “Lord, even the demons submit to us in your name.” Luke 10:17 (NIV)

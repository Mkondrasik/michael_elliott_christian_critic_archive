King Arthur

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 7, 2004
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: False teachers; deception; discernment; God’s Word; truth

Author-verified transcription

All legends have to begin somewhere. Producer Jerry Bruckheimer (Pirates of the Caribbean), director Antoine Fuqua (Training Day), and screenwriter David Franzoni (Gladiator) have taken the familiar legend of King Arthur and have deconstructed it to give the story historical roots.

Set in 452 AD, their film tells the tale of Lucius Artorius Castus (Clive Owen) – a commander assigned to protect the Roman occupied territory in Britain. Serving under him are the offspring of a band of Sarmatian cavalry soldiers who, in exchange for peaceful co-existence for their people, have agreed to give their allegiance to the occupying Roman army.

As their fifteen years of indentured servitude draws to a close, only a handful of these soldiers remain. Before Lancelot (Ioan Gruffudd), Tristan (Mads Mikkelsen), Bors (Ray Winstone), Gawain (Joel Edgerton), Galahad (Hugh Dancy), and Dagonet (Ray Stevenson) are permitted to return to their homes, they are given one last mission.

The Roman Empire has already begun to crumble. Before they abandon Britain to the quickly encroaching Saxon army, Lucius Artorius (known simply as Arthur) and his remaining knights must travel behind the Saxon lines to rescue an important Roman family. It won’t be an easy task. Not only is the Saxon army ruthless and greater in numbers and strength, Arthur and his knights must also face the native Woads, led by the mysterious Merlin (Stephen Dillane), who ferociously resist all foreign occupation.

Franzoni is very clever in how he takes the elements of the legend and inserts it into this story. The origins of Excalibur and the round table are introduced. The ideals represented in Camelot are shown to be first represented in Arthur’s erroneous image of a Roman utopia.

Clive Owen has a commanding presence as Arthur. His character is driven by an overwhelming sense of morality and righteousness and he conveys strong charismatic leadership qualities that make it easy to believe that men would risk their lives for him.

Keira Knightley portrays Guinevere, who in this film is a Woad warrior, rescued by Arthur and joins him in fighting against their common enemy, the Saxons. The portrayal of her character is problematic and does not initially seem to fit with the overall tone of the picture. But as the story progresses, we come to accept the character and the relationship that forms between her and Arthur.

The rest of the cast form a fine ensemble. The knights enjoy a believable camaraderie and the villains are given well developed back stories which help to add depth and layers to what is essentially a formulaic picture. Think of it as a cross between Braveheart and The Magnificent Seven.

Another theme which I found fascinating to consider is how the religious characters were depicted in the film. Set a mere 400 years since Jesus walked the earth, it becomes clear that Christianity had already been polluted and corrupted from within. The majority of Christians in the film perform despicably evil acts only to hide behind the name of God. It is Arthur, who has been isolated from the corruption, who maintains a pure faith in a God of love and mercy.

Scriptures warn us of putting our trust in men who pervert the truths of God’s Word for their own purposes. It is why God gave us the written Word. So we might have a standard of truth against which we can hold all things.

For I know this, that after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them. Acts 20:29-30 (KJV)

By adhering to God’s Word as our sole source of truth, we may avoid the deceptions and lies of those who profess a righteousness they do not practice.

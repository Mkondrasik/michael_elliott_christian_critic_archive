Author-verified transcription

There are some films that bowl us over with their special effects. Others which thrill us with the larger than life heroics of their characters. In The Bedroom takes a different approach. It floors us through its simple honesty, intimately showing us a set of characters who are living through a time of emotional devastation.

Frank Fowler (Nick Stahl, The Man Without A Face) is a good kid. He’s mature, responsible, and has a solid future ahead of him. His parents Matt (Tom Wilkinson, Black Knight) and Ruth (Sissy Spacek, The Straight Story) have every reason to be proud. They’re not especially pleased that he’s chosen to have a summer fling with Natalie (Marisa Tomei, Someone Like You), an older woman with two young children, but outside of some mild admonitions for him to focus more on his college preparation, they try not to overreact to the situation.

Natalie’s estranged husband (William Mapother, Swordfish), is still in the picture however and his proclivity towards violence pretty much ensures that this summer fling will end up having tragic consequences.

Exactly how the characters deal with those consequences and the effect it has on their respective relationships lies at the heart of this film. Actor turned director Todd Field makes an impressive debut behind the camera, eliciting strong performances from each member of his cast, many of whom are deservedly drawing critical acclaim and award nominations for their efforts.

Tom Wilkinson is a strong but quiet presence who is struggling to keep an even keel in the wake of an unbearable tragedy. As his wife, Sissy Spacek vividly communicates immeasurable emotional depth simply by her judgmental looks and stony silences.

Marisa Tomei, in her finest role since My Cousin Vinny, gives a brave and emotionally raw portrayal which rivals the best of any I’ve seen this year. Nick Stahl embodies the youthful exuberance and naiveté to be found in someone who is in the middle of bridging the gap between childhood and manhood.

In The Bedroom examines grief that occurs at such a painful level that it is difficult to even imagine it unless one has experienced it for one’s self. What is so remarkable is that it does so without pandering to the audience or stooping to cheap exploitation. Field even has many of the harsh or grievous events take place off screen, leaving us to focus entirely upon the reactions of those who remain instead of the events themselves. Much of the time, those reactions are so internalized by the actors that the on screen tension is palpable. When the emotional eruptions do occur, it feels real, raw, and heartbreakingly understandable.

There are those who complain that the end of the film doesn’t maintain the delicate balance that Field has achieved throughout its previous 100 minutes and that he allows the film to slip into an unbelievable and melodramatic final act unworthy of all that preceded it. While I can see that point of view, I believe that, in the final moments of the film, the director is not condoning the actions of his characters but showing us that such actions will not assuage the grief that they feel.

Certainly, the film makes a strong statement regarding the limitations of our legal system. There is a certain amount of truth in the statement, “Justice delayed is justice denied.”

Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil. Ecclesiastes 8:11 (KJV)

But make no mistake, man’s justice alone will never eradicate grief. Only God’s justice will bring true peace. This is a hope that we must cling to even in the midst of extreme sorrow. Most especially then.

And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. Revelation 21:4 (KJV)

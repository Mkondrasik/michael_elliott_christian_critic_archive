Henry Ford knew that he couldn't simply make the same product forever if he was to expect people to keep buying it year after year. It may be time for Harrison Ford to learn the same lesson. After all, he's been remaking the same movie for almost twenty years.

This time, in Firewall, the 63-year old actor plays Jack Stanfield, a computer security expert whose family is being held hostage and will die unless he can come up with a way to hack into the flawlessly designed system he designed for his bank's protection.

Because these high-tech crooks, led by the sinister Paul Bettany, are able to watch his every move via surveillance cameras, Jack can neither call on nor rely on help to come unbidden and so he must worm his way out of a seemingly impossible situation against seemingly overwhelming forces. And if that weren't enough, Jack's bank is in the middle of a merger and the new boss doesn't much like him. I guess to some the threat of unemployment is worse than death.

Joe Forte's screenplay is strictly by-the- numbers (even though those numbers don't always add up), and he's blessed to have Ford on board. He's so familiar with this type of material he makes it look good - or at least tolerable, which, for a February thriller, is all that we ask.

Director Richard Loncraine (Wimbledon) relies a bit too heavily on using "gimmicky" camera shots (i.e. from surveillance cameras, mirrored images, etc) to give the feel of an intelligent thriller but there's not enough smarts in the script to pull it off. Still he cranks up the tension when needed and skirts past the incredulous with a directorial straight face.

It might have been more plausible to keep the conflict as a battle of wits between an inventive, well-seasoned problem solver and a worthy but unmerciful opponent but Forte follows this formula to the letter by having Ford's character jump through the physical hoops designed for a much younger actor. Ford is game for it and it works to a degree but one can't help wondering how much Ben-Gay the poor man went through when the cameras stopped rolling.

Virginia Madsen is a bit underused as the bound and trussed wife/mother who between screams must keep up a brave front for the children. Paul Bettany makes for a decent villain although he too is held back by the limitations written into the script. The actors who play his henchmen all "hench" quite well and each have their moments in the brief spotlight.

Bettany's character continued to give assurances to Jack that once he did what they wanted, he and his family would be released to resume their lives. Jack didn't believe him and for good reason. Lies come easily to those who intend to do evil. They are not to be trusted as is illustrated in the Scriptures by observing the manipulations of the father of lies - the devil.

Ye are of your father the devil, and the lusts of your father ye will do. He was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it. John 8:44 (KJV)

The "assurances" he gave to Eve in Genesis 3 ("ye shall not surely die;" "ye shall be as gods") were bold and blatant lies that, because they were believed, led directly to man's fall. Trusting in a lie is a surefire way to destruction and misery.

Buffalo Soldiers

Original publication date
July 25, 2003
Rating
2 stars

1 hr: 38 min

Author-verified transcription

Some films are blessed with timing. Buffalo Soldiers is not. Its darkly satirical look at life on a US military base at time of peace certainly makes us feel like we're watching the wrong film at the wrong time.

It is set in Germany near the end of the Cold War. The American soldiers stationed there apparently have no sense of a mission or of duty. They barely have a sense of morality. In a world where our military men and women are placing themselves at risk daily, such a film sends a demeaning and unconscionable message.

Welcome, we are told, to the armed forces of 1989. The draft has been abolished and the American volunteer army is now comprised of miscreants and criminals who entered the service because it was the lesser of two evils. Jail was the only other option.

Among the miscreants is one Roy Elwood (Joaquin Phoenix), a bored, disillusioned but resourceful company clerk, who uses his position to acquire items that might have value in the thriving black market. Think of him as a Radar O'Reilly who has somehow gone horribly bad. When an assortment of tactical weapons falls into his lap, Roy doesn't hesitate to capitalize on this unexpected bounty. He trades them to a foreign dealer in exchange for a warehouse full of heroin.

His actions go unnoticed by his ineffectual CO, Colonel Berman (Ed Harris), who is too busy worrying about his own lackluster military career. But the new, hard-nosed, top sergeant (Scott Glenn), who spent three tours in Vietnam, is all too familiar with operators like Elwood and begins to make life difficult for the black marketeer. Elwood makes the mistake of falling for the sergeant's daughter (Anna Paquin) which only serves to inflame her father's animosity towards him.

The actors perform their respective roles as well as could be expected. There's nothing the matter with the talent behind the film. It is the message the film communicates that feels so wrong. Buffalo Soldiers is reminiscent of M.A.S.H. and Catch-22 in its irreverent humor, but it has the misfortune of being distributed in a post-9/11 world where, for most people, a man in uniform is viewed with more appreciation than he perhaps was a few decades earlier. To append Bob Dylan's familiar lyric, "Times, they are a changin' back."

It is an issue to which Miramax is certainly sensitive. They acquired the rights to the film after it appeared at the Toronto Film Festival on September 7, 2001. A few days later, the world was turned upside down. More accurately, it was our perception that was irrevocably altered. Studio executives wisely kept the film on the shelf for the next two years as they waited for the political climate to shift. In my opinion, they didn't wait long enough.

The men and women of our armed forces are asked to willingly live by a code of honor and discipline. They are called to display valor, courage, and endurance, being separated from their families while being under the ever present threat of enemy attack.

The life of a soldier is a life of service and sacrifice. The Bible uses it as an analogy for how we are to view our walk with Christ.

Thou therefore endure hardness, as a good soldier of Jesus Christ. No man that warreth entangleth himself with the affairs of this life; that he may please him who hath chosen him to be a soldier. 2 Timothy 2:3-4 (KJV)

For all its posturing, Buffalo Soldiers serves no purpose. It has no political statements to make, no insightful observations upon which to expound. It is a throwback to a more self-indulgent time that I, for one, have no interest in revisiting.

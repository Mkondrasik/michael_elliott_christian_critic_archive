Racing Stripes

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 14, 2005
Rating
2½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 34 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Believing; Negativity; Encouragement

Author-verified transcription

A zebra who thinks he's a race horse? Though it may sound like an unusual concept for a film, Racing Stripes is simply another story about an underdog battling against seemingly insurmountable odds to emerge a champion in his chosen field. We've seen it before in many different guises but, thankfully, the formula still works... to a degree.

Stripes (voiced by Frankie Muniz) is a zebra who, through an unfortunate weather related mishap, finds himself being raised by a Kentuckian family. Surrounded by thoroughbreds being groomed for racing success, Stripes naturally assumes that he too is a race horse and will settle for nothing less than an opportunity to prove himself on the track.

Unfortunately for Stripes, no one of any importance believes that a zebra has a place among the highly trained and well-bred horses which make up the field of the highly regarded Kentucky Open.

No matter. In this flimsily constructed fable, Stripes will have no trouble finding a spot among the purebreds. What happens next will be entirely up to him and the heart he demonstrates to have.

Hayden Panettiere plays Channing, a young girl who bonds with the zebra and believes that he has the makings of a champion. Bruce Greenwood is her father, an ex-horse trainer who walked away from the business after his wife died in a riding accident. Still mourning the loss, he strictly forbids Channing from mounting any horse... even a striped one.

Where I believe director Frederik Du Chau errs is in his attempt to "Babe-ify" the story by giving voice to the ranch animals. None of the creatures have much of a distinctive personality and the ones that do are not that appealing.

Frankie Muniz provides the voice for the horizontally-lined animal and Mandy Moore purrs her way as a white filly being trained as a jumper. Dustin Hoffman and Whoopie Goldberg don't add too much to the proceedings in their respective roles as a miniature horse (and unheralded brains behind the trainer's success) and an old goat.

Far too much screen time is given to a pair of flies, Buzz and Scuzz (played by David Spade and Steve Harvey). They're almost as annoying as real flies can be. Joe Pantoliano plays a pelican named Goose who is running from the mob. A "hit bird," his only ammunition is the stuff that, judging by the state of my windshield, birds have in ample supply.

The film is most successful when all this animal nonsense is forgotten and the story settles upon the relationship between Channing and her father. Had Du Chau trusted his human actors to carry the film, it would have been far more moving and effective. As it is, it's a slight and innocuous family film that is tolerable but only just.

An object lesson that we can take from the film is the level of negativity that surrounds Stripes. He may have had the heart of a champion but had he allowed himself to believe all the "neigh-sayers" around him, he never would have left the starting gate... let alone crossed the finish line.

Our believing has a direct impact on what we receive in this life. It is one of God's immutable laws that even Jesus Christ faced in his time.

And he [Jesus] did not [do] many mighty works there because of their unbelief. Matthew 13:58 (KJV)

In order for Stripes to realize his dream, he had to ignore the derision from those who did not believe and not allow it to affect his own believing. He did this by turning from the negativity and surrounding himself with those were encouraging him to succeed.

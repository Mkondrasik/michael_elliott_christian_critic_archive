Bringing Down the House

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 7, 2003
Rating
1½ stars
Running time
1 hr: 45 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

1 Corinthians 7:33-34a1 Corinthians 7:3Ecclesiastes 3:1-9

Translation: NIV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Balance; marriage; work and family; priorities; stewardship of time

Author-verified transcription

Bringing down the House commits the worst possible sin for a comedy. It's not that funny. Even when it goes for the easy laugh, more times than not it fails to hit the mark. Uptight middle-aged white men trying to act like they're from the 'hood? Old women smoking pot and getting stoned? The film contains nothing but recycled gags which are worn so thin that the audience can see right through them. Bringing down the House is not going to be bringing down any house... at least not with laughter.

Steve Martin (Novocaine) stars as Peter Sanderson, a divorced, kind of square tax attorney who has not quite gotten over his ex-wife (Jean Smart, Sweet Home Alabama). At least he's advanced to the point where he can start a casual flirtation with another lawyer via an Internet chat room. When he finally lands a face-to-face date with her, she turns out to be a "sista" with plenty of attitude. Charlene (Queen Latifah, Chicago) is an escaped con who has turned to Peter for help in proving her innocence.

Charlene's broadly played ethnicity causes a problem for Peter who lives in a white Wonder bread kind of world. After she refuses to leave him alone, he strikes a deal with her. He agrees to help her with her case if she will pretend to be a nanny to his kids. It is the only explanation that seems to satisfy his racist neighbors and prejudiced clients who are questioning her presence in his life.

Of course, we immediately see where all this is leading. Charlene is going to shake up the ordered world of the straight-laced lawyer but at the same time she will bring some color into his monochromatic life, thus making him a better person.

The best thing about the film is the supporting cast. There are certain actors who always seem able to rise above weak material and two of them are in this film. Jean Smart is apparently incapable of striking a false note. At least I've never caught her in one. And nobody plays droll better than Eugene Levy (Best in Show). Here, he's a legal associate to Peter who becomes sexually smitten with Charlene.

Unfortunately the weight of the film is on the leads and they simply aren't able to make this tarnished rock of a movie shine like a gem. Steve Martin tries hard enough by returning to a physical brand of comedy for which he was once well known. But it is too broad and ultimately pointless. His characterization doesn't support the "wild and crazy" antics of his younger days. Queen Latifah is certainly photogenic and she has personality to spare. It's the material she's been given that is holding her back here. But then, as one of the film's executive producers she only has herself to blame.

Buried underneath all the racial lampoons and sexual jokes is a message that is worthy of unearthing. Peter's divorce was caused by his workaholic nature. By investing more time into his business life than he gave to his home life, he lost what he once cherished the most. We must learn to shape our lives so they are properly balanced. Otherwise, we send them veering off into directions we don't really want them to go. This balance is something to which the apostle Paul referred in his letter to the Corinthians.

But a married man is concerned about the affairs of this world - how he can please his wife -- and his interests are divided. 1 Corinthians 7:33-34a (NIV)

Time continues to be our most precious commodity and how well we manage or spend the time we have will dictate how successful we will be in meeting the various demands that are placed upon it.

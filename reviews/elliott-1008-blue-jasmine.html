Author-verified transcription

Blue Jasmine is a Woody Allen film without having a Woody Allen or a Woody Allen counterpart in it. Instead, the luminous Cate Blanchett takes center stage and holds it throughout the running time of the film. And you’ll find no complaint from this corner. The film is in capable hands.

Jasmine (Cate Blanchett) is a successful New York socialite who came from humble beginnings and has little choice but to return to them. Her entrepreneurial-minded husband (Alec Baldwin) has been tried and convicted of fraud; the government has seized all assets; and she has absolutely no idea how to live within a budget that starts and ends with zero dollars.

Arriving on her sister Ginger’s San Francisco doorstep, fresh from a nervous breakdown and loaded with the pharmaceuticals she believes help her to get through the day, she finds herself needing to depend upon the kindness of family (whom she had always treated as strangers.) Ginger (Sally Hawkins) willingly takes her in and thus begins Jasmine’s attempts and failures to rebuild her life.

Allen plays with the timeline, jumping back and forth chronologically to show us Jasmine’s persona on both sides of the success line. It is, at times, difficult to follow but as the film progresses, the film’s structure becomes clearer to us and easier to track.

Blanchett does some nicely nuanced work as a woman on the verge of a complete mental meltdown. Hawkins, as her lower to middle class sister, provides some color and shading of her own. But, to be honest, I preferred many of the male actors whose work have not received as much notice as the female stars. Baldwin, as the lying cheat of a husband, is very believable without overplaying the bit; Bobby Cannavale, as a working class stiff, shows an energy and powerful commitment to his choices; Andrew Dice Clay as Ginger’s ex-husband with a justifiable axe to grind towards Jasmine and her husband, proves to be a surprisingly effective casting choice, totally nailing his character. Peter Sarsgaard, as a possible suitor to Jasmine (and her potential ticket out of the poverty hell she has descended into), evokes a relaxed charm and naturalness.

While there is much that could be said regarding Jasmine’s plight and how she handles her situation, we shouldn’t ignore what caused her plight in the first place. Whenever we put “making money” or “trusting in money” high on our priority list, we run the risk of causing our own eventual fall or disgrace.

“Those who want to get rich fall into temptation and a trap and into many foolish and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction.” 1 Timothy 6:9 (NIV)

Notice that the scriptures do not state that the rich fall into temptation and a trap… only those who WANT to get rich. There is nothing sinful about being wealthy as long as we are able to keep our perspective right. Having a motivation to become wealthy can lead people down paths that take them further from God’s light and love. Wealth can and should be a by-product of living righteously.

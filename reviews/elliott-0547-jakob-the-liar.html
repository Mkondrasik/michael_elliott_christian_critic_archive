Author-verified transcription

Hope is a strong yet delicate thing. Strong because it enables us to endure incredible sorrow and pain. Delicate because it can crumble under the slightest pressure if it is not based upon truth. The newly released Jakob the Liar ultimately fails because it does not recognize the fallacy of its own premise.

Based upon the novel by Jurek Becker, the film stars Robin Williams (Patch Adams) as Jakob Heym, a Polish Jew once known for his latkes (potato pancakes). However, in the German patrolled ghettos of 1945 Poland, potatoes are a rare commodity... as is hope. Suicides, on the other hand, are everyday events.

Starved for any news from the outside world, Jakob chases a sheet of newspaper caught in the swirling wind. His pursuit lands him in trouble from the guards who direct him to the commandant's office. There, he overhears a German news report of a battle with Russian forces some 400 kilometers away.

When he relays this information to his fellow Jews, they get the impression that Jakob has an illegal radio, an offense punishable by death. His insistent denials only serve to convince them further and they continually come to him for more news of the war's progress.

Jakob reluctantly becomes a heroic figure in the eyes of the Jewish community. Seeing the impact his news reports have in restoring hope to his community, Jakob begins inventing news reports to feed his people. Suicides immediately disappear as those in the ghetto begin to believe the end of the war is imminent.

Mr. Williams is restrained, apparently from carrying the full weight of the film and its subject matter on his back. The humor in this film is dark and self-effacing which does not allow him to engage in his special brand of improvisational lunacy. In his opening narration, after telling a joke about Hitler, Jakob explains that humor is how the Jews were able to survive the terrible events they faced. Ironically, five minutes after that statement, Jakob happens across the victims of the newest suicides to occur in the ghetto. It would seem that "gallows humor" does have its limitations.

Hannah Taylor-Gordon (The Reef) plays a precocious ten year old Jewish girl who falls under the protective care of Jakob after she is rescued from a train headed for the concentration camps. Liev Schreiber (Scream) is a buffoon of an ex-boxer who becomes instrumental in spreading the rumor about Jakob's radio.

Hungarian born writer/director Peter Kassovitz (Bonjour Tristesse) and his production team do an admirable job in depicting the deplorable conditions of the ghetto life in war-ravished Poland. The story is a depressing one and the setting matches it perfectly.

The similarities between Jakob the Liar and the brilliant Life is Beautiful are both obvious and unfortunate. The former suffers greatly by comparison. Life is Beautiful demonstrated the indomitable spirit of a man motivated by love which was able to elevate him above his surroundings. Jakob the Liar never rises above the oppression and dreariness of wartime Poland.

Perhaps it is because the hope planted by Jakob was false, one which was motivated by fear and desperation. The scriptures tell us that the hope we carry in our hearts should serve as "an anchor to our souls." This is true only because the hope of a believer is based upon a promise made by the God Who cannot lie.

False promises or false hopes lead to false expectations, which result in disappointment and despair.

How then comfort ye me in vain, seeing in your answers there remaineth falsehood? Job 21:34 [KJV]

In contrast, hope that is based upon the truth of God cannot disappoint. It will continue to be that strong anchor to our soul, providing stability and comfort in times of need and able to withstand any pressure brought against it.

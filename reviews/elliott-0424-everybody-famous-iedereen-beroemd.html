Author-verified transcription

Everybody Famous!, the Belgian entry for the 2000 Academy Awards, will inevitably be compared to the 1983 film, The King of Comedy. Both films center around the kidnapping of a star in order to advance the career of a wannabe. Where Scorsese's film provided a biting commentary on the nature of fame and the lengths that people will go to achieve celebrity status, Everybody Famous! has a lighter, more entertaining message at heart.

Jean Vereecken (Josse De Pauw) has big dreams. Not for himself of course. His tedious job as a bottle inspector at the nearby factory has all but erased any personal fantasies he may have once entertained. His dreams now are for his overweight and insecure daughter, Marva (Eva van der Gucht) who, he believes, has the talent to become the next pop music superstar.

Not everyone sees Marva through the loving eyes of a doting father. Her appearances in the local talent shows are embarrassingly bad as she squeezes into a Madonna inspired corset and painfully makes her way through one of the American star's popular numbers. To her father's chagrin, she's yet to be allowed to finish a song.

Vereecken refuses to quit believing in Marva. He's convinced that she needs only the right opportunity and the right song. He thinks he has the song which he "wrote" by humming into a tape recorder. The opportunity presents itself when he crosses paths with Debbie (Thekla Reuten), the Flemish version of Christina Aguilera.

He kidnaps Debbie and for a ransom, demands that his humming tape be developed into a hit record and that Marva be allowed to sing it on national TV. Not only does Debbie's manager (Victor Löw) agree, he starts manipulating the events to his advantage, taking the "kidnapping" to a level which Vereecken never expected.

All of this is played for laughs and there are plenty to be found. Josse De Pauw shines as an average man who desperately wants more than an "average" life for his daughter. His ill-conceived and amateurish plan is not helped by the "assistance" of his naive and tenderhearted partner, Willie (Werner De Smedt), who treats their victim more like a house guest.

Eva van der Gucht finds the proper balance as the young overweight girl who knows that people will always judge her first by her appearance. She keeps the hurt that accompanies that awareness just under the surface of the protective facade she constructs. The scene where she sings for a children's puppet show, hidden from view behind the makeshift stage, lets us see that her father's belief in her talent may not be completely unfounded.

Victor Löw steals more than a few scenes as Debbie's scheming manager who recognizes that this kidnapping might prove to be an opportunity to sell more records than Madonna and The Beatles combined.

Writer/Director Dominique Deruddere (Crazy Love) constructs an unusual and somewhat unbelievable scenario but places within it characters that are both real and appealing. Even as they behave in ways that are neither smart nor beneficial, we find ourselves rooting for them because their hearts are basically good.

The character of Jean Vereecken illustrates the biblical observation that a father loves with his heart, not his eyes or ears. Vereecken sees his daughter not for what she appears to be, but for who she really is and for everything she could be. We have a heavenly Father who sees us with the same kind of vision.

But the LORD said unto Samuel, Look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; because I have refused him: for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart. 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV)

The elements which make a person uniquely special will be found only by looking beyond the surface appearance. If we would simply look at ourselves in the same way that God views us, two things would immediately occur: Our self-esteem would skyrocket; and our obsession with outward appearances would show itself to be both shallow and ridiculous.

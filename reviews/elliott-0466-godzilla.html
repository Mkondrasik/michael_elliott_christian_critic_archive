Author-verified transcription

If you haven't yet heard, Godzilla is one really big lizard! I mean BIG! So big that when it sits around the house, it really does sit AROUND the house! (ba dum bum!) It's so big, every time it stretches, it changes zip codes! (ba dum bum!) We're talking big... Its shadow alone weighs 3,000 pounds! (ba dum bum!)

No, the makers of Godzilla don't try to pass off these old jokes as fresh. Sadly, they don't need to. They obviously have an ample supply of stale humor and well worn plot devices which they used with great abandon in order to piece this piece of hype together. Technical wizardry aside, Godzilla is a really dumb movie.

Matthew Broderick (The Cable Guy) stars as Niko Tatopoulos, a scientist with the NRA (Nuclear Regulatory Agency) who is studying the effects of radiation on the Chernobyl earthworms. Suddenly, he is called away to handle a bigger problem... much bigger. So big, it makes Chernobyl seem like nothing more than a microwave oven on the fritz. (ba dum bum).

He is joined by Philippe (Jean Reno, Mission Impossible), a mysterious "insurance agent" who feels a personal responsibility for the appearance of the creature; Audrey (Maria Pitillo, Dear God), Nick's college girlfriend who is trying to break into television journalism; and Animal (Hank Azaria, The Bird Cage), a TV cameraman who gets up close and personal with the title character.

Oh yes, there's also Godzilla. Did we mention that it's big? A cross between Jurassic Park's T-Rex, The Lost World's raptors, and NBC's Manimal, Godzilla stands head and shoulders above them all. (ba dum bum!)

This is no bit player in a cheesy rubber suit. The 1998 creature is a result of some masterful craftsmanship on the part of computer animators, miniaturists, and robotics engineers. The creature is a remarkable achievement. But it is the only redeeming feature of the movie (unless you have an unexplainable urge to see New York City thoroughly trashed by the monster and those firing missiles at it).

Throughout the film, we kept getting a sense of "been there, seen that." The filmmakers, instead of originality, opted to rely totally on previously proven formulas, reworked dialogue, and lots and lots of explosions. Big ones. So big, they make "Fat Boy" look positively anorexic. (ba dum bum!)

Obviously it is hard to take this film seriously. But even with all of the forced wit and improbable situations, there is a spiritual truth buried in the dialogue. Early on in the film, as the military minds are trying to figure out how to find the hiding Godzilla, Nick makes a simple but highly insightful observation. He says, "Find out what its needs are. He'll come to you."

As Christians, we are commanded by God to reach out to all nations with His truth. Many have taken this godly exhortation as a justification to practice an in-your-face, militant brand of religious evangelism. It's the kind of thing that gives Christianity a bad name. God is all love. He neither wants nor expects people to be browbeaten into submission. His heart is for people to give their lives freely with meekness and thanksgiving.

As evangelistic-minded Christians, we are most effective when we deliver "a word fitly spoken" [Proverbs 25:11 KJV]. God promises to meet all needs. We should take the time to discover what the needs are in the lives of those to whom we speak. We should show them the promises of God that will specifically address those needs. If they have a heart for God, and a desire for deliverance, they will respond. And as they do, they will not be disappointed. Our God is big enough to supply any need. He's big enough to provide all that we could ever ask or think. How big is He? He's so big that when He goes fishing, He uses Godzilla for bait. (ba dum bum - Cha-Ching!)

Small Time Crooks

Sermon / spiritual topic: Wealth; riches; money; contentment; God's provision

Author-verified transcription

Many believe Woody Allen's newest film, Small Time Crooks, to be a throwback to the madcap comedies of his earliest days. Comparisons to Take the Money and Run, Bananas, and Sleeper are being made with regularity by the media critics. After seeing Mr. Allen's latest endeavor, I'm inclined to quote the prolific philosopher "Anonymous," who once said "nostalgia isn't what it used to be." To my eye, Small Time Crooks only lives up to the first two words of its title.

Woody Allen and Tracy Ullman (Tracy Takes On...) play a married couple for whom a plate of turkey meatballs and an evening of Yahtsee might be considered "high living." A failure as a bank robber, Ray Winkler (Allen) now works as a dishwasher while his wife, Frenchy (Ullman), an ex-stripper turned manicurist struggles just to keep a little money in the bank.

Ray decides to use the little they have in savings as "seed money" for a new caper. He brings in three dimwitted partners in crime and masterminds a plan that has Frenchy selling cookies from a rented storefront while the gang is in the back of the shop tunneling to the bank vault down the street. This poorly conceived plan is doomed to failure... and success. The film is mildly amusing as we watch the bumbling burglars botch the bank job, but the film shifts gears once these lowlifes come into their newfound wealth and try to rise from the hoi polloi and join the haute couture set.

The problem is that the humor of this film sputters as often as its lead character. Some of the lines are full of the customary Allen wit, but all too often the words do not ring with the stinging sarcastic accuracy of films gone by. What pleasure is derived comes from the portrayals of some of the secondary characters: Elaine May (In The Spirit) as May, the dim-bulb of a sister brought in to help in the cookie store; Michael Rapaport (Mighty Aphrodite) and Tony Darrow (Mickey Blue Eyes) as the pair of partners so dumb that "they never finished kindergarten because they were drafted;" and Ms. Ullman as the acerbic housewife with questionable taste turned patron of the arts wannabe whose idea of cultural improvement begins with memorizing the dictionary. (She never gets out of the A's, so while her aspiration is to be admired, in actuality, her accretion is the antitheses of auspiciousness.)

Perhaps Mr. Allen's nebbish New York persona is wearing thin on me but I found him to appear uncomfortable and unfocused in front of the camera. As a result, Ms. Ullman's performance suffered. Most of the time it was required for her character to play off his and without their relationship ringing true, much of what their characters did appeared to be scripted, forced, and unbelievable.

Still, there were moments to enjoy. An extended scene where the tunnelers tap into a NY water main is very amusing and a network TV interview of the classless (and clueless) nouveau riche also contains a chuckle or two. But beyond that, there is little in Small Time Crooks that will generate more than a mild smile or nod of recognition. It is a far cry from the guffaws that greeted Mr. Allen's earliest films.

The main point of the film (if there is one - Mr. Allen is on record as saying that he only set out to make a funny picture) is the effect that incredible riches will have on an individual. From God's perspective, the amount of money one has is not the definitive means to judge whether one is wealthy or not.

There is that maketh himself rich, yet hath nothing: there is that maketh himself poor, yet hath great riches. Proverbs 13:7 (KJV)

To this day, the love of money remains the root of all evil. It is a temptation to which our generation is particularly prone. If we wish to graduate from the "small time" to the "big time," we should aspire to seek out the riches of God. We need not be crooks. These are riches that need not be stolen. He freely gives them to all who ask.

Author-verified transcription

It has been said that you can't go home again. Braff, in his film Garden State, proves that sometimes home is the only place to go... even when it is New Jersey.

Braff plays Andrew Largeman, a young man who, when we first meet him, is laying in a semi-conscious state in bed listening to his father's message on his answering machine. The message is that his mother has died.

As Andrew returns home for his mother's funeral we slowly learn a few things about his life. He works as a waiter in an LA restaurant; he was in a movie where he played a mentally challenged quarterback; and he has been on anti-depressant medication since he was ten years old. It's that latter fact that explains why Andrew seems so disconnected from the world around him.

For some unexplained reason, Andrew decides to leave his medication in LA during his trip back east and as the film progresses, he slowly begins to rediscover himself and come to terms with the "ghosts" of his past that continue to haunt him. fall. During this journey of self- discovery, he meets and spends time with a number of quirky characters - some from his childhood and some which he meets along the way.

The person who seems to have the most impact on him is a young girl named Sam (Natalie Portman) whom he meets in the waiting room of his neurologist's office. Andrew finds himself being drawn to Sam, despite the fact that she is an unrepentant though charming liar.

He also spends time with his best friend Mark (Peter Sarsgaard), now a pot-smoking grave digger; and avoids spending time with his father (Ian Holm) who wants to talk to his son about "things."

Clearly Braff is traveling along familiar cinematic ground with this tale of a young man's self- discovery and quest for independence and deliverance. Although the story is familiar, Braff includes so many quirky characters and situations that it remains fresh and, until the closing moments, unexpected.

Braff, who looks like a young Ray Romano, underplays his role a bit too much by making Andrew the calm eye in the swirling storm of unusual characters which revolve around him. Still this is an impressive debut for a young man who not only directed the picture but also starred and wrote the screenplay.

He is served well by a seasoned cast of professionals. Natalie Portman is a remarkable actress, even when taking on unremarkable roles. She brings to Sam a depth of human emotion and reality that grounds the unusual traits given the character by the writer. Peter Sarsgaard, so brilliant in last year's Shattered Glass, has a natural, easy-going manner that blends well in this ensemble. Veteran actors Ian Holm, Jean Smart, and Ron Liebman also appear in small roles to give the production added value.

The film does contain a fair amount of profanity, drug use and sexual references and, for this reason, an R rating is warranted.

At the core of the film is the idea of dealing with unresolved issues. We come to learn that Andrew's prescribed medication has been keeping him (and his loved ones) from coming to terms with certain events of his past. By coming home again and taking himself off the prescribed drugs, Andrew is able to face the "demons" of guilt and condemnation and rid himself of those burdens once and for all.

God commands us to "travel light" through this world. That command includes the emotional baggage we tend to carry. Trying to forever carry a lifetime of regrets can cause even the strongest man to stumble and fall. It is best to cast them off and leave them behind us so we might embrace all that the future holds.

Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, Philippians 3:13 (KJV)

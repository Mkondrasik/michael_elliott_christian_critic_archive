Author-verified transcription

Love and Death on Long Island is not as much about love as it is about obsession. John Hurt (Contact) plays a widowed and reclusive English author with the unlikely name of Giles De’Ath. He has purposely avoided entering into the modern technological age by eschewing the use of television, computers, answering machines and movies.

One day, he wanders into a theater thinking he is about to see a filmed adaptation of an E.M. Forster novel. Instead, what he is treated to is a fictitious teenage sex comedy called Hotpants College II. He is about to walk out when who should appear on screen but none other than teen-age heartthrob, Ronnie Bostock (Jason Priestley, TV’s Beverly Hills 90210).

Mesmerized by his looks, De’Ath sinks back into his seat with the stirrings of a schoolgirl crush beginning to blossom within him. The crush quickly becomes an obsession as he sets out to learn everything there is to know about Ronnie. Reading every fan magazine imaginable, it isn’t long before he’s a Ronnie Bostock expert and on a plane to New York with plans to meet him. (I believe they call that stalking.)

There is something a bit pathetic about watching a man on the shady side of middle age becoming infatuated with the image of a young guy. Regardless of how artistically accomplished the film may be, the story line is still perverse.

Mr. Hurt is very amusing in the scenes where De’Ath, in order to pursue his obsession, is forced to accept twentieth century technology. Even buying a movie ticket is an unfamiliar adventure. And when he sets out to buy a VCR so he may watch Ronnie’s B-movie career, he is unaware that a television is also required.

Mr. Priestley, as the object of De’Ath’s obsession, does an unobtrusive job. Truth is, the role could have been ably filled by any number of male models. It may not be the fault of Mr. Priestley that he gives an unmemorable performance... There simply wasn’t much in the way of a character written into the script.

Working from the novel by Gilbert Adair, writer/director Richard Kwietniowski (Cost of Love) clearly wanted the emphasis to stay on the character of the aging writer and his growing fascination with the young and rising star. De’Ath’s emergence from the technological dark ages to accept and utilize the various electronic devices of the time seems to symbolically parallel his acceptance (and, one imagines, eventual practice) of his “budding” homosexuality.

It is interesting that De’Ath never questions himself as to whether or not this new interest of his is a healthy one. He obviously had some sense of shame as we see him hiding his fan magazines and scrapbook of clippings. He even gave his housekeeper the explicit and unusual instruction of never cleaning his office area for fear she may stumble onto his secret. This need for secrecy certainly should have given him an indication that there was something improper about the thoughts he was entertaining.

A man is still in control of his thoughts. And thoughts dictate action. Throughout the Word, God instructs us to renew our minds; to bring into captivity (get control of) every thought to the obedience of Christ. God could not have given those instructions to us if it were not achievable. Of course, it requires an individual commitment and exercised discipline on the part of every man to think, speak, and act in a manner pleasing to God. To live righteously... Now there is an obsession worth having.

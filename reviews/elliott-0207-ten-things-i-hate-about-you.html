Ten Things I Hate About You

Author-verified transcription

To say that 10 Things I Hate About You is a modern-day version of William Shakespeare's Taming of the Shrew gives far too much credit to the former while undervaluing the worth of the latter.

First time screenwriters Karen McCullah Lutz and Kirsten Smith have kept some of the names (Kat, Bianca, Verona) as well as the basic premise whereby a father insists that his eldest daughter marry (or in this case, date) before allowing his younger daughter to do so. To compare the two works beyond that is futile. One is an enduring comedy classic of love and relationships. The other is an easily forgettable, sexually obsessed piece of film obviously tailored to the hormone driven youth of America.

Kat Stratford (Julia Stiles, Hamlet) is an intelligent, attractive and independent high school senior who chooses not to date (much to her father's glee). Not that she has been getting many offers... her shrewish manner and sarcastic wit keeps potential boyfriends at bay. Her younger and more popular sister, Bianca (Larisa Oleynik, The Baby Sitters' Club), has got suitors lining up outside the door. But Dad (comedian Larry Miller), an obstetrician who has seen too many teenage pregnancies, has laid down the family law: When Kat dates, so can Bianca. But then again, when Kat dates, hell will apparently freeze over.

When Cameron (Joseph Gordon-Levitt, Halloween H2O) meets and falls for Bianca, he hatches a plan to find a suitor for Kat. Patrick Verona (Heath Ledger, Two Hands), class hoodlum, is chosen as the most likely candidate to be able to withstand and wear down Kat's tough veneer.

The young cast of 10 Things I Hate About You is engaging enough. We like them. What is objectionable about the movie is the sexual climate that it presents as being acceptable. I recognize that the Hollywood product is targeting the teenage consumer market but that it does so irresponsibly should not be tolerated.

Director Gil Junger, in his feature film debut, does not attempt to reach the hearts and minds of his audience, as did Shakespeare in the play upon which this film is based. Rather, Mr. Junger's aim is considerably lower. Had Mr. Junger set the film (with all its overt sexuality) other than in a high school, perhaps we would have a different reaction. But to watch student Kat flash a teacher to distract him or a high school counselor consumed with writing a lurid romance novel rather than helping the students of her school is offensive, especially to parents who are trying to instill decent and moral standards for their children to follow.

Adults have the responsibility to help children as they transition to adulthood. Movies, such as this one or Cruel Intentions, which pander to a child's emerging sexual awareness, is detrimental to that child's maturing process.

Shakespeare's original Taming of the Shrew has much more to offer us in terms of insight. In his play, the shrew (Kate) is not as much "tamed" as she becomes willing to accept her godly role as wife.

Wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands, as unto the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church: and he is the saviour of the body. Ephesians 5:22-23 [KJV]

As we submit ourselves to the will of God, it frees God to work within us to will and do of His good pleasure. As we obey, we are blessed. And there is no blessing bigger than that which God provides as we obey His Word.

That lesson is missing in 10 Things I Hate About You. In fact, all lessons are missing from the film. What is left is sophomoric jokes, puerile humor, and a pseudo-maturity attempted by teenagers who have not yet grown up. Shame on these filmmakers for catering to the errors and misguided sensibilities of youth.

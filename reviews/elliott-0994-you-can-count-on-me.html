You Can Count On Me

1 hr: 51 min

Author-verified transcription

You Can Count On Me is a quiet little film that has managed to make some big noise during a season that has not been known for being particularly loud. After winning the Grand Jury Prize at the Sundance Film Festival, it has gone on to win numerous acting, directing and screenplay awards and nominations from critics' groups around the country.

It is a simple non-action oriented story built around the range of emotions that run between an orphaned brother and sister. Except for their parents dying while they were still small children, nothing major happens in their lives. It is the detailed crafting of the characters and the exactness of the small town environment that make this film both interesting and real.

Laura Linney (The Truman Show) stars as Sammy, a young single mom raising her son, Rudy (Rory Culkin, Richie Rich). Her life is, on the surface, quite normal: She has a boyfriend with whom she is reluctant to commit to a deeper relationship; a job with a new boss (Matthew Broderick, Ferris Bueller's Day Off) whose priggish manner and time clock mentality grates on her nerves; and a church pastor who counsels by using "how do you feel about it" psychology rather than "what does God say about it" standards.

Into this life she excitedly welcomes back her brother, Terry (Mark Ruffalo, Ride With The Devil), who has grown into a shiftless wanderer with little sense of responsibility and less ambition. Even though she discovers that he only came to town to borrow money, Sammy nonetheless invites him to stay with her and Rudy and implores him not to cut his visit off early.

A marvelous ensemble cast displays great depth of feeling in this character study of a film. Laura Linney shines as a flawed individual whose flaws are born of a kind but direction-less heart. In many ways, her life is as aimless as her brother.

Mark Ruffalo has pegged a difficult to describe character. Terry is an aimless enigma throughout the film and Ruffalo plays him with such utter conviction that he arrests our attention and actually makes slackness something interesting to watch.

Matthew Broderick can play an uptight perfectionist as well as anyone and he does so here with wry humor and deadpan expressions.

This is an impressive directing debut for writer/director Ken Lonergan (screenwriter for Analyze This.) There aren't a lot of cinematic tricks and no MTV fast cut editing. Instead, Lonergan relies upon his actors to tell their characters' stories and adeptly allows them the freedom to do so.

As can be said of us all, each of these characters have their good traits as well as their bad ones. We see them make poor choices in life and struggle with the consequences afterwards. In particular, an adulterous affair serves as a focal point in the story and is the catalyst for a scene between Sammy and her pastor.

When asked for the church's stand on adultery, the pastor haltingly responds, "It's a sin. But we don't like to focus on that." He then tries to get her to discuss her own feelings. His ineffectiveness in this scene speaks loudly as to the responsibility of spiritual leaders to always communicate God's standard of truth to help His people discern right behavior from wrong.

Sammy entered this scene desiring to have a firm guideline to follow. She wanted to know the doctrine of truth to apply to her life and situation. God gave us His Word to serve as that doctrine.

I will instruct thee and teach thee in the way which thou shalt go: I will guide thee with mine eye. Psalms 32:8 (KJV)

Both Sammy and Terry are lost souls. Good hearted, kind individuals who are traveling through life without a map or compass. Stumbling, making mistakes and doing the best they can without a clear understanding of how to attain and maintain righteousness. There are millions of people like them in the world and there isn't one of them whose life would not be enriched and improved by a working knowledge of God's Truth.

Center Stage

May 12, 2000

3 stars

1 hr: 55 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Dance; worship; wholehearted service

Author-verified transcription

The American Ballet Academy is for the creme de la creme. Only a dozen or so new students are accepted each year. And of all those who study there, only a very few are accepted into their professional company. It is this highly competitive world that serves as the background behind Center Stage.

Cooper, the golden boy of the professional company, whose talents are exceeded only by his ego; Jody, the fresh-faced ingenue whose technical skill is deficient but whose love and desire to dance knows no bounds; Eva, the extremely talented dancer with a chip on her shoulder the size of Lincoln Center and an attitude to match; Maureen, the technically proficient student who questions whether she has the heart of a dancer; and Charlie, the sweet faced, good-natured guy who vies with Cooper for the attention of the ingenue.

Added to the mix are the oppressive stage mother, the understanding dance instructor, the all-powerful artistic director, and a troupe of young dancers who serve as the backdrop for this story of "making it" in a field where so few do.

Director Nicholas Hytner (The Object of My Affection) and producer Laurence Mark (As Good As It Gets) wisely chose to cast their film with dancers rather than actors. As a result, the acting level is sufficient to move the story along but, oh my... the dance sequences are indeed something beautiful to behold.

The story itself is a simple one: These ballet students work diligently at the Academy, perfecting their skills and building towards the year-end student workshop performance which is the launching pad for many a career.

American Ballet Theatre superstar Ethan Stiefel plays Cooper. His athleticism and ability to incorporate modern moves into a classical art form makes his dancing very accessible and enjoyable for all ages. Amanda Schull, an apprentice with the San Francisco Ballet, plays Jody Sawyer, the aspiring dance with the wrong body type and the nontraditional technique who nonetheless has made it to this elite circle because when she dances from her heart, she is impossible not to watch.

The film gives an accurate depiction of the arduous and oftentimes unrewarding life of a dancer: Hours of rigorous training, driving one's body to explore the outermost limits of its capabilities, the personal sacrifices that must be made to excel in this competitive field, and the pitfalls that exist (one of the characters is bulimic as a result of the incredible pressure she is under to control her weight and body type.)

There is a level of physical sensuality to the film which gives the film its PG-13 rating. Dance can be sexually suggestive and Center Stage utilizes this as a means to tell its story.

Choreographers Susan Stroman and Christopher Wheeldon each produce some wonderful dance moments. In particular, Ms. Stroman's final narrative ballet which combines the best of jazz, rock and classical forms is simply electric, exploding off the screen with a highly charged energy which is reminiscent of Gene Kelly's Broadway ballet in the MGM musical Singing In The Rain. Thoroughly enjoyable.

And that is what dance can do. It can express the inner joy and emotion more dynamically than any other art form. Biblically, there are many examples and exhortations to be cited where dance is an appropriate means of worship.

Let them praise his name in the dance. Psalms 149:3a (KJV)

Of course, there is a technical skill to dancing that can be developed and honed, but unless the technique is being used as a means to communicate the inner joy, happiness, or passion of the individual, the dance will be shallow and cold. The same can be said of our spiritual lives. We can learn the "technique" of being a Christian: praying, tithing, etc.. But unless those actions come from the fullness of our hearts, they can have no discernible lasting impact.

Blow

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 7, 2001
Rating
2½ stars

2 hr: 4 min

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Love of money; greed; material wealth; consequences

Author-verified transcription

Blow is not really a film about cocaine. It is a film about money... and how one man's desire for material wealth could lead him to become the chief US supplier for the illicit drug.

The film is based upon Bruce Porter's book, Blow: How a Small-Town Boy Made $100 Million with the Medellin Cocaine Cartel and Lost it All, which was, in turn, based upon the life of George Jung. Jung is currently serving a 15 year sentence resulting from his involvement in the drug trade. To hear him tell it, during the 1970's, he was responsible for 85% of the cocaine being distributed within the United States. Curiously, the filmmakers manage to turn him into a sympathetic character.

Johnny Depp (Chocolat) stars as the New England kid who becomes an internationally known drug dealer. After a childhood remembered mainly for the financial difficulties his family continually had, George leaves for the sunny beaches of California with his friend Tuna (Ethan Suplee, Remember the Titans). Introduced to a flamboyant hairdresser (Paul Reubans, PeeWee's Big Adventure) who keeps his clientele supplied with ample amounts of marijuana, George begins selling joints on the beach.

Enticed by the easy money, he broadens his operation and, using his stewardess girlfriend (and her uninspected bags) as transport, begins to build a bi-coastal business. He finally hits the big time when he gets busted for marijuana possession. Prison turns into a learning experience for him. He enters with a "bachelor's in marijuana," but he leaves with a "doctorate in cocaine."

Upon his release, he is quickly introduced to Pablo Escobar (Cliff Curtis, Three Kings) and becomes the major conduit for transporting cocaine to the West Coast. The high life isn't permanent and the devastating decline which Jung experiences is indeed a sad waste of a life.

Johnny Depp, who has never shied away from playing controversial characters, does an amazing job as Jung. That he could even approach a level whereby the audience sympathizes with his character is testimony to his ability.

I didn't particularly care for the performance of Penelope Cruz (Woman on Top), who played Jung's Colombian wife. To my eye, her histrionics were forced and too pronounced. They served to distract rather than add to the scenes which called for them.

However, a standout performance is given by Ray Liotta (Hannibal) who plays Jung's loving father. The conflict which is at war in the heart of his character is fully realized and the scenes between Jung and his father are among the best in the film. Rachel Griffith (Hilary and Jackie) also does a first rate job portraying Jung's shrewish and materialistic mother.

One of the main reasons we feel some compassion for Jung is because director Ted Demme (Life) chooses to keep the focus on George rather than on the destruction and pain caused by the drugs which he sells. No moral judgment regarding the use or sale of drugs is made during the telling of this story. Rather, the story is told from the perspective of George Jung and it is obvious that he was so self-absorbed that he remained oblivious to the horrific consequences which his actions caused the nation.

His father tried to talk some sense into him at an early age. Aware that his father was having financial troubles, Georgie expressed his concerns to his pop who replied, "Money isn't real. It doesn't matter. It only seems like it does."

George not only ignores this "advice," he takes the opposite position. In his mind, money is the only thing that does matter. And even though he was living high and mighty for a time, he eventually found out that such "happiness" isn't permanent.

He that hasteth to be rich hath an evil eye, and considereth not that poverty shall come upon him. Proverbs 28:22 (KJV)

Scriptures say that the love of money is the root of all evil. Nothing that we see in Blow or in the life of George Jung gives proof to the contrary.

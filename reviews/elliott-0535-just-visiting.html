Author-verified transcription

As we all have experienced, some "visitors" can quickly outstay their welcome. Fifteen minutes into Just Visiting, it was obvious that this was going to be such an occurrence.

Adapted from the successful 1993 French comedy, Les Visiteurs, Just Visiting is unique in that both French actors are reprising their roles for the American version, as is the original writer/director Jean-Marie Poiré. Still, even with so much borrowed from the original, something was definitely lost in the translation.

Count Thibault (Jean Reno, Ronin) and his trusty servant André (Christian Clavier, La Soif de l'or) are thriving in the midst of the great unwashed and therefore aromatic 12th century. However, on the eve of his wedding, Thibault mistakenly drinks a witch's potion. During the hallucination which follows, he runs a sword through his beloved.

To correct this great tragedy, Thibault and André turn to an addled-brained English wizard (Malcolm McDowell, A Clockwork Orange) who instead of sending them back in time before the murder occurs, transports them forward to Chicago of the year 2000.

There, with the help of his descendant Julia (Christina Applegate, Don't Tell Mom The Babysitter Is Dead), Thibault struggles to make sense of his surroundings and its modern conveniences all the while looking for ways to get home to his own time. André, on the other hand, is being introduced to the sweet taste of non-servitude by the hippie gardener who works next door (Tara Reid, Josie and the Pussycats) and may not wish to return to his old life of groveling at his master's feet while eating scraps off the floor.

The "fish out of water" device is one which is no stranger to Hollywood. It has been done many times before and many degrees more effectively than we see here. Much of the "humor" is merely the oh so predictable confusion of the two inept time travelers coping with such strange "creatures" as automobiles and televisions. And what comedy would be complete without a flatulence scene, a regurgitation scene and a scene of our characters bathing in a toilet?

Jean Reno is badly miscast. He is far more effective playing the straight man to his costar's turn as a Jerry Lewis-like clown. Christina Applegate and Malcolm McDowell, with their minor characters, fare better. She, by fading into the background with her rather simplistic and unchallenging role and he, in fu manchu mustache, by wielding the wizard "shtick" with much gusto. Matt Ross (American Psycho) and Bridget Wilson Sampras (The Wedding Planner) are also on hand as the stereotypical villain and mistress.

One of the major differences which occurred during the "Americanization" of the film is in the depiction of social categories. My understanding is that the original film emphasized the distinctions between nobility and the servant class to a far greater extent, heightening those distinctions by transporting a master and slave to modern times where that relationship is no longer supposed to exist. As such, the comedy made an important social statement.

This particular element was so watered down in the American version that it failed to make much of an impact. It is a pity, as the social commentary might have given Just Visiting the proper edge that it needed to keep our interest.

Looking at the social implications from a different perspective, the idea of servitude is nothing new to those of a biblical mind:

But now being made free from sin, and become servants to God, ye have your fruit unto holiness, and the end everlasting life. Romans 6:22 (KJV)

The difference, of course, is that the service we owe to God is not due to our social standing or our parentage. It is a decision that we make of our own free will in response to all that God has freely given to us. To serve is a mature, willful and loving act which is greatly exalted in the spiritual realm. No groveling is required or expected.

Boiler Room

“If anyone tells you that money is the root of all evil, they don’t have any.” With that pitch, the recruiting officer of J.T. Marlin welcomes his newest trainees to the Boiler Room. On the ladder of stock brokerage respectability, the boiler room, or “chop shop” rests on the bottommost rung.

Into this world enters Seth (Giovanni Ribisi, The Mod Squad), an enterprising college dropout looking for a way to make serious money and earn his disapproving father’s love and respect. After running an underground (illegal) casino out of his apartment, Seth is enticed to stop dealing for chump change and to set his vision for the mountain top. The firm of J.T. Marlin assures its trainees that they will become millionaires within 3 years. Not bad for a 20 year old who can’t decide what he wants to be when he grows up.

The question the trainees don’t ask and that the movie illustrates so vividly is what will be asked in return for the promise of obscene wealth. Seth learns at the hands of his trainers how to lie, manipulate, and otherwise coerce his “clients” into releasing their money to him.

In the world of fast talking con men, Seth blossoms. And yet vestiges of decency remain. He’s incredulous that his coworkers are making all this money but haven’t a clue how to spend it. He manages to talk a everyday Joe (Taylor Nichols, The Last Days of Disco) into investing with his firm, and actually feels bad when the guy loses his entire savings.

Like the movies Wall Street and Glengarry Glen Ross, both of which receive prominent mention in this film, the crux of the matter is money. Lots of it. And whether the pursuit of it must mean the forfeiture of everything else... Things like morals, ethics, compassion, legalities...

In writer/director Ben Younger’s first feature film, high pressure telemarketing combines with stock trading to form a look at a despicable practice run by self-serving individuals whose only concern for their fellow man centers around how they can use them.

Mr. Ribisi is at his best when on the phone, trying to close a deal on yet another unsuspecting client. A love interest between Seth and a secretary at the firm (Nia Long, The Best Man) doesn’t really work and is extraneous to the central themes.

Vin Diesel (Pitch Black) is explosive as Chris, one of the young old timers at the firm who takes Seth under his wing to teach him the tricks of the trade. Nicky Katt (The Limey) is a bit too one-dimensional as Seth’s immediate group leader to be totally effective.

Ron Rifkin (The Negotiator) has some powerful moments as Seth’s father who tries to straighten his child out by his open condemnation of his actions. The scenes between father and son are real and raw with emotion.

Ben Affleck (Reindeer Games) has the role of Jim Young, the recruiter who builds the fire in the bellies of the young men who want to take the fast road to riches. His scenes are basically monologues - inspirational speeches designed to whet the lust and appetites of the money hungry.

But they are based on a very old devilish trick. The quote mentioned at the beginning of the review is actually from the Bible. But it has been misquoted. According to God, money is not the root of all evil.

For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows. 1 Timothy 6:10 [KJV]

There is nothing wrong with money. It is merely a tool and a necessary one at that. But when the desire to obtain it becomes the dominant force in one’s life... When the love of money is held in higher regard than the love of God, one’s life is out of balance and many sorrows will surely follow.

The devil is a tempter and he is the god of this world (2 Corinthians 4:4). As Seth stated in his opening narrative, when we see stuff like lottery winners or Internet IPO windfalls, it plants seeds. We start thinking that maybe we don’t really need to work to get rich. In this devilish world, that may be so... But what the devil tries to hide from us is the fact that we’ll be the poorer for it.

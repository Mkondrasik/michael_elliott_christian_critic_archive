Amazing Grace

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 23, 2007
Rating
4 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Colossians 3:232 Chronicles 31:20-212 Corinthians 9:8

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Christianity

Author-verified transcription

#Amazing grace, how sweet the sound that saved a wretch like me...#

This well-known hymn with its inspiring lyrics has blessed countless souls throughout history. One such soul was William Wilberforce who was greatly influenced by the man who wrote the tune, John Newton.

In the late 1700s, the African slave trade was not just a commonly accepted practice; it was viewed as the bedrock to a nation's economic stability. Wilberforce (Ioan Gruffudd), a likeable member of the British Parliament, is tapped by a small group of visionaries to lead the charge on a movement to abolish the slave trade throughout the British Empire. One of these visionaries was William Pitt (Benedict Cumberbatch), a childhood friend of Wilberforce's and a man who would one day be England's Prime Minister.

The first anti-slavery resolution brought before the Parliament by Wilberforce was easily defeated. It would be a long uphill climb before he tasted any kind of success. Wilberforce devoted his life and risked his health and reputation for the cause he came to embrace so passionately.

Part of his passion came from the first hand accounts he heard from his pastor, John Newton (Albert Finney). Before ever penning the titular hymn, Newton served as the captain of a slave ship for eighteen years. Coming to realize that slavery was a sin against God and man, Newton left the trade and began a life of spiritual meditation and reflection, becoming the preacher of a small English church.

Newton once told Wilberforce that he lived in the church with "20,000 ghosts" of the slaves he transported in such an inhumane way that most died during the passage. The knowledge of such things led to a passion to end them that would carry Wilberforce through the struggles that lay ahead of him.

Ioan Gruffudd is a fine central figure to the film, capturing the man's gentleness while, at the same time, letting us see the fire that burned within him as he considered the injustices of slavery. It is an intelligent performance that is a tribute worthy of the man he plays.

Benedict Cumberbatch gives quiet support as Pitt who, for political reasons, is forced to stay behind the scenes of this very public debate. Romola Garai adds warmth and tender encouragement as Barbara Spooner, Wilberforce's wife-to-be. Albert Finney is mesmerizing in his all-too-brief appearances as John Newton. And Rufus Sewell turns in a quirky performance as Jacobin conspirator Thomas Clarkson.

Director Michael Apted has mounted a handsome production and the technical aspects of his film are first rate. Not only is it an engaging drama, it is a much needed look at an historical event that may have been forgotten by many.

It almost didn't happen at all. Wilberforce found his faith in God returning with such conviction that he contemplated leaving politics to pursue a meditative life. "Would you use your beautiful voice to praise your Lord or to change the world?" is the question that was asked of him. The only answer, of course, is both.

Our faith does not work well in a vacuum. It must infuse all that we do. Whether we are a politician working to pass a bill or a carpenter working to build a cabinet; following our faith enables us to perform whatever tasks we have in ways that are pleasing to God.

And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men. Colossians 3:23 (KJV)

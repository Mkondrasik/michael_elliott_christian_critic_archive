Author-verified transcription

Oh, the irony. A hardworking, morally-minded young man from the heartland of America defies the odds and earns an academic scholarship to the prestigious New York University. This immediately gets him labeled as a “loser” by the drug dealing, emotionally stunted, and insanely popular slackers with whom he shares a dorm room. Such is the tale of LOSER, directed by Amy Heckerling (Clueless.)

Jason Biggs (American Pie) stars as Paul Tannek, the hapless protagonist who is both socially awkward and unbelievably naive... two attributes which the hip New Yorkers apparently don’t much appreciate. While his future rides on keeping his GPA high enough to merit his scholarship money, his eyes keep drifting to the only friendly face he has seen since biting into The Big Apple. It belongs to Dora Diamond (Mena Suvari, American Beauty) who is, sadly to say, in the midst of a physical relationship with her caustic and demeaning English professor (Greg Kinnear, As Good As It Gets.)

Dora, though more experienced than Paul, is just as naive. Her gentle nature shines through despite the despicable situations in which she manages to find herself. Paul is smitten.

The film takes the obvious course: Paul and Dora become friends; Paul feels something more; Dora senses something but doesn’t know what; Paul backs off because of Dora’s relationship with the teacher; the teacher treats her badly - Paul is there to comfort her; Paul’s roommates treat him badly - Dora is there; and the story progresses to its all too inevitable end.

The pity of this predictable and vapor-thin tale is that Mr. Biggs actually manages to present a character that is at once charming, likable, virtuous, socially awkward, and unbelievably blind to deceit and corruption. He plays the world’s perception of “dork” without being one. It is just a shame that the other characters are so distasteful as to make the filmgoing experience an uncomfortable one.

The bigger pity is the waste of a cameo appearance by Dan Aykroyd as Paul’s father. Amazingly, in this zephyr of a film, we have a parental figure who actually gives his teenager some decent advice. What’s more, their father/son relationship is shown as being mutually loving and supportive. Oh, that this was a better movie so this brief interchange between a wise father and his loving son could be appreciated by more! This relationship is the healthiest part of the film.

But the focus is on the unhealthiest -- the obstacles that come between our young lovers to be. On his side, it is his perversely twisted roommates who slip drugs to girls in order to have sex with them and lie in order to get Paul ejected from the dorm. On her side, it is the professor/lover who treats her like the nonentity he considers her to be. It is abundantly clear that Paul and Dora are classified as “losers” only in the sense that they “fail” to fit into a crooked and perverse world in which they do not belong.

The irony of the title should not be lost to us. It is especially true that we, as Christians, should never allow others to dictate to us the definition of our own identities.

When we stand long enough upon the Word of God, and are as outspoken in our beliefs as God commands us to be, “loser” may be one of the milder words to be thrown our way. Remember the instruction of our Lord:

If ye were of the world, the world would love his own: but because ye are not of the world, but I have chosen you out of the world, therefore the world hateth you. John 15:19 (KJV)

The key is not to believe what the unbelieving world may think of us. We have a true standard by which we may judge ourselves. A standard, unshakable by time, uninfluenced by prejudice, greed, or envy. A standard established by the Creator of life Who says that all who turn unto Him shall not be “losers” but shall be forever victorious.

Road to Perdition

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 12, 2002
Rating
3 stars

2 hr: 00 min

Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Sin; Unrighteousness; Forgiveness

Author-verified transcription

According to the dictionary, one definition of perdition is “the loss of a soul, i.e. eternal damnation.” But Road to Perdition is more about the struggle not to travel that road. More to the point, it is about a father who is on that road but wants to keep his son from following.

In Chicago of 1931, the mere mention of the name Michael Sullivan (Tom Hanks, Castaway) would generate both fear and respect from those in the know. Not only is he a loyal “employee” of crime boss John Rooney (Paul Newman, Where The Money Is), he’s as much of a son to him as can be possible without actually sharing DNA. Any difficult job that Rooney needs handled falls to Sullivan. In an era that includes Al Capone and gangland activities, such jobs most certainly include murder.

Sullivan has always been careful to keep his “career” from being discussed within his own home, although the unspoken reality hovers over the family like a cloud. When Michael Jr., his 12 year old son (Tyler Hoechlin, Train Quest), accidentally witnesses his father in action, Sullivan must suddenly deal openly and honestly with his son regarding what he does and why.

When it becomes clear that the mob does not trust a 12 year old to keep quiet about what he has seen, Sullivan and son take to the road to escape the contract placed on their lives. In order to survive and eliminate future threats, Sullivan must teach his son some tricks of the trade he never wanted him to learn.

Road to Perdition is confidently directed by Sam Mendes whose only other film credit was the award-winning American Beauty. This film, however does not have the feel of a sophomore effort. Mendes shows a level of artistry and craftsmanship which is truly enviable. The pace of the film is admittedly slow, but our interest never flags.

As Sullivan, Tom Hanks plays the closest thing to a bad guy as he’s probably ever going to get. His familiar on-screen persona softens the character he plays to the point that our focus is shifted from the fact that he’s a cold blooded killer and allows us to concentrate on his humanity which is evidenced through the relationship with his son.

What a treat to see Paul Newman in a project worthy of his presence. He is stellar as the patriarchal head of the Rooney crime syndicate with professional loyalties that equal but don’t quite overlap family bonds. Jude Law (AI: Artificial Intelligence) has some nice moments as a perversely troubled hit man who is contracted to kill Sullivan.

Road to Perdition would have failed as a project without a strong actor to play Michael Jr. This character is truly the heart and soul of the film and Tyler Hoechlin more than rises to the challenge.

An advisory note: This film deals with the lives of mobsters and hit men. Violence, language, and criminal behavior are on evidence throughout the film. It also includes a fair amount of religious referencing although not as much as is reported to be in the graphic novel upon which this film is based.

While I thought that the thematic elements could have been strengthened, the core of the film is remains evident: a wayward father wanting to give his son every opportunity to live his life in a manner that he did not... regardless of the risks to himself. At one point, when Rooney and Sullivan are discussing the complexities of the situation they are in, Rooney makes the point that “none of us are going to heaven.” Sullivan immediately replies, “Michael could.”

His love and desire for his son is certainly understandable. What is sad is that Sullivan so limited God that he thought he was beyond forgiveness.

This is a faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptation, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief. 1 Timothy 1:15 (KJV)

The road to perdition, or perhaps better stated, the road of unrighteousness is one that can be taken by anyone at any time. But no matter how far down that road one travels, thanks to God’s love and forgiveness, it is never too late to change course.

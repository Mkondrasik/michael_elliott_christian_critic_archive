School of Rock

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
October 3, 2003
Rating
3 stars

1 hr: 48 min

Original Author Copy

Author-verified transcription

Jack Black, in true Spinal Tap fashion, turns his manic energy up to "eleven" in the hardcore rocking comedy, School of Rock.

Black (Shallow Hal) plays Dewey Finn, a rock and roll purist extraordinaire who believes that the music he worships is about more than scoring with chicks, getting wasted or becoming famous. To Dewey, rock and roll is all about rebellion and "sticking it to the man." Unfortunately, his band is all about the chicks, the drugs, and the fame. Fed up with his 20 minute guitar riffs and embarrassed by his intense passion, the band votes to dump him from the group so they might better prepare for the prestigious "Battle of the Bands" contest.

Dewey's passion is undeterred. He vows to start a new band but first there's a little matter called rent that he has to handle. Cash poor, he takes the first opportunity to make money that comes his way. He pretends to be his nerdy roommate and accepts a substitute teacher's position at a highly regarded prep school.

Lo and behold, what does he find there but a collection of classically trained musical prodigies? Granted, they're only ten years old and don't know AC/DC from the rest of the alphabet, but if he works them relentlessly during the school day, he might just get them ready to compete in the "Battle of the Bands." So he discards the school's regular curriculum (which he had no interest in teaching anyway) and begins "Rockology 101."

Black's tour-de-force performance provides the energy and quirkiness needed to move this film over the many rough patches in the script. He succeeds on multiple levels. Largely because of his work and the way he conveys Dewey's passion for rock and roll, this becomes a story that an audience can connect with and care about.

The premise is certainly a winning one: an antiestablishment rocker teaches a younger and more privileged generation on the fine points of "rocking out." The kids in the film, in addition to being musically accomplished, are a lot of fun to watch. From the shy, overweight girl whose lack of self-image masks a killer voice to the uptight Chinese keyboardist who doesn't know how to be cool, each of these young people have their own backstories which adds to the richness of the tale.

Joan Cusack (Where the Heart Is), as the strict and overly proper principal of the prep school, almost does the impossible. She nearly steals this film from the ever manic Jack Black. She gets my vote for the most under appreciated talent working today in Hollywood. Her scenes are golden.

But in the end, this is Mr. Black's film which he shares with his younger costars. Screenwriter Mike White (Chuck & Buck), who also appears in the film as Dewey's nebbish roommate, and director Richard Linklater (Waking Life) wisely build their film around Black who rewards them by staying on warp drive from beginning to end.

The passion his character displays for rock and roll has its effect on people. While some may be embarrassed by it, others become inspired by it. There is absolutely nothing wrong with being passionate. God wants us either hot or cold - lukewarm does not interest Him. It is our passion for the truth that God has given us... a passion that emanates from us as we live our lives... that makes an impact. Those who hunger for the truth will be attracted by it.

And they said one to another, Did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures? Luke 24:32 (KJV)

When we are passionate about a subject, our words and our demeanor change. We become more urgent and more excited. It is as if we care that people understand our passion and agree with us. In our case, those of us who are passionate for the truth of God, truer words have yet to be spoken.

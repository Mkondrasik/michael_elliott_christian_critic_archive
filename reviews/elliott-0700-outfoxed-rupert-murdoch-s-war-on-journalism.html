Author-verified transcription

To say that filmmaker Robert Greenwald doesn't care for the Fox News Channel is like saying that Michael Moore is a little bit peeved at President Bush. In Outfoxed: Rupert Murdoch's War on Journalism, Greenwald attacks the Fox Channel and its self-described "fair and balanced" news programs with the fervor of a pit bull. Unfortunately, his attacks are without Moore's acerbic wit and humorous appeal.

Greenwald's aim is to show how Fox News, far from being an unbiased member of the fourth estate, serves as a mouthpiece for the GOP and the Bush administration. Utilizing footage from the channel and testimonies from Fox ex-employees, Greenwald tries to hoist the news organization on its own conservatively ideological petard.

The problem is that there's no revelation being made here. It is difficult to claim that Fox's conservative bent is being concealed when it is so readily apparent to anyone who watches the network. Without more damning evidence of a sinister hidden agenda on the part of Fox's executives, the documentary shows itself to be just one more piece of left wing propaganda being slung about this political season. It is so focused on presenting just one side of the issue that it takes on the feel of a poorly done infomercial. For a little over an hour, Greenwald tries to sell us on his brand of fairness in journalism without exhibiting it himself.

A number of Fox's broadcast personalities are painted in an unfavorable light - largely due to their use of a bombastic and arrogant style that invites criticism. In one elongated segment, Bill O'Reilly exchanges heated words with Jeremy Glick, the son of a Port Authority worker who died in the Sept. 11 terrorist attacks. Glick signed an anti-war advertisement which O'Reilly felt used anti-American rhetoric. The dialogue between the two went badly and ended with the host continually cutting his guest off and finally having him escorted from the studio.

Other key moments to the film include the circulation of corporate memos (many by Fox VP John Mooney) which are used to show the deliberate slanting of news stories through the employment of phrases like "some people say..." allowing the reporter to then insert his opinionated commentary under the guise of reporting the news. The memos shown fail to present the "smoking gun" that might prove Fox News guilty of purposely misleading the public.

The documentary does offer some statistics that raise questions regarding how Fox News watchers perceive important issues of political concern. However, it is impossible to accept any of the "facts" presented by this overtly judgmental film without further research. I have no way of confirming if any of the statistics used in this film are accurate and I cannot accept them on face value.

What Greenwald fails to address in this 77 minute bludgeoning of the conservative news channel is the counter programming that exists on the other networks. Perhaps he didn't read Bias by Bernard Goldberg which exposes the liberal leftist leanings of CBS News and the other network news channels. Then again, perhaps he just didn't want to remind us of it. No matter. Whether we read the book or not, the liberal sentiments that exist and influence the reporting of the news on the other network stations are clear to see.

Fox News Channel does lean (politically speaking) in the opposite direction. Perhaps that is why so many are attracted to it. If we are going to blast them for being biased to a particular ideology, we are going to have to blast all others. The better solution is to be aware that all news sources spin what they deliver to serve their own interests and thank God that he has blessed us with the ability to discern truth from error.

We are of God: he that knoweth God heareth us; he that is not of God heareth not us. Hereby know we the spirit of truth, and the spirit of error. 1 John 4:6 (KJV)

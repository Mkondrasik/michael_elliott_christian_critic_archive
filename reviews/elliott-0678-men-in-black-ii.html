Author-verified transcription

The best sequels that come from the movie factories of Hollywood are those that prove they have more to reveal about their characters (ie. Godfather II). The worst are those that are hurriedly slapped together in order to financially capitalize on the popularity of the original (ie. Speed II). Men in Black II falls somewhere in between these extremes. It certainly wasn't rushed into production immediately after the 1997 blockbuster hit, but neither does it share anything new about the characters or their situation.

We can recall the premise. Aliens have, on a small scale, inhabited the earth. In order to control their activities, a super-secret organization known as MIB (Men in Black) has been developed to monitor them and rein them in whenever they become unruly. In order to be effective and minimize human panic, they must also remain completely covert. Thus, any civilian who becomes aware of aliens or MIB operations, must have his memory erased, or as they like to call it, neuralized.

Neuralized was exactly how we left the top MIB agent in history, Kay (Tommy Lee Jones, Double Jeopardy) at the end of the first film. Unfortunately, as the sequel opens, it is Kay's memory that happens to contain the information needed to save the world from global destruction. And so, the filmmakers must figure out a way to de-neuralize him. Easier done than said.

Reunited with his former partner, Jay (Will Smith, Ali) Kay must then reach into the depths of his erased memory to discover what he has done with the "light of Zartha," an object so valuable that an evil, vine-like alien bug named Serleena (Lara Flynn Boyle, Happiness) decides that it must come to earth disguised as a Victoria Secrets' lingerie model in order to recover it.

Meanwhile, Jay must not only deal with his old partner reappearing and assuming authoritative control but he also must grapple with his surprising feelings towards a civilian eyewitness (Rosario Dawson, Josie and the Pussycats) whom he can't bring himself to neuralize.

This convoluted and ultimately disappointing plot amounts to very little as the actors seem satisfied to lazily collect their paychecks and simply let the CGI animators upstage them with their various alien creatures. However, except for the pug named Frank and some rather disgusting maneuvers from Serleena's tentacle-like vines, the animation and makeup effects are uninspired.

Perhaps that's because it's all recycled material. What was fresh and original in the first film feels tired, stale, and unfunny here. With no reason to produce a sequel other than the obvious financial ones, director Barry Sonnenfeld (Big Trouble) and screenwriters Robert Gordon (Galaxy Quest) and Barry Fanaro (The Crew) fall back to copy the original film, hoping that no one would notice. And who knows? In a world that makes Adam Sandler's Mr. Deeds the number one box office champ, anything is possible.

As far as gleaning a message from Men in Black II, it comes from the observation of Kay, who at one point says, "We are who we are... even if we sometimes forget." Granted, it isn't a very deep or profound message that Kay delivers, but it is nonetheless true... and biblical.

For if any be a hearer of the word, and not a doer, he is like unto a man beholding his natural face in a glass: For he beholdeth himself, and goeth his way, and straightway forgetteth what manner of man he was. James 1:23-24 (KJV)

It is all too easy to forget who we are in Christ and instead live our lives according to the world's standards. That does not make us any less the children of God. It simply means that we are not heeding our Father's instruction and exhortation.

God continues to remember who we are even if we sometimes forget. He lovingly implores us to "de-neuralize" ourselves from the world's programming and return to being a "doer of the Word."

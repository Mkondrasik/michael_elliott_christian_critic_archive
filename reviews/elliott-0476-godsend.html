Author-verified transcription

There is no worse thing that a parent can suffer through than the death of his child. That is the central event around which Godsend revolves.

Paul and Jessie Duncan (Greg Kinnear and Rebecca Romijn-Stamos) are a pair of urbanites happily raising their eight-year-old son Adam when an accident delivers to them an unexpected tragedy. Even before they have had time to grieve, Dr. Richard Wells appears to them to offer an opportunity to do it over. He explains to them that he has perfected the cloning process allowing for their son to be reborn.

At first horrified by the immorality of such a suggestion, they become so overwhelmed with their grief that they agree to all the conditions that Wells presents. They leave the city, move into a home in the country provided by Wells, and agree to keep this highly illegal procedure a secret.

Nine months later, Jessie gives birth to a second Adam and, once again, life is happy in the Duncan household. It is happy until Adam crosses the threshold and goes past the age where the first Adam died. That's when things start getting weird.

Adam starts getting visions and hearing voices. His demeanor changes. He becomes disobedient and distant. He becomes prone to acting upon destructive impulses. Dr. Wells chalks it up to a normal case of night terrors but Paul suspects something deeper and more sinister is at play. He aims to get to the bottom of what is wrong with his son or else go public with Dr. Wells' big cloning secret.

It is hard to pinpoint exactly where this film begins to fall apart, but fall apart it does. By the end of the film, the story has gotten so ludicrous it is hard to muster any interest over what the outcome might be. And even then, we're still disappointed.

Greg Kinnear does a credible job as the doting father who suspects that the cloning procedure somehow triggered a genetic defect in his son. Cameron Bright is appropriately creepy as the son who seems to be straddling two worlds... the living and the dead.

But Rebecca Romijn-Stamos is merely adequate in her role and Robert De Niro has evolved into nothing more than a shadow of his former self. It is a flat and lifeless performance ill-befitting his abilities.

Nick Hamm, a respected British theatrical director, presides over this self-destructing combination of weak thrills, a poorly constructed plot, illogical characters, and a final twist we see coming from the first 30 minutes of the movie.

It doesn't even allow for an interesting debate on the pros and cons of the cloning issue as the film doesn't bother to explore the moral implications of the scientific breakthroughs that have been made.

The clearest "lesson" that can be observed from watching the characters is that making decisions based upon deep emotional responses will often lead to devastating and harmful results. At such times, the wise and prudent course would be to seek out people we trust to solicit their advice and recommendations. When our logic is being affected by troubling emotions, it is not the time to be making decisions that will have unseen ramifications. Paul and Jessie learn the hard way. We don't have to.

Where no counsel is, the people fall: but in the multitude of counsellors there is safety. Proverbs 11:14 (KJV)

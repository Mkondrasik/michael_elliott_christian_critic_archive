Author-verified transcription

In Wonder Boys, Grady Tripp is a writer in an unusual position. Instead of suffering from writers' block, he is in desperate need of one. His life is obviously out of control: his wife has left him, his students are either trying to seduce him or emulate his drug induced behavior, his married girlfriend is pregnant, and his manuscript, the long awaited follow-up to his wildly successful first novel published over seven years ago, has reached a mind numbing 2611 pages and is showing no signs of coming to a finish.

With the coming of the annual "Wordfest" weekend celebration, things really begin to get interesting for Grady. His editor comes to town with a six foot something transvestite, his prized student shoots and kills his pregnant girlfriend's husband's dog, and a total stranger appears to be stalking his car.

Giving credit to director Curtis Hanson (L.A. Confidential) and his talented cast, this frenetic, somewhat scattered story line is cogently told with no small amount of humor.

Michael Douglas (A Perfect Murder) turns in a funny but sad performance as a man who, having found success early (a "wonder boy"), is so frightened of failure that he is unable to escape it. Grady is the human equivalent to a rudderless ship which has no ability to steer itself down a planned course.

Tobey Maguire (The Cider House Rules) gives another deadpan depiction of a boy/man coming of age. As the talented but morbidly morose James Leer who looks to Grady as his debauched mentor, Mr. Maguire properly foreshadows the film's "wonder boy" curse of early success.

Francis McDormand (Fargo) plays Sara Gaskell, the chancellor of the university where Grady teaches, the wife of the head of the English department where Grady works, and the woman Grady has impregnated. Calm, in the face of the craziness around her, she is the needed "straight man" that magnifies the humorous quirkiness of Grady. And her love of him is what gives this apparent loser whatever degree of credibility he has at the beginning as well as the possibility of redemption at the end.

Robert Downey Jr. (Bowfinger) gives an over the top performance as Grady's struggling editor, the amorally bisexual Terry Crabtree who focuses in on James as his next "project." The irony of Mr. Downey appearing in a film which deals with how the early success of an individual can bring personal ruin to his life is eminently palpable.

There is no condoning the actions depicted in the film, notably the ones between the professor and his students. Even in a movie, to show a trusted professor sharing dope with a student, or looking the other way as one of his students, drunk and high, is seduced into having a homosexual encounter goes way beyond decency. The film is based on the novel of University of Pittsburgh graduate Michael Chabon and is, one would hope, a work of pure fiction.

Still, Mr. Hanson's accomplished direction and Mr. Douglas' layered performance combine to produce its share of funny moments.

At the root of Grady's troubles is his paralyzing fear of making decisions or choices. It is why his work in progress is continuing ad infinitum, why his marriages fail, and why he continually looks to escape life in the haze of marijuana smoke. What he doesn't seem to realize through most of the picture is that not making a decision is still a decision. It is just a weak and passive one. God has given us the means by which to make strong choices in our lives. To not do so out of fear or slothfulness is distasteful to Him.

I know thy works, that thou art neither cold nor hot: I would thou wert cold or hot. So then because thou art lukewarm, and neither cold nor hot, I will spue thee out of my mouth. Revelation 3:15-16 [KJV]

God has set a clear choice before us and has given us the free will to make it. Nothing... Not fear, not stubbornness, not slothfulness, not peer pressure... Should stop us from moving forward with our lives, making the choices God would have us to make.

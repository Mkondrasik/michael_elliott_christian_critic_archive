The Constant Gardener

Author-verified transcription

Every so often a film comes along that is critically acclaimed for reasons which totally escape me. The film I generally use as an example of this is the Academy Award winning film The English Patient which, for me, cured whatever insomnia I might have had. I now have a second film that I can use. Ironically they star the same actor. The Constant Gardener with Ralph Fiennes has been reaping rave reviews. It nearly put me to sleep.

It's not that I can't see the quality that is evident behind the film. It's just that I can't appreciate the quality of the film itself. Some individual components are quite excellent. But, for me, it just doesn't gel together to make an evening's entertainment worth nine dollars.

It opens with a low ranking, quietly conservative British diplomat named Justin Quayle (Ralph Fiennes) bidding an airport farewell to his wife, an activist spitfire of a woman named Tessa (Rachel Weisz). In the next scene, he is informed that she has been murdered. He takes the news with stereotypical British aplomb.

Driven by a mixture of curiosity, grief and jealousy, Quayle begins to look into his late wife's interests and activities and uncovers governmental corruption which no doubt led to Tessa's untimely death.

Part murder mystery, part social commentary, and part love story, The Constant Gardener fails to engage its audience at any level. The primary reason for this disconnect is the manner in which Fernando Meirelles (City of God) has chosen to shoot the film. Using a hand-held camera, the images bounce around the screen so frequently there was a concern that the projectionist might have been having some kind of seizure. It was both irritating and alienating.

The performers tried and nearly succeeded in overcoming the director's visual style. Rachel Weisz, in particular, did outstanding work as an outspoken crusader for human rights. Though her character is deceased and seen only in flashbacks, she remains the beating heart of the film.

Ralph Fiennes has the harder, if less showy role. Beginning as an oh-so-proper English bureaucrat, he slowly morphs from investigator to social activist as he feels obliged to complete what Tessa had started. I'm sure Fiennes brought great nuances to his role - nuances so subtle that you might not even recognize that they are there. I certainly didn't and as a result was bored with his performance.

What I did recognize was that Tessa, for reasons of her own, chose to keep secrets from her husband. That choice forced her to take risks without first obtaining Quayle's approval or agreement. That's not a good thing. God is the architect of the marriage relationship and He built into it some safeguards meant to protect those who follow His design.

"But every woman that prayeth or prophesieth with her head uncovered dishonoureth her head: for that is even all one as if she were shaven." 1 Corinthians 11:5 (KJV)

To understand the above verse, we must recognize that in a marriage, the husband is considered to be the head of the wife.. even as Christ is to be the head of the man. This figure of speech emphasizes the importance for the wife and husband to come to one accord before taking action. To go out with one's "head uncovered" is to take action without the spiritual insurance policy that God makes available.

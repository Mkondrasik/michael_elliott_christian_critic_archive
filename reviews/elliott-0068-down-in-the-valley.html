Down in the Valley

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
May 19, 2006
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Isaiah 44:20John 8:321 John 2:21

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Lies

Author-verified transcription

A bored teenager looking for love and adventure gets more than she bargained for when she hooks up with a cowboy drifter in Down in the Valley.

Tobe (Evan Rachel Wood), short for October, does her teenage best to ignore her authoritative father (David Morse) and tolerate her sullen little brother (Rory Culkin) but she makes it abundantly clear that she’d rather be left alone.

On her way to the beach she runs across a gas-pumping cowboy (Edward Norton), from South Dakota who seems out of place in California’s San Fernando Valley. She impulsively invites him to join her. Claiming never to have seen the ocean, he quits her job and comes along for the ride. This begins a rather creepy love affair between the underage girl and the thirty-plus cowboy (named Harlan) that her father understandably opposes.

As the film progresses we see that there is something seriously wrong with Harlan and the story is filled with a sense of foreboding as the characters begin to spiral down to what will have to be an unhappy end.

Edward Norton’s performance as a old west character in a modern environment almost saves the film from a script that starts out strong but doesn’t finish particularly well. Adopting a laconic manner that fits the character like a well worn pair of Levis, he “aw shucks” his way in and out of trouble.

Evan Rachel Wood and Rory Culkin do some good work as a rebellious teen and her scared-of-the-dark, tag-along brother. David Morse is saddled with a poorly written stereotypical character of a father who can’t connect with his own children. Bruce Dern breathes life into a minor role of a California horseman who unfortunately crosses paths with Harlan.

In the end, writer/director David Jacobson’s film is about lost and lonely people who, unhappy with reality, are all too ready to believe the lies they prefer. Even when the lies are exposed, they would rather close their eyes to the truth and remain in their romanticized, make-believe world (that is, until the lies turn ugly and tragedy waits at the door.)

No lie, no matter how romantic it may appear or desirous as it may seem, is ever preferable to the truth. While scriptures promise that “the truth shall make you free,” lies serve only to place us under bondage. As we believe the lies told to us (or worse, the ones we tell ourselves) we are bound to them – our actions are dictated by them – and they have no substance. They sap us of our strength instead of helping us to build it.

He feedeth on ashes: a deceived heart hath turned him aside, that he cannot deliver his soul, nor say, Is there not a lie in my right hand? Isaiah 44:20 (KJV)

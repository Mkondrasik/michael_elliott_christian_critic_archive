Author-verified transcription

New Line Cinema's PRICE OF GLORY tells the story of a man whose unrealized dreams of becoming a world champion boxer are transferred to his three sons... with more than a little prodding from Papa.

Jimmy Smits (My Family) plays Arturo Ortega, whose quest for the championship is cut short due to the unscrupulous actions of his manager who sacrificed him and his career for a quick payday. Now an assembly line worker, he vows that his sons will have the life of which he once dreamed. Since boxing is the only means he knows by which to bring that life to them, it is boxing which becomes the overriding life force of the Ortega family.

The boys, under his tutelage, excel and show great promise, even as signs of stress and inner turmoil begin to rise to the surface. Arturo is a hard and unforgiving trainer with qualities he mistakenly carries over to his role as father to his boys.

The eldest, Sonny (Jon Seda, Selena) is a talented and intelligent fighter who eventually begins thinking of a life outside of the ring. The news of his impending marriage is not joyfully embraced by his father which starts the making of a rift between them.

The middle child, Jimmy (Clifton Collins Jr., Light It Up), is more of a plebeian fighter, preferring to mix it up toe to toe than to use any finesse or strategy. He desperately seeks his father's approval while choosing to ignore his training advice. Thus begins the making of a rift between them.

The youngest boy, Johnny (Ernesto Hernandez in his film debut), mirrors Arturo the most. His blind adoration of his father will lead to a tragic end, ultimately making a rift impossible to mend.

Each of the aforementioned actors perform their roles well though not memorably so. The film simply failed to capture the heart or empathy of the audience. Perhaps it was because Mr. Smits' character, while driven to have his boys succeed in the only arena in which he had expertise, so clearly loved them that the schism which later develops between father and sons did not seem particularly threatening to their relationship.

In a supporting role, Maria Del Mar (Eclipse) as Arturo's wife adds a sympathetic female perspective to the Latin machismo which permeates her screen family. Paul Rodriguez (A Million to Juan) plays it straight as an errand boy to fight promoter Nick Everson (Ron Perlman, Happy, Texas) who desires The Fighting Ortegas on his boxing card... on his terms.

The boxing scenes are actually few and far between, this being more a film about a family divided and restored rather than about any particular athletic bout. Perhaps that explains why the film's climatic championship fight has an anticlimactic feel to it, seeing that the main story line and conflict of the film had already been resolved.

Arturo learns that chasing "glory" comes at a great and dear price. His obsession with obtaining a boxing championship was so great, he lost sight of the truly important lessons his sons needed to learn from him. By chasing his dream, he nearly chases away his family.

For all flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass. The grass withereth, and the flower thereof falleth away: 1 Peter 1:24 (KJV)

Manmade "glory" is both temporal and temporary, unworthy of the sacrifices men make to attain it. In contrast, those who focus on the higher things of God are the recipients of a promised glory which shall never fade. A glory so precious that God gave His only begotten son as payment so that it might be made available unto men.

To whom God would make known what is the riches of the glory of this mystery among the Gentiles; which is Christ in you, the hope of glory: Colossians 1:27 (KJV)

The full manifestation of that glory is an anticipated reality. But, as with all that God has promised, it shall come to pass and we shall stand as champions, resplendent with the full glory of God.

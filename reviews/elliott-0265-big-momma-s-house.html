Big Momma's House

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 2, 2000
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 39 min
Source
Published Web Version

Sermon / spiritual topic: Patience; endurance; perseverance; waiting; completion

Author-verified transcription

In the new Martin Lawrence comedy, Big Momma's House, it is sometimes difficult to determine who Mr. Lawrence is portraying... Big Momma or the house. The central joke of the film is based upon an FBI agent who goes undercover by posing as an elderly and overweight black woman. Actually, that statement may be a bit erroneous... It's not the central joke of the film -- it's the only joke.

Martin Lawrence (Blue Streak) plays Malcolm, a daredevil of an FBI agent who has martial arts skills and is a master of disguises. The film opens with him infiltrating an Asian gambling ring which succeeds in showing us within the first five minutes everything we need to know about his character.

When a murderous bank robber (Terrence Howard, The Best Man) escapes from prison, he and his partner John (Paul Giamatti, Private Parts) are assigned to tail the fugitive's ex-girlfriend, Sherry (Nia Long, Boiler Room), who has long been suspected of being an accomplice in the robbery but never charged with the crime. She high tails it to Big Momma's (Ella Mitchell, Which Way is Up) house where the FBI await.

But before Sherry arrives, Big Momma is unexpectedly called out of town, leaving it up to Malcolm, with an large assist from his prosthetics crew, to become Big Momma in order to find out how much Sherry knows and catch the bad guy who is sure to come after her. He promptly falls in love with the girl but must hide his feelings beneath the 350 pound fat suit he wears.

As I said earlier, the film is one long joke milked in different settings: Martin Lawrence as a fat woman playing basketball; Martin Lawrence as a fat woman dancing in church; Martin Lawrence as a fat woman in a karate class; and so it goes.

As a fat woman, Mr. Lawrence is unmistakably funny, albeit crudely so. Flatulence, bathroom humor, sexual innuendoes, and off-color language abound. Many of the jokes are at Big Momma's expense (or should that be expanse?) Still, he manages to exude a certain amount of charm and warmth through the make up and rubber suit.

Nia Long is beautiful and appealing as Sherry, although the character is drawn in such a way that there is no doubt as to whether she was ever involved in the robbing of the bank. Paul Giamatti is relegated to doing nothing more than standing by, looking incredulous as his partner gets himself deeper and deeper into ridiculous situations. Terrence Howard, as the bad guy, is underutilized. He manages to bring an appropriate tension when he appears on screen, but those moments are few. Ella Mitchell, as the cantankerous Big Momma, who allows herself to be used as the butt (literally speaking) of many jokes, emerges with her dignity bruised but otherwise intact.

Director Raja Gosnell (Never Been Kissed) is unable to elevate the material from the crude and scatological depths in which it resides. There may be an audience for such humor (the screening crowd with whom I watched the film was certainly loud and boisterous in their appreciation), but I don't count myself among them. The film's moral point (one's actions and appearances might lie but truth can still be in one's heart) was misleading and too easily made.

A better lesson can be found midway through the film in a brief and inconsequential scene. Malcolm is teaching Sherry's son Trent (Jascha Washington, The Wood) how to catch fireflies. The secret, he says, is to move slow, be patient, and wait for them to come to you.

For ye have need of patience, that, after ye have done the will of God, ye might receive the promise. Hebrews 10:36 (KJV)

In a world where instant gratification seems to be the name of the game, we need to be reminded from time to time that patience (or better said, endurance) has its own rewards. Oftentimes, we are in such a rush to claim the victory, we forget that the game must first be played to its completion.

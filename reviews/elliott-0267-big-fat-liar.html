Big Fat Liar

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 8, 2002
Rating
2½ stars

1 hr: 28 min

Published Web Version

Sermon / spiritual topic: Truth; lying; honesty; trust; integrity

Author-verified transcription

To be politically correct, we could say that fourteen year old Jason Shepherd (Frankie Muniz, Malcom in the Middle) is truth challenged. To put it more bluntly, we could simply call him a Big Fat Liar.

After being caught in an updated version of "the dog ate my homework," Jason actually has to sit down and complete a writing assignment. On his way to turn it in he runs into film producer Marty "the Wolf" Wolf (Paul Giamatti, Planet of the Apes) and accidentally leaves his homework in the unscrupulous producer's limo. Jason is suddenly placed in the unusual position of using the truth as an excuse and learns what most habitual liars come to discover... no one will believe him.

When Jason hears that Marty is planning to make a movie based on his story, he decides that the only way to earn back the trust and respect of his parents, teachers and friends is to travel to Hollywood and confront Marty, forcing him to announce that the idea for his movie came from a high school student who really did do his homework. Big Fat Chance.

When Marty refuses to cooperate, the gloves come off and Jason, with the help of his school chum Kaylee (Amanda Bynes, The Amanda Show) and an army of "Wolf-haters," plots to give this Hollywood hot shot the comeuppance he so richly deserves.

Big Fat Liar is an average kid's comedy which surprisingly contains more than a few elements which enable it to endear itself to older audiences. The two child leads have winning personalities that guarantee to wrest a smile or two from our lips. The ever expressive Frankie Muniz makes for a solid central character and Amanda Bynes is immensely appealing in her role of Jason's resourceful gal pal, Kaylee.

Painted (literally) as the bad guy, Paul Giamatti is exceedingly funny. Despicable, devious and arrogant beyond all measure or reason, Wolf is a character which allows Giamatti not only to "chew the scenery" but actually digest quite a bit of it.

Director Shawn Levy (The Famous Jett Jackson) knows that he's making a film for the preteen demographic and does not apologize for the silliness of the story. The earnestness with which he has his performers give to their roles, even in the face of such a slight and implausible story, is exactly what elevates this film above the mundane.

Having much of Big Fat Liar take place on the back lots of Universal Studios adds two more enjoyable elements. First, to a pair of young teenagers, a film studio's prop and costume departments is like the largest toy store in the world. Second, it can be fun to spot sets and set pieces from Universal Pictures of years past. Cameo appearances or supporting parts are filled by familiar characters such as Urkel, Screech, The Fall Guy, and ET (known respectively as Jaleel White, Dustin Diamond, Lee Majors Jr., and a puppet).

The film's message is a bit two-faced. Sure, the point seems to be that lying is a bad thing. But audiences can see for themselves exactly how much fun it can be as Jason schemes, lies and maneuvers to exact his revenge on someone who is a bigger liar than he is.

God is clearer in His exhortation to us. Telling the truth is better than lying... always.

Wherefore putting away lying, speak every man truth with his neighbour: for we are members one of another. Ephesians 4:25 (KJV)

The long term benefits of always speaking the truth far outweigh any immediate gratification that comes with even a little "white lie."

The getting of treasures by a lying tongue is a vanity tossed to and fro of them that seek death. Proverbs 21:6 (KJV)

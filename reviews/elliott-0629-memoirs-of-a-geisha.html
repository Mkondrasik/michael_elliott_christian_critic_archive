Author-verified transcription

There are many worlds that co-exist within our world. Some we know about - others are completely alien to us. Of all the worlds shrouded in mystery and secrets, few offer the same allure that the world of the geisha does.

In Memoirs of a Geisha, based upon the best-selling novel by Arthur Golden, we are given a look inside the geisha house to see a life that has nearly vanished from existence. Set in another time, we follow a young girl who moves from poverty to being a menial slave to ending up as the most famous geisha in Japanese history.

Neither a prostitute nor a wife, the geisha is a refined woman specially trained to entertain important men. Skilled at music, dance and conversation they are thought of as “moving works of art” and spend years of training and discipline to perfect the transformation from woman to geisha.

Young Chiyo (Suzuka Ohgo) is sold to a geisha house by a poverty-striken father. Not overwhelmed with the idea of being a geisha, she does a turnabout when she receives an act of kindness from a stranger she knows as “the Chairman” (Ken Watanabe). Suddenly all she wants is to be a geisha so she might be a part of his world.

Under the tutelage of famed geisha Mameha (Michelle Yeoh), she becomes just that. She is renamed Sayuri and, over time, blossoms into a beautiful and cultured woman (Ziyi Zhang) who becomes the toast of Kyoto, Japan.

Director Rob Marshall painstakingly tries to capture the exotic and now-extinct world that Sayuri inhabited and his efforts are evident on every frame. Lusciously shot and exquisitely imagined, the film is a work of art... it just doesn’t tell a story that engages us on a visceral level.

We may feel, at times, to be transported to the land of the geisha but we have no emotional investment in the epic tale to make us want to stay there. Much like the geisha, the film is an illusion of a fantasy but it is also supposed to be a love story and that’s where it fails.

Marshall has cast some of the top names of Asian cinema and, for the most part, they perform admirably. Ziyi Zhang and Michelle Yeoh (both of Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon) and Gong Li (Raise the Red Lantern) were selected as the main geishas (despite the fact that none are Japanese). Ken Watanabe (The Last Samurai) and Koji Yakusho (Shall We Dance?) are the central male figures. All do what they can with a script (by Robin Swicord and Doug Wright) that hamstrings them at every turn.

An interesting lesson is shown by the actions of young Chiyo. When the Chairman gives her money as an act of charity, she turns around and gives the money to the temple along with a prayer that she might one day be a geisha so she might be with him always.

The spiritual act of tithing predates Christianity. It is one of God’s immutable laws that spans all of time. Giving equals receiving. When Chiyo gave, she set spiritual wheels in motion that would eventually culminate in her receiving that which she most desired.

But this I say, He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; and he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully. Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver. 2 Corinthians 9:6-7 (KJV)

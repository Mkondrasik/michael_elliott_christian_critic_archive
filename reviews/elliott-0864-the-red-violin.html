Author-verified transcription

Who knew a piece of wood can have such a large, lasting impact? It can, once it has been fashioned into the “single most perfect acoustic machine” ever made.

The Red Violin tells five distinct stories of five different times and cultures. The common thread -- a masterpiece of a violin which survives centuries of use and misuse.

Director Francois Girard and his co-writer Don McKellar, who previously collaborated on the similarly structured Thirty-two Short Films About Glenn Gould, present to us an interesting, involving, and culturally fascinating look at how music spans time and worlds to touch our souls.

Each tale lasts about 20 or 25 minutes. It’s long enough to get a glimpse of insight into the characters’ lives, short enough to want to know more. By not involving us too deeply with the personalities of each story, the filmmakers keep our focus upon the violin, its music, and the way it affects the lives of those who hear it.

We first see the violin as it is made by 17th century Italian master craftsman Nicolo Busotti (Carlo Cecchi, Stealing Beauty) as a gift for his child-to-be.

From there, it passes through many hands before it lands in the grasp of a Viennese child prodigy (newcomer Christoph Koncz) who is discovered in an 18th century orphanage by the cash poor impresario George Poussin (Jean-Luc Bideau, Marion) with plans to make him famous.

The violin is passed via gypsies until it becomes the property of 19th century English concert violinist Frederick Pope (Jason Flemyng, Deep Rising), who uses the instrument as an way to direct or stimulate his passion (artistic as well as physical.)

From him, the violin finds its way to China where it becomes the favorite possession of Xiang Pei (Sylvia Chang, Eat Drink Man Woman.) Unfortunately, her affection for it must be kept hidden as the 20th century Cultural Revolution denounces all Western music and musical instruments as being anti-Party.

Finally, the violin resurfaces in present day Canada where it becomes the centerpiece of a high-priced auction, and the culmination of a lifelong dream for Charles Morritz (Samuel L. Jackson, Star Wars: The Phantom Menace), an appraiser specializing in musical instruments.

The filmmakers use two devices to connect these disparate tales. The auction brings together the descendants of the various owners of the red violin who are there to try and regain possession of this very special instrument. At the same time, we periodically flash back to 17th century Italy where Busotti’s wife (Irene Grazioli, Mediterraneo), who is troubled about her pregnancy, decides to have her fortune told. It becomes readily apparent that the premonitions we are hearing relate to the violin’s fate rather than that of the woman.

Each tale has its elements of special interest. Samuel L. Jackson continues to be a most enjoyable actor to watch. His work here is understated and remarkably clean. Of the five, the story of Frederick Pope and his mistress Victoria Byrd (Greta Scacchi, Emma) is the least attractive, perhaps because there is so little appeal to their characters.

While there are many spiritually erroneous aspects, (the fortune telling as well as an unspoken curse), the construct of the movie is most interesting. As we follow the violin through the centuries, learning of its history and of the men and women associated with it, we gain a deeper understanding and appreciation for the violin’s worth.

The most valuable thing in the world today (to those of us who believe) is the Word of God. It is constructed in much the same way as this movie. Though written by many men, the Bible has a single author. The scriptures state that the Bible is “God-breathed.” [2 Timothy 3:15] Each seemingly unconnected record has been woven by God into an almost incomprehensibly beautiful tapestry of truth. As we see how each record fits so perfectly with another, our appreciation and understanding of the magnitude and inviolate integrity of God’s Word grows.

Author-verified transcription

T.C. Williams High School - Alexandria, Virginia - 1971. Racial integration comes to the suburbs in the new Disney movie, Remember The Titans. Based upon a true story, this high school’s football squad (nicknamed the Titans), during this most unusual year, proved themselves to be winners in the grandest sense of the word, not only by their accomplishments on the gridiron, but also by their successes in the higher stakes game of life.

How this team overcame the racial tension of the day to set itself up as an example for its home town, which was itself in the midst of struggling with the imposed integration, serves as the driving inspiration for this entertaining and poignant drama directed by Boaz Yakin (A Price Above Rubies.)

Denzel Washington (The Hurricane) stars as Herman Boone, who is pulled into this maelstrom of prejudicial hatred by his politically motivated appointment to the position of head coach of the Titans, replacing the popular and successful Caucasian coach, Bill Yoast (Will Patton, Gone in 60 Seconds.) Yoast, after considerable soul searching, decides to stay on as Boone’s assistant coach and defensive coordinator in order to keep the white players from boycotting the season (and thus sacrificing potential college scholarships) in protest.

Boone, with his no nonsense approach, brutal honesty, and insistent demand for perfection, slowly begins to turn an interracial group of players, who can’t stand to be in the same room together, into a true team who slowly learn how to work together to achieve a common goal.

After their summer camp where they managed to forge an understanding and form a working relationship with each other, they return to town where the racial tension remains dangerously high. The teammates must decide whether they will risk alienation from their own race in order to maintain the tenuous team unity which was built over the summer.

Mr. Yakin’s no hurry direction is complimented by the understated acting of both Mr. Washington and Mr. Patton. A rarity in today’s film making, the true “star” of this movie is the story itself. Each of the performers act unselfishly and by so doing manage to build an ensemble which strengthens the overall production. To single out a performer and highlight his or her performance would not only be inadvisable, it would be contrary to the “team first” message of the story.

And what an inspiring story it is... on so many different levels. In Coach Boone we see the qualities of inner strength and conviction, the importance of preparation and planning, and the unwillingness to accept anything other than the best from himself as well as from those who work for him.

In Coach Yoast, we see that humility is indeed a virtue which strengthens, and that there are more important things than personal glory. Through the players, we see the importance of leadership and how the strongest must help and support the weaker in order for the entire group to prevail. Through the townspeople we see how men and women will respond when positive examples are placed before them and how preconceptions can change when confronted with truth. All of the above are beautiful illustrations of the truth which is contained in the Word of God.

In contrast, and in an apparent nod to our “tolerant” times, there is a minor subplot dealing with a player who may be a homosexual which gives the team one more prejudicial obstacle to overcome. The unspoken allusion is that sexual preference, like skin color, is hereditary. In this, the film errs as there is no scientific evidence to prove this supposition and lots of spiritual evidence to refute it. It is, however, a minor element, lasting only a few minutes in the film.

Perhaps the most important thing we learn is that the color of one’s skin doesn’t matter a whit. This is by far the most valuable lesson of the film and the one which has the greatest impact because it isn’t preached or sermonized with grandiose verbiage. It is simply lived by those on the screen. It is displayed for us through these characters who grew to understand, appreciate, and love one another... not for what they were - but for who they were. The Titans. May they always be remembered.

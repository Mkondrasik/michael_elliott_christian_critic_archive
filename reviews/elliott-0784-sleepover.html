Author-verified transcription

"Thank heaven for little girls." For, as Maurice Chevalier so charmingly warbled, "little girls get bigger every day." In Sleepover, they just seem to be getting bigger (i.e. older) a lot sooner than they did in my day.

Julie (Alexa Vega) and her fourteen year old friends are saying goodbye to 8th grade and looking forward to starting their freshman year in the fall. What better way to celebrate than with an old-fashioned slumber party?

Their good times of extreme makeovers and girlish behavior are interrupted when the "mean girls" of the school led by ex-friend Stacie (Sara Paxton) get bored, and decide to challenge Julie and her gal pals to a scavenger hunt. At stake -- bragging rights and the coveted "cool" lunch spot by the high school fountain.

If that doesn't seem like much, it's probably been a long time since you were exposed to the status consciousness of the teenage mind. As Hannah (Mika Boorem), Julie's best friend, explains, "This isn't about winning a contest -- it's about who you're going to be in high school." The bad news is that, if this scavenger hunt has anything to say about it, it's a good bet they'll graduate as felons.

To successfully win the contest, these young girls will have to engage in such "fun" activities as vandalism, breaking & entering, grand theft auto, underage drinking, stealing, lying, and a little peeping tom-foolery. But they giggle all the way through it so it must be okay.

The appropriateness of their actions goes largely unnoticed by the prepubescent members of the audience who seem to be ones for whom the film was made. They laughed as the lead characters got closer to winning the contest. They cheered as they got closer to losing their hearts to boys who look old enough to be worried about statutory rape charges.

The young actresses all do moderately well with their roles and manage to bring a certain level of adolescent appeal to the film. Alexa Vega and Mika Boorem are given the most well-defined characters as the leaders of their gang and Kallie Flynn Childress has a nice moment or two as an overweight child in search of her own identity.

The adults come off as being well meaning but moronically blind to the events of the night. Sam Huntington as Julie's slacker brother and a trio of skateboarding dorks just come off looking moronic.

What I found troubling about the film is the way director Joe Nussbaum and screenwriter Elisa Bell so cavalierly combine youthful innocence and teenage sensuality. They seem almost eager to push the bar of sensual permissibility further down the age ladder. It has been done before but whereas Catherine Hardwick's Thirteen shocked viewers with its depiction of young girls gone wild, Sleepover simply plays it for laughs which, to me, is less honest and, subconsciously, more disturbing.

Our culture does our young people a severe disservice by tempting them to reach for experiences before they are emotionally ready to have them. Julie complains that her mother treats her like a child. What she fails to recognize is that, at fourteen, she is a child.

Sleepover depicts 9th grade almost as being a right of passage into adulthood. Someone should tell the 5th graders who will make up the audience for this film that it isn't true. Besides, no one need be in a hurry to grow older. Aging is one thing that will come all too soon without our helping it along.

"Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown." Stephen Frears' The Queen opens with this quote from Shakespeare's Henry IV and it is quite fitting. The queen in question is none other than Elizabeth II and the time in which the film is set is within recent memory. The situation revolves around the tragic 1997 death of Princess Diana, who may have divorced her way out of the royal family but never from the hearts of the English people.

It is Diana's overwhelming popularity that causes such a problem for the royal family. Ruled by Elizabeth II (Helen Mirren), they are ill-prepared to treat the event with anything other than the proper protocol: a private ceremony. But the English people will not stand for it.

Newly elected Prime Minister Tony Blair (Michael Sheen) understands the demand for a public demonstration of grief but is not in a position to demand it from the sovereign leaders of his nation. He must walk a fine line as he tries to advise the one person whose responsibility it is to advise him.

The people loved Diana and saw her as their own, living inside the castle. The royals saw Diana as an outsider and a troublemaker, bringing media scrutiny into a life that was long considered off limits to the press.

The film brilliantly presents both sides of the issue and walks a delicate balance so that neither position should feel slighted. There are no enemies or bad guys in this film. There are just different perspectives... and both have their day in the sun.

Helen Mirren, as the titular character, gives an award-winning performance. While there are still two months before the Oscar chase is over, she has to be considered the front runner... her work is that good.

Michael Sheen, as Tony Blair, is her equal but may be overlooked come award time due to the subservient role he must play. But he, more than anyone else, helps to sell Mirren as the queen. His deferential treatment and eventual defense of her add to her believability.

James Cromwell may give the most unsympathetic performance of the film as Philip, husband of Elizabeth. He shows the character to be emotionally cold and unresponsive to the expressed needs of his fellow Brits. While Elizabeth seems to debate within herself, Philip seems to have no need for self-examination.

Frears and company take a bold move in making this film. After all, they are subjects to the very queen they depict. They seem to understand this and allow her the greatest respect in the context of the story they are telling. But -- tell it they do and that may be the last thing she would want.

When all is said and done, what remains true is that a life was lost before its time. And it was a life that had meaning to many people. For whatever reason, there were many people who were grieving its loss and for that reason, those who lead those people had a responsibility to help them through their grief.

Any leader knows that one must put aside self interests in order to properly lead others. Leading is a form of service and service, properly done, must be selfless. We must all share in our grief or our joy if we are to remain likeminded towards our goals.

And if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. If one member is glorified, all the members rejoice with it. 1 Corinthians 12:26 (KJV)

Flightplan

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
September 23, 2005
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Luke 15:4Ezekiel 34:121 Timothy 2:3-4

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: The lost; salvation; parental love; reconciliation

Author-verified transcription

One of the most terrifying moments in a mother's life occurs whenever her child is missing or in danger. Even more terrifying would be to be told that the missing child never existed in the first place. Such is the unimaginable horror that faces Kyle Pratt (Jodie Foster) aboard Flightplan.

Recently widowed, Kyle departs Berlin with her daughter Julia (Marlene Lawston) and her husband's casket. They are bound for America to lay his remains in its final resting place. Three hours into the flight, the already distraught Kyle awakens to find her daughter has vanished. Her anxiety mounts as she searches the plane's cabin to no avail.

Her increasingly erratic behavior catches the attention of the air marshal (Peter Sarsgaard) and flight crew. Recognizing that the child could not have wandered off the plane, they are rather ambivalent about the situation which simply sends Kyle another rung up the emotional ladder. To make matters worse, there is no record of Julia's presence on board the aircraft – no boarding pass, nobody who remembers seeing her. The crew begins to think that perhaps the child is a figment of Kyle's emotionally disturbed frame of mind. And who's to say that they are wrong?

Director Robert Schwentke keeps the red herrings flying past us so fast that it is understandable if we might feel trapped in the middle of a certain San Francisco fish market. Unfortunately, the herrings are rather obvious and instead of directing us away from what's happening they instead allow us to rule out what isn't.

Foster begins the film in such a tightly wound emotional state that there isn't much room for her to take the character anywhere but over the top. Her initial histrionics seem more of a screenwriter's contrivance than an outgrowth of her character development.

Everyone else in the film has been directed to so underplay their roles (to set them up in contrast to the hyperactive Kyle) that the film plays like everyone except for Foster is on a muscle relaxant drug. Peter Sarsgaard plays the calmest air marshal working in a post 9/11 world.

There are a number of plot holes, any one of them being large enough to hide a 787, within the screenplay. However, none of them are large enough to obscure the last 30 minutes of the film. Once all the red herrings have been discovered and the film gets down to what's really happening, the story becomes engrossingly entertaining. It just takes an hour to get to that point.

Kyle does demonstrate the love a parent has for her child. When a child is missing or lost, a loving parent will move heaven and earth to be reunited with him. God, as our heavenly Father, is no different.

What man of you, having an hundred sheep, if he lose one of them, doth not leave the ninety and nine in the wilderness, and go after that which is lost, until he find it? Luke 15:4 (KJV)

God declares in His Word that his desire is for all men to be saved. Until they are, they remain lost to Him. We have been entrusted by God to help reconcile those who remain lost. We should have the same determination to save them that Kyle demonstrated in the search and rescue of her daughter.

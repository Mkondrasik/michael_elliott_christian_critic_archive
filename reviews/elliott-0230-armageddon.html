Armageddon

Author-verified transcription

We've had Die Hard on a skyscraper in Los Angeles... Die Hard at an airport in Washington DC... and Die Hard in the subways of New York. Since it looks like we're running out of places on this earth where we can Die Hard, why not take the plot and move it off the planet?

This could have been the question posed by the makers of Armageddon, a movie which, while technically not in the Die Hard series, certainly contains all of its elements.

Bruce Willis (Die Hard) plays Harry Stamper, the best oil driller in the world. He and his charming albeit dysfunctional crew of roughnecks become the earth's only hope for survival against the global killing asteroid headed our way. Too bad for the earth. After watching these misfits go through their "astronaut training," it becomes clearly evident that, in their hands, our planet is destined to become asteroid dust.

While a similar movie, Deep Impact, concentrated primarily (and somewhat poorly) on the psychological effects of knowing the world is about to end, Armageddon is all action, following the efforts of our heroic buffoons and the obstacles they encounter as they try to prevent human extinction. They tell jokes too. Lots of jokes. Steve Buscemi (The Big Lebowski) has the lion's share of the witticisms which he delivers with great flair. Mr. Willis, Ben Affleck (Good Will Hunting), and Peter Stormare (The Big Lebowski) as a Russian cosmonaut also manage to add a degree of levity to the production. The humor helps to bring welcome relief from the almost continual visual onslaught of explosions, collisions, narrow escapes and pulse pounding action.

There's also time for Mr. Affleck to share some romantic scenes with Liv Tyler (Stealing Beauty) who plays Harry's daughter, Grace.

All in all, Armageddon is infinitely more watchable than the earlier released Deep Impact. The tension runs considerably higher. The plausibility level is just as remote but hey... we're not exactly awarding extra points for accuracy.

Director Michael Bay (The Rock) knows exactly what we want as he keeps the screen filled with effects that are truly extraordinary: meteor showers, oil rig disasters, space station mishaps, and of course, the reason for all this mayhem, an asteroid the size of Texas. (Movie note #112: All global killing asteroids will tend to be the size of Texas.)

While all of this is great fun to watch, it is also completely unfounded by the Scriptures. The movie opens by saying that an asteroid strike millions of years ago is what killed the dinosaurs and that such a strike will inevitably happen again. While such a statement helps to set the stage for the drama (comedy?) to follow, it is simply not true. What killed the dinosaurs is documented in Isaiah 14:12-17 and Revelation 12:7-9. It was not a physical force that decimated the world, but rather a spiritual force. Not only will such an event not repeat itself, God assures us that the world shall endure. The tribulations described in the book of Revelation among others will not occur until after Christ returns for his church, many whom will remain alive to see that day come. Since that day has not yet arrived, human life and the earth upon which it resides shall continue. That is a promise of our God.

What happens to the earth after Christ comes to bring us to our Heavenly Father is anybody's guess. With God's people gathered together in the heavens, the entire world will be encased in spiritual darkness. The devil will have free rein to wreak his havoc. He could have asteroids hit the earth that will make Texas look like Dubuque. But we won't be there to experience it. We will be with our Lord in the air, starting the eternal journey which we have been promised by the One Who never lies. While unbelievers will be facing a literal Die Hard, we shall live forever in the presence of our Lord.

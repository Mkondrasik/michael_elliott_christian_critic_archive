Tarzan

Author-verified transcription

America, beware... Jungle Fever awaits you. Disney's animated Tarzan swings into action from its opening moments and keeps its momentum throughout. This 48th filmed rendition of Edgar Rice Burroughs' popular tale is indeed a memorable one.

Here is a Tarzan who moves as we have only imagined him. Not bound by the conventional limitations of physics, gravity, or human capabilities, the Disney animators allow this ape-man to perform dazzling superhuman feats. Thankfully, there is a strong story to support such wonderful artistry.

The movie opens with Phil Collins singing "Two Worlds" which beautifully underscores the nonverbal background story. By the time the song ends, Tarzan's human parents are dead, killed by a leopard. A baby gorilla is also dead, thanks to the same leopard. Kala (voice by Glenn Close), the mother of the slain baby gorilla, saves Tarzan from the great cat and decides to raise him as her own.

This decision is met with a great deal of skepticism from Kerchak (voice by Lance Henriksen), her "husband" and leader of the gorilla herd. Although he relents and allows the hairless baby to remain, he refuses to accept it into his family.

As Tarzan (voice by Tony Goldwyn) grows, this becomes the driving motivation for him to excel. He plans to become "the best ape ever" in order to win Kerchak's approval and love. During his formative years, his friends included Tantor (voice by Wayne Knight), a cowardly elephant and Terk (voice by Rosie O'Donnell), a smart-aleck ape.

Tarzan's life undergoes a change once he gets his first glimpse of man. Professor Porter (voice by Nigel Hawthorne), his daughter Jane (voice by Minnie Driver), and their safari guide Clayton (voice by Brian Blessed) are visiting Africa in hopes to see gorillas, of which the good professor is something of an unqualified expert.

Knowing there are others who look like him, Tarzan must now choose between the "family" who raised him and the species to which he belongs.

The animation and artwork of this film is spectacular. The lushness of the forest, the rotating, ever-changing perspective, and the motion given to Tarzan's "tree-surfing" method of traveling are all jawdroppingly stupendous.

The vocal talent which give personalities to the animators' artistic renderings are, with only one exception, perfect. Mr. Goldwyn gives to Tarzan the strength, longing, and curiosity needed in such a complex character. Miss Driver deserves whatever awards can be given to a voice. Her Jane is absolutely delightful: humorous, coquettish, bold - she steals every scene in which her character appears.

Rosie O'Donnell as the wisecracking animal sidekick seems out of place. Perhaps if she were saved for the imagined sequel, Tarzan Visits New York, her accent wouldn't have been as glaringly obtrusive.

Another character who seemed out of place, (no fault of the voice) was that of Clayton, the big game hunter. His "villain" was one-dimensional and under-realized. It may seem odd to say, but frankly, he was a bit "cartoonish" for this movie. There was enough of a story here without the need to trivialize it by adding a lame bad guy/good guy showdown. Still, the kiddies should enjoy it - although parents should be advised that there are some dramatically intense moments and depiction of the life and death struggle of jungle life which could upset the very young. Nothing, however, is graphically displayed.

One cannot miss the emphasis on the importance of family in this film. Tarzan becomes the man he is because of the nurturing of his adopted mother and his desire to please his adopted father. As we are spiritually nurtured by those who can teach us and as we strive to please our spiritual Father, we too grow and develop in ways that make us noticeably stronger.

I'm also pleased to say that Disney has kept this story somewhat spiritually clean. Unlike many of their other movies, there's no mysticism, spiritualism, magic, ghosts or goblins to spiritually confuse our youth. Focusing on family love, Disney has made an adventure film over which all ages can go ape.

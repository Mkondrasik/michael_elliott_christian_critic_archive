Exorcist: The Beginning

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 20, 2004
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Acts 13:10James 4:7Ephesians 6:11-17

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Evil; spiritual warfare; resisting the devil; the Word of God

Author-verified transcription

Ask people to name the scariest movie of all time and odds are that the 1973 film based upon William Peter Blatty’s The Exorcist will appear near the top of everyone’s list. This classic tale of a supernatural struggle between good and evil managed to tap into a primal fear that few films have equaled. The sequels which followed certainly didn’t begin to approach the effectiveness of the first. The latest attempt, Exorcist: the Beginning, is no exception.

Swedish actor Stellan Skarsgard stars in the role that his famed countryman Max von Sydow originated: Father Lankester Merrin. In this prequel, set a couple of decades before the first film, Merrin is a deeply conflicted man. Haunted by the past and the horrors of Nazi atrocities he witnessed during WWII, Merrin lost his faith and retired his priest’s collar to become an archeologist.

When an ancient church is unearthed in a remote section of Kenya, Merrin is hired by the Catholic Church to locate and retrieve a relic which is thought to be there. Of course, what he finds is no ordinary archeological discovery. An evil like none other seems to surround the area and places Merrin at the center of a deadly conflict between the British forces and the African natives.

This movie has been widely reported to have been laced with controversy and strife. Paul Schrader (screenwriter of Taxi Driver, Raging Bull, and The Last Temptation of Christ) was initially hired to direct the film. He completed his film and was subsequently fired by the studio because he did not deliver the R-rated blood and gore movie that was expected. Director Renny Harlan (Cutthroat Island, Die Hard 2) was brought in and he allegedly rewrote and reshot up to 90% of the film. It is a pity. To my mind, Schrader’s version would be infinitely more interesting than Harlan’s commercially viable but artistically generic piece of horror schlock.

Skarsgard, as always, does admirable work. As Merrin, he is a bit slow to come to terms with the supernatural events taking place around him but his is an honest portrayal. The climactic showdown between the fallen priest and the fallen archangel is fueled by his fervor and though weakened by Harlan’s use of cheesy CGI-effects, the emotional truth he wields helps to carry the scene.

The film is not recommended for those offended or repulsed by disturbing images of gore and violence and profane language. Harlan has gone out of his way to provide more than enough to warrant the R rating the studio desired for the film.

One of the questions the film poses involves the very nature of evil itself. Is it a separate entity embodied in a spiritual being or is it rather a human condition that is present within each one of us? The answer, as we might expect, is not as simple as being one or the other.

Scriptures testify that there is an evil spiritual force at work in this world which sets itself in direct opposition to God and his truths.

He said, O son of the devil, full of all guile and of all cunning, hostile to all righteousness, will you not stop perverting the right ways of the Lord? Acts 13:10 (KJV)

At the same time, each one of us is subject to the lies which the evil one promotes and so we must actively work to repel his suggestions and temptations and keep evil thoughts and intents from entering our hearts.

As the movie intimates, the power of God manifested by a believing man or woman is the only force able to prevail against the spiritual evil of this world. The Word of God is the primary weapon in our spiritual arsenal. The more Word we know, the better equipped we are to resist the devil so he will flee.

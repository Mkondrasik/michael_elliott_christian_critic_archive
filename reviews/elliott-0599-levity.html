Author-verified transcription

Ed Solomon is best known for scripting such lightweight but popular films as Bill & Ted’s Excellent Adventure, Men in Black, and Charlie’s Angels. In Levity, he explores the opposite end of the spectrum with a slow-moving, humorless, but thoughtful examination on redemption and forgiveness.

Billy Bob Thornton (Monster’s Ball) stars as Manuel Jordan who, as a young teen, shot and killed a teen aged clerk during a convenience store robbery. Convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment, Jordan makes for an unusual convict. He actually agrees with his sentence. From the minute he pulled the trigger, he was filled with remorse, knowing that in that one act he had done something that could never be forgiven.

After serving 22 years behind bars, Jordan is understandably confused and disoriented when the parole board suddenly commutes his sentence to time served. He unexpectedly finds himself a free man. Free from everything except the continual guilt that he feels.

Drawn back to the scene of the crime, Jordan searches for a redemption which he doesn’t believe he deserves from a God he doesn’t believe exists. Through a series of accidental occurrences he begins working for a community center run by an unorthodox preacher (Morgan Freeman, Sum of All Fears). It is through his work there that he meets Sofia Mellenger (Kirsten Dunst, Spider-Man), a party girl so disillusioned with life that she spends most of it too drunk or high to fully experience it.

While not helping at the center, Jordan begins stalking (in a good sense) the sister (Holly Hunter, Moonlight Mile) of the boy he killed. He’s looking for some way to help her, thinking that it might (but probably won’t) atone for his sins.

Like his earlier film, The Man Who Wasn’t There, Thornton almost sleepwalks through this role as if he believes that we’ll interpret his passivity as deep introspection. While he doesn’t distract from the film or its story, I can’t say that his performance adds anything.

Both Kirsten Dunst and Holly Hunter give multi-layered performances that allow us into what their characters must be feeling. Morgan Freeman’s character remains an enigma for too long and so the surprising revelation about him doesn’t register fully.

However, Ed Solomon’s film does hold our interest and while it doesn’t answer the questions it raises, it does provide plenty of conversation starters. What is redemption and how does it work? Are there sins that cannot be forgiven? What is genuine remorse and how does it fit into the big picture? Is incarceration intended to be a punishment or an opportunity to rehabilitate? Where is God in all this?

That latter question is readily knowable. God is everywhere present. Even when Jordan confesses his disbelief, God is still there... waiting... ready and willing to forgive him his sinful past. God always does it better than the rest of us. His heart should be ours. When genuine remorse is demonstrated our response should be one of forgiveness just as God’s is.

If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us [our] sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 John 1:9 (KJV)

It takes practice and a spiritual perception but it is available to have a forgiving heart. It is also available not to live in condemnation, regardless of what has taken place in the past. Being cleansed from all unrighteousness is a phrase that requires no footnoted explanation. God is more concerned about our future than our past. When we, from our heart, ask for forgiveness and desire to change our ways, God will always honor that desire and make it available for us to receive the redemption that has already been paid.

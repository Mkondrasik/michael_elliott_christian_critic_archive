Small Soldiers

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
July 10, 1998
Rating
1 star
Running time
1 hr: 50 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

James 4:1-22 Timothy 2:1-41 John 5:21

Sermon / spiritual topic: Parenting; Media Discernment; Warfare

Author-verified transcription

Parents, beware. There will be no mercy. The impulse will be to take your child to any animated movie that on the surface appears to have been produced with them in mind. What we must remember is that Hollywood does not have our children's best interests at heart. That job belongs to us, the parents.

Small Soldiers is a commercial running continuously for 108 minutes and based on the hype being generated, your child will soon be clamoring to see it. Think twice. This movie will do nothing more than instill a desire in your child's mind for the toys that are being displayed on the big screen. Toys that will disappoint because advertising, packaging and especially this movie can make them look smarter, more fun, and more real than they actually are.

In fact, such is the premise of the movie: Globaltech, a military contractor, has purchased a toy company. The key executive, played by Denis Leary (Suicide Kings), wants to promote a line of toys that will "do what they do on the commercials." A line of toys that will play back with the child. As a result of this vision, the concept of the Commando Elite action figures was born. And since toy heroes require a toy enemy, the alien race of Gorgonites were also developed.

The problem is that the computer chip placed inside of these toys was manufactured by a munitions company. The toys, which can learn and interact, are also capable of plotting the destruction of an entire race of toys, in this case, the Gorgonites and any who may align themselves with them.

Here's where the humans come in. Gregory Smith (Krippendorf's Tribe) plays Alan, the son of a toy store owner. He gets his hands on a case of the first Commando Elite line of toys and the "fun" commences. Kirsten Dunst (Interview With a Vampire) is his puppy love interest who helps him to fight off the toy opposition.

The real fun comes from identifying the voices of the various toy characters. If you see this film, stick around for the credits. You may get a surprise or two.

Just don't get the idea that this is a children's movie. Even though it is being marketed as family fun, and even though the main characters are animated, the violence, and language and the sexual overtones are such to warrant caution. Especially since the characters in the film are sure to be prominently displayed in our toy stores before the first toy-buying holiday.

The real danger is to allow anything, whether cartoon or commentary, to trivialize warfare. Even though "it's just a movie," it gives no appreciation for what really happens in a warlike conflict. The toys that "die" don't even stay dead. They are resurrected in other forms. That simply doesn't happen in real life. Once you die, you're dead. You don't come back as a Suzy Homemaker or as a Frankenstein's monster.

All war originates from the devil spirit realm. The devil is the author of death. And war is an enterprise which accommodates death in great abundance. It is not something that needs to be glorified or exalted. Nor is it something to be summarily dismissed.

It is interesting to note that the sympathetic toy characters are not the American fighting men of the Commando Elite, but rather the odd looking alien creatures known as the Gorgonites. What kind of confusing message is this to send to our children?

I'm sure that director Joe Dante (Gremlins) and team did not set out to intentionally subvert American ideals. Their motivation, judging by their product, was more self-serving. They wanted to make money. Lots of money. Somewhere along the way they must have gotten the impression that making money was the American ideal. I wonder where that idea came from?

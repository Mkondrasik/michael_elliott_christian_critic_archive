Author-verified transcription

The new war film, Flyboys, takes us back to an era where it was still thought that mortal combat contained rules that men of honor and decency followed. It is based on the actions of the Lafayette Escadrille, the WWI squadron of American pilots who volunteered to fly for the French military before their own country entered the fray.

An old-fashioned film about a war from an earlier age is sure to be filled with plenty of clichés. We have the cowboy maverick (James Franco) who has the ability to back up his bravado; the spoiled child of privilege (Tyler Labine) who joins to prove something to his affluent daddy; the black fighter (Abdul Salas) who looks to the skies to escape racism; and the legendary ace (Martin Henderson) who is the sole surviving member of his original squadron.

We also have the religious Bible-reading guy; the guy who is too scared to go back up in the plane after his first mission; the guy who can't seem to hit anything he shoots at; and, lest we forget, the attractive French girl (Jennifer Decker) who nurses our cowboy aviator back to health (physically and romantically.)

But what happens on the ground between aerial missions is the least interesting aspect of the film. The movie excels at depicting the gravity-defying dogfights that these biplanes and tri-wing planes engaged in at close quarters.

Using a combination of live footage and motion capture technology (the first film to apply this process to airplanes), director Tony Bill and his special effects team recreate memorable action scenes that are thrilling to watch.

It is important to remember that in 1916, airplanes were relatively new inventions. They were fragile constructs, patched together with canvas and chicken wire. Even if the pilots could avoid being killed by enemy bullets, it was still possible that the plane would do them in. The new recruits arriving to the French aerodrome were promptly informed that their life expectancy would be measured in weeks, not years.

We follow these young boys, each there for their own, often selfish, reasons as they develop into men whose lives are dependent upon their skills and the skills of those who fly with them. The camaraderie and brotherly love they share and exhibit may be a movie cliché but clichés exist because they generally hold universal truths.

While war should never be glamorized or idealized, there are elements to war that can be seen as beneficial. The bonds that are forged by men who are forced to rely upon one another in time of war are as strong as any they'll form during their lifetime. In war, men see the sacrifice of their brothers-in-arms; sacrifices that often result in the saving of lives.

It is the ultimate expression of love; and love - unselfish, undeserved, freely given love - is the strongest bond there is. It is stronger than hate; stronger than death; stronger than revenge; stronger than fear; stronger than war. Love, as cliché as it may sound, conquers all.

Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony. Colossians 3:14 (KJV)

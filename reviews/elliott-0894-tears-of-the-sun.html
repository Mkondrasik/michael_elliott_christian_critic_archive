Author-verified transcription

"The only thing necessary for the triumph of evil is for good men to do nothing." This quote from the 18th century British statesman and philosopher Edmund Burke flashes on the screen at the end of Tears of the Sun and does a nice job of summing up that which we have just witnessed. What begins as a typical war rescue adventure film eventually morphs into an exploration of matters dealing with basic humanity and compassion.

Navy SEAL Lieutenant Waters (Bruce Willis, Unbreakable) has a job to do. The democratic regime in Nigeria has been overrun by rebel forces commanded by a ruthless despot with a penchant for genocide. An American doctor (Monica Bellucci, The Brotherhood of the Wolf), working at a Nigerian mission providing humanitarian aid, is in danger as the Muslim rebels sweep across the country on a murderous rampage against any and all Christians. Waters and his elite team have been assigned the task of locating and extracting the good doctor without engaging the enemy.

There's one small catch. The doctor won't go unless her people, about 70 of them, are extracted with her. She knows what will become of them if they are left behind to face the rebels alone. She opts to put their safety above her own.

In order to complete his mission, Waters must comply with her wishes even though they contradict his orders. He begins to lead this human caravan through the dense Nigerian jungle en route to the Cameroon border. But they are inexplicably and relentlessly being chased by a large portion of the rebel army. As he and his men see firsthand the atrocities committed by the rebel forces, their mission takes on a more personal nature. The SEALs begin to realize that to remain "disengaged" in the face of such evil would allow that evil to prevail.

Bruce Willis gives a tightly controlled performance as the leader of the SEALs whose training and sense of duty cross paths with his personal sense of right and wrong. It is an internal battle as real as the external ones he faces and Willis conveys it to us very effectively. The actors cast as his team members establish an ensemble style which helps to express the camaraderie that exists among men whose lives often depend upon one another.

Monica Bellucci plays an impassioned do-gooder whose genuine affection for the people she is helping registers very clearly. It is a nicely balanced performance with enough emotional power to offset the stern and sober persona of Willis' character.

Director Antoine Fuqua (Training Day) does a fine job in showing us both the horrors of war as well as the caliber of men who willingly wage it for all the right reasons. It is a violent film, as any film honestly portraying war should be, but the violence is not gratuitous nor does the film delight in showing us how expertly their makeup artists can replicate the bloody aftermath of a fire fight.

Where The Quiet American was critical of US involvement in the affairs of other nations, Fuqua's film examines the opposite side of that coin. However, Tears of the Sun is not interested in exploring the political aspects of US foreign policy. The questions it asks are more personal, dealing with the ethical and moral consequences of inaction. It asks us to consider that some things go beyond politics and governments. Some things are just wrong on the most basic of human levels and if we have the ability to stop them, we should... or we should at least try. Not to do so would also be wrong.

Defend the poor and fatherless: do justice to the afflicted and needy. Deliver the poor and needy: rid them out of the hand of the wicked. Psalms 82:3-4 (KJV)

The words of Edmund Burke have never been truer or more relevant. God's Word says "Resist the devil and he will flee." Conversely, if no resistance is offered, the evil will stay. When we have the power to resist, do we not also have the moral obligation to do so?

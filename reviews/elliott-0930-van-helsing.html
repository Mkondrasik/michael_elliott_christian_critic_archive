Author-verified transcription

The summer season is upon us. This means that the movies will be getting bigger, bolder, and much more expensive. First out of the gate is Universal Pictures' return to monster movies - Van Helsing.

With a budget rumored to be as high as 140 million dollars, Van Helsing is an action-packed, special effects laden adventure boasting not just one classic movie villain but four. The money is certainly evident on the screen but unfortunately for the filmmakers, money is not the only ingredient to making a good motion picture.

The movie opens with black and white footage depicting the torch-carrying villagers of 1887 Transylvania storming Dr. Frankenstein's castle just as he brings his monster (Shuler Hensley) to life. What we learn in that scene is that Frankenstein's experiments were financed by none other than Count Dracula (Richard Roxburgh) who has his own reasons for wanting the monster to live.

The movie then jumps ahead a year to introduce us to Gabriel Van Helsing (Hugh Jackman), a mysterious agent for a secretive organization within the Vatican. After tracking down and dispatching the evil (and gargantuan) Mr. Hyde (Robbie Coltrane), Van Helsing is sent to Transylvania to rid the world once and for all of Dracula. But how do you kill a creature that is already dead?

He is accompanied by Carl (David Wenham), a friar who doubles as a kind of medieval Q to Van Helsing's James Bond. In Transylvania, they team up with Anna Valerious (Kate Beckinsale), the last of a long family line that has been devoted to a century's old vow to kill the evil count. They find that in order to get to the vampire, they must first get past the werewolves who seem to do his bidding.

There's a lot of high-priced activity on the screen but the film suffers from two major factors. First, Sommers tries to adopt the same campy humor that was sprinkled atop his two Mummy pictures. The problem is that he goes too far and pushes the entire tone of the film off kilter. Secondly, the film is hampered by the overplaying of Richard Roxburgh as the central villain. He takes his characterization way over the top - to the point that it often looks like he's been directed to appear in a totally different film.

Sommers does have some fun with the monster myths and legends which have accumulated via the many films and stories developed over the years. Silver bullets, wooden stakes, holy water, crucifixes, sunlight, and more are used with varying degrees of effectiveness.

There is a considerable amount of computer enhanced special effects resulting in a dazzling display of artistic expertise but not so much that it can hold our interest for the nearly 150 minute running time.

Of all the characters on screen, the most appealing is the Frankenstein monster. Created for evil purposes but not bound to evil intents, his personification is the most human. Most of the characters in the film want to see the monster dead because they fear him and what he might do. Van Helsing refuses to kill him because he sees no evil in the monster. The monster's sole desire is to be allowed to exist... to live... while causing no harm to anyone.

Many times, we force a judgment onto somebody based upon our fears or prejudices. We are wrong to do so. We should not judge a person based upon where they have come from because we may have come from worse situations. What should concern us is not where a person has been or even where he is. The question which we should want to know is where does this person want to go with his life and if it is a goal that is pleasing to God, how can we help him to find the way?

Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment. John 7:24 (KJV)

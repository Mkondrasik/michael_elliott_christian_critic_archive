Author-verified transcription

Sometimes a guy just wants to see a naked lady. That seems to be the message delivered by Mrs. Henderson Presents, a true story (or true enough) about an English widow, a dilapidated theater, and the beauty of the female form.

Judi Dench is that widow, a woman of independent means who is looking for a life’s purpose now that her husband has passed on. She stumbles across The Windmill, a dilapidated theater for sale, and decides to make it her new hobby.

She hires Vivian Van Damm, (Bob Hoskins) an out of work director, to be her theater manager. He insists on having total artistic control which sets up the relationship dynamic that deliciously drives the movie.

His idea is to run a non-stop musical review called Revuedeville. It is an idea that works well enough... until the other theaters in town begin copying it. It is her idea that saves The Windmill from closing - have the performers work without costumes... or clothes of any kind.

This is England after all, a staid and proper little country where this kind of thing just isn’t done. She gets begrudging permission from Lord Chamberlain (Christopher Guest) after making the argument that many pieces of great art contains nudes. He agrees but, with the rejoinder that art doesn’t move, his one stipulation is that The Windmill girls must remain stationary while disrobed.

Van Damm makes it work, building musical numbers around a tableau of posed girls in various states of undress. It was very artful, and quite daring for its place and time. It was an instant success and became even more popular once the bombs started falling during WWII and The Windmill became the only theater on the West End to remain open.

The nudity in Mrs. Henderson Presents is tastefully depicted and artfully done. It is rated R because of the nudity but it is not a typical R rated film. Director Stephen Frears (Dirty Pretty Things) takes the high road, as Van Damm must have done in the 30s and 40s, presenting a charming and entertaining film.

The main attraction is the odd relationship that forms between Van Damm and Henderson. Bickering like an old married couple about the direction the theater is going, it is clear that they both enjoy butting heads. Dench and Hoskins are brilliant together and combine to make the film better than its script.

Frears does take a few wrong turns by following a few melodramatic subplots that take away from the overall tone of his film. But when he stays focused on the theatrical productions and his two central performers, he is right on the money.

Van Damm treated all his performers with respect and propriety. He saw them as artists and so, this is how they saw themselves. When a disparaging critic snidely comments that it is hardly difficult - all they have to do is stand there... one of the featured girls smiles and agrees. A few scenes later, we see just how difficult it can be to “just stand there.” Standing in the face of adversity is the bravest and boldest thing one can do.

Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand. Ephesians 6:13 (KJV)

God’s armour is described in the following verses: the loincloth of truth; the breastplate of righteousness; feet shod with the preparation of the gospel of peace; the shield of faith; the helmet of salvation; and the sword of the Spirit which is the word of God. So armed, we can stand against any enemy, clothes or no clothes.

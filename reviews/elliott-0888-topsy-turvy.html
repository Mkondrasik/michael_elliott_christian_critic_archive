Author-verified transcription

"The king of topsy-turvydom" is how a critic once described the literary styling of W. S. Gilbert who, with Sir Arthur Sullivan, wrote some of the most beloved comic operas in the English language. Writer/Director Mike Leigh (Secrets & Lies) now offers us an in depth look at their collaborative efforts in his new film, Topsy Turvy.

The year is 1884. Gilbert and Sullivan are the sweethearts of the English stage, having already triumphed with HMS Pinafore and The Pirates of Penzance. But signs of discontent are brewing. Following the lukewarm critical reception of their latest opera, Princess Ida, Sullivan announces that he can no longer continue working on these "trivial soufflés," instead desiring to lead his music down a more seriously minded path. In so doing, he rejects Gilbert's newest idea for a libretto as repetitive flippancy, a rehash of their previous work which he believes is killing his growth or development as a composer.

At an impasse, the future of their famed partnership indeed looks bleak... until Gilbert attends a Japanese exhibition. Experiencing a new culture gives him the inspiration to write The Mikado, an idea which Sullivan readily embraces.

The rest of the film deals with the details of mounting a theatrical production in the 19th century. Mr. Leigh takes us backstage, as it were, involving us in the collaborative effort known as theater.

For those who appreciate the art and scenic designs of a historical film set in the Victorian era; or for those who share a love of the theater and enjoy seeing the machinations of a work in progress; or for the rabid Gilbert & Sullivan fans, Topsy Turvy will be a delightfully satisfying treat. However, anyone not falling into one of those three categories will more than likely find themselves engaged in a three hour endurance contest.

It is not that the film is bad. Obviously, great care has been taken in the making of the film. In fact, so much detail has been provided that Mr. Leigh oftentimes does not take the time to explain or elaborate on what we are seeing. Whether it be Gilbert's relationship with his wife, or the various personal foibles of the members of the company, we see just enough to get interested in learning more, only to move onto something else, never to return again.

Jim Broadbent (Little Voice) makes a wonderfully proper authoritative English gentleman. As W. S. Gilbert, he remains staid and conservative, both in public as well as in his personal relationships, yet it is his character who is responsible for most of the drollness on the screen. A scene which shows him meticulously directing three of his actors (in a five person scene) is especially amusing.

Allan Cordurner, (The Imposters) as the second half of the famous duo, compliments Mr. Broadbent, almost as well as Sullivan complimented Gilbert. Of smaller stature, but with a greater appetite for life's experiences, Mr. Cordurner's portrayal of Sullivan appropriately shows the great contrast between the two dissimilar partners. The one thing they did share was the passion for their respective crafts and the tremendous discipline needed to shape it to their artistic vision.

It is seeing this type of discipline which reminds us of the exhortation of God. Whether correcting an actress on the proper pronunciation of "corroborative" or reproving a musician for coming in a half beat too late, both Gilbert & Sullivan were perfectionists in their fields.

If natural man is able to hone his abilities to such a level of excellence, shouldn't the spiritual man be able to exhibit the same discipline concerning the spiritual matters of God?

Know ye not that they which run in a race run all, but one receiveth the prize? So run, that ye may obtain. And every man that striveth for the mastery is temperate [self-controlled] in all things. Now they do it to obtain a corruptible crown; but we an incorruptible. 1 Corinthians 9:24-25 [KJV]

At the end of our race is eternal life, the knowledge of God, and the heavenly rewards of the future... something which will be infinitely better than a good review.

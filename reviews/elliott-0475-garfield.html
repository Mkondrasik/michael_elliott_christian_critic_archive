Author-verified transcription

There's no false advertising to be found in the trailer for the summer comedy Garfield: The Movie. The trailer leaves us with the strong and unmistakable impression that the film is going to be a boring, unfaithful, and unfunny bastardization of the popular comic strip by Jon Davis. That's exactly what we get.

As devoted comic strip readers know, Garfield is the orange pet of Jon Arbuckle, a hapless but lovable loser. Fat, lazy, and sardonic, the lasagna loving Garfield is never at a loss for a humorous excuse or rationalization for his self- centered behavior.

Unfortunately for everyone concerned, the film's screenwriters (Joel Cohen and Alec Sokolow) don't capture the familiar comic strip character in their writing. This Garfield has been neutered (figuratively speaking). He may look familiar but it is clear that something is missing.

That indescribable something is what has made this fat cat such a popular and beloved character and its absence sinks this film right from the start. The plot is hardly original, being oddly reminiscent of Toy Story (and no doubt a number of other films before it). Garfield, jealous of Jon's new pet dog Odie, is responsible for the canine's eventual disappearance. Out of guilt and self-preservation, Garfield decides to leave the safety of his cul-de-sac in order to embark on a quest to rescue Odie and redeem himself in the eyes of Jon and the other neighborhood animals.

Director Peter Hewitt (The Borrowers) makes the ill-advised decision to computer-animate the central character while surrounding him with live action supporting characters. The mix does not work. We are never drawn into this hybrid world and are instead reminded as to how false and forced it is.

Bill Murray provides the voice for Garfield and while he remains one of the funniest comedic actors working today, much of his humor is visual. He's out of his element and is unable to fashion a memorable character of this cat using just his vocal skills.

Breckin Meyer plays Jon as a bland nice guy who's a bit shy around girls. It is not the most interesting choice that could have been made and it is far removed from the exaggerated dorkiness of his comic book counterpart. Jennifer Love Hewitt, as the short-skirted vet and recipient of Jon's unexpressed affection, has little to do and less to wear.

Stephen Tobolowsky plays the villain, a local TV personality who steals Odie in hopes to use him to further his career, with cartoon-like zeal but he lacks the foundation that would come with a fully realized character.

Of course, Garfield himself could be the poster child for selfishness and laziness - two very undesirable traits. The filmmakers did try to paint a nobler picture by showing that his decisions lead to consequences thus forcing him to think and care about someone else (Odie). By doing so, the film gives its CGI star a positive character arc.

Learning to give, share and include others into one's life is part of being alive. There's nothing wrong with looking out for number one. It is just that we shouldn't stop there. We should never act as if the other numbers don't exist or worse - that they don't matter.

But as touching brotherly love ye need not that 1 write unto you: for ye yourselves are taught of God to love one another. 1 Thessalonians 4:9 (KJV)

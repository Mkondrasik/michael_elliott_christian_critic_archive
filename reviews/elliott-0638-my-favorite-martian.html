Author-verified transcription

The problem with Disney's new remake of My Favorite Martian, which ran as a popular TV show in the sixties starring Ray Walston and the late Bill Bixby, is simply that the filmmakers never properly identified the target audience for whom they were making the film.

Far too silly and sophomoric for adults; way too much sexual innuendo and locker-room humor for children. End result: Disney has made a film that will dissatisfy just about everybody.

The basic plot is borrowed from the original series - TV Producer Tim O'Hara witnesses the crash of a UFO. The Martian on board goes home with Tim, where he pretends to be his Uncle Martin so he can keep his presence on earth a secret until his ship can be fixed and he can return to Mars.

Throw in two love interests for Tim, a chase scene through sewer pipes where a miniaturized car out races a Roto Rooter snake, and an anthropomorphic space suit and you've pretty much got the movie.

Jeff Daniels (Pleasantville) is appropriately affable as Tim. It is his goofy, good-natured quality that earns My Favorite Martian its one star. His performance is just about the only redeeming feature of this movie.

Christopher Lloyd is merely a mass of unfocused energy as Martin. Playing a derivative of his Back To The Future Doc Brown character (without the humor and originality), Mr. Lloyd quickly becomes tiresome to watch.

Director Donald Petrie (Grumpy Old Men) uses technologically advanced special effects that is meant to appeal to the younger audience, but again... with all the gratuitous sexual inferences, perhaps that audience shouldn't be there in the first place.

My Favorite Martian was written by Sherri Stoner and Deanna Oliver who last teamed up on the 1995 screenplay for Casper. As with that film, they rely heavily on the special effects without giving much thought to character development. The effects featured in the film include Martin's need to continue to chew his “Martian gum” in order to maintain his human form. Of course Elizabeth Hurley, Daryl Hannah and Jeff Daniels all have an opportunity to chew the gum so we can see them morph into different alien creatures. Also included is a miniaturizing device which leads to that car chase through the sewers; and a Martian space suit that literally has a life of its own.

The wisecracking Zoot (the Suit), whose voice is provided by an uncredited Wayne Knight (Newman of Seinfeld) is the source of much of the inappropriate humor. Whether drinking a detergent based martini while being “stimulated” by the agitation cycle of a washing machine or making lustful overtures on a sun dress in a clothing store, this suit should have been re-tailored before it was included in a so-called family film.

While seeing a uninhabited space suit dance, sing, moon the camera, or save the day might be construed (in some cultures) as a newfangled form of entertainment, the idea of attributing human characteristics to inanimate objects is hardly original. Psalms 115:4-8 logically shows us the lunacy of idolatry... of attributing power or glory to an object which holds neither.

Their idols are silver and gold, the work of men's hands. They have mouths, but they speak not: eyes have they, but they see not: They have ears, but they hear not: noses have they, but they smell not: They have hands, but they handle not: feet have they, but they walk not: neither speak they through their throat. They that make them are like unto them; so is every one that trusteth in them. Psalms 115:4-8 [KJV]

Idols are fashioned by man to be worshipped by man. In Old Testament times, we gave them human or animal forms, carved in wood or molded in metal. Today's idols are more subtle. They take the forms of careers... money... fame... Those things are still fashioned by men and are still worshipped by men. In the end, they have no more lasting power than the idols of old. There is only one power who prevails over all. Only one power who can bring forth our deliverance. That power belongs to God.

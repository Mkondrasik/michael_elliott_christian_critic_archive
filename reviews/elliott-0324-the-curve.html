Author-verified transcription

Within the first ten minutes of The Curve, a student character asks a counselor to list the warning signs of suicidal tendencies. She rattles off a few... spending a lot of time alone, listening to depressing music, watching videos by first time directors...

First time director Dan Rosen includes the back handed jibe at himself in an obvious attempt at the same tongue-in-cheek, inside-joke hipness that was so effective in Scream. Disappointingly, the key word in the above sentence is "obvious." To make a mystery/thriller that works, it helps to throw in a few unexpected twists. The Curve has more twists than a Dairy Queen soft ice cream cone, but none of them come as a surprise.

Tim, Chris, and Rand are college roommates with their sights set on graduate school. When less than perfect grades look to ruin their vision of attending Harvard, the urban myth of the "suicide clause" begins to sound appealing. Supposedly, if a roommate commits suicide during the school year, the surviving roommates are given an automatic 4.0 for that semester enabling them to deal with the grief and loss without the added pressure of class assignments.

Chris, who desperately wants to attend Harvard Business School, allows Tim to talk him into a plan which includes killing Rand by throwing him off of a nearby cliff. But when authorities can't find the body, their 4.0 is in jeopardy and their sanity isn't too far behind.

This imbecilic plot is impossible to take seriously. It is not helped by the fact that Matthew Lillard (Scream) plays Tim as an overly loud and obnoxious psychopath from the very beginning. It is impossible to believe anyone remaining roommates with this guy, let alone agreeing to be his accomplice in murder.

Michael Vartan (It Had To Be You), as the seeming pawn around which this unseemly plot revolves, moodily underplays his role appropriately. As a result, he fares better than his two male co-stars. Randall Batinkoff (As Good As It Gets) plays Rand with an unexplained surliness.

The females of the cast don't improve matters much. The best of the lot is Keri Russell (TV's Felicity) who at least doesn't embarrass herself although the script doesn't help her. Tamara Craig Thomas (Tromeo and Juliet) does the best she can in the role of Rand's abused girlfriend and Keri's roommate. Dana Delany (Housesitter) is simply wasted in her role of school counselor.

There is nothing redeeming in this plot involving murder and suicide. Scriptures say that the devil holds the power of death (Hebrews 2:14), so we know the spiritual source of such deeds.

I find this "urban myth" idea of bestowing a 4.0 to surviving roommates intriguing. It is the kind of liberal but mindless act that gives the appearance of compassion but in truth encourages only weakness that we can and do find in certain sectors of our society.

While the practice of the "suicide clause" is fictitious (one hopes), the logic behind it can be seen in other areas of life. Social programs such as welfare, it can be argued, while helping some in genuine need, has also done much to raise a generation expecting to receive something in exchange for nothing.

One of the fundamental principles in the Word of God is that a person will reap what he sows.

Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap. Galatians 6:7 [KJV]

In other words, as a person works, he will receive the fruits of his labor. To teach a person that he can benefit and excel without exertion or application of effort, is to teach a person a lie.

There is only one true way to get a 4.0 and that is to first study, and then apply the knowledge one receives as a result of that study. This is why God tells us:

Study to show thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 Timothy 2:15 [KJV]

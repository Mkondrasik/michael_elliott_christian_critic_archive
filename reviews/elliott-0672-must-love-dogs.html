Author-verified transcription

Writing a screenplay for a successful romantic comedy has got to be as difficult as writing a successful personal ad. After all, what can you say that hasn't been said before? The correct answer, as writer/director Gary David Goldberg well knows, is nothing. The trick is not in saying something new - it is in saying something old while, at the same time, finding a way to keep it fresh.

He succeeds with his newest venture - Must Love Dogs. It is a charming, old-fashioned romantic comedy with enough modern touches to strike a chord with today's audiences.

Diane Lane stars as Sarah, a divorced 40-something teacher who is more than a little bit gun shy when it comes to diving back into the dating pool. Her sister Carol (Elizabeth Perkins) gives her an unsolicited hand by posting her profile on an Internet dating site. Sarah, like it or not, suddenly has offers - lots of them.

One is coming from Jake (John Cusack), a philosophic boat builder who is, like Sarah, recovering from a relationship gone sour. Their first date is far from perfect but shows enough promise that leaves them both thinking about a future meeting.

In between those meetings, Sarah must juggle all the other men who keep getting thrown at her including, in one humorous blind date, her own father (Christopher Plummer).

Goldberg's screenplay, based upon the novel by Claire Cook, is smart and filled with snappy dialogue which the talented cast gleefully embraces. The interchange between characters is fast, offhand, and delightfully charming.

Diane Lane headlines the picture and comfortably leads her costars through a low key and almost breezy approach to the material. Her visual "takes" or reactions to the curve balls that life keeps throwing at her character are very humorous and endearing.

It has been reported that John Cusack rewrote the lion's share of his dialogue to better suit him. True or not, the words fit him well, serving him up as a poetic but overly intense soul.

In a cast filled with an abundance of talent, Elizabeth Perkins, as Sarah's older sister, shines. Adopting an attitude that only a sibling can appreciate, she stays in the shadows of a scene but manages to make her presence known by deliciously tossing her lines out casually which increases their humorous punch tenfold.

Goldberg, best known for his TV sitcom writing, helms the film capably by keeping the cast skipping along the surface of the relationship waters. There's nothing too deep going on here and Goldberg knows it. What's on the surface is where the fun is.

The fun, of course, is in watching the comedic drama that is otherwise known as dating. It hasn't changed much over the years. The methodology may be different, what with all of our technological advances, but when you come right down to it, we're left to answer the same age old question: Is the person you are dating someone whom you would choose to be with for the rest of your life?

It's not really a question of chemistry or a magic combination of emotional feelings and lustful desires. Love is and always will be a choice that we make. How we arrive at that choice differs with each individual. But the decision to love exists within each one of us and is ours alone to make.

For this is the message that ye heard from the beginning, that we should love one another. 1 John 3:11 (KJV)

If love was not a choice, the quoted scripture could not have been written. We should love. It is God's will that we do. But he has left the decision to us.

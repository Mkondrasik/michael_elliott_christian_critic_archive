The Brothers Grimm

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 26, 2005
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 58 min
Source
Published Web Version

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Superstition; fear; deception; truth; exploitation

Author-verified transcription

Long before Barney, Bert and Ernie came upon the scene, parents used to entertain their children with Grimm's Fairy Tales. Grim was an apt description. When we stop to think about it, many of them contained horrifyingly macabre elements.

In The Brothers Grimm, director Terry Gilliam and screenwriter Ehren Kruger take a promising concept and ruin it with a disappointing hodgepodge of styles. Borrowing familiar childhood tales (Hansel and Gretel; Little Red Riding Hood; and Sleeping Beauty among others) and giving them a quasi-historical origin seems like an idea ripe with possibilities. The problem is that by the time the film has gotten to us, ripe has turned rotten.

Wilhelm (Matt Damon) and Jacob (Heath Ledger) Grimm are a pair of brothers renowned throughout the 19th century land for their ability to purge communities of curses and witchcraft that have plagued them. What the simple townsfolk don't know is that the Grimm Brothers are manufacturing the curses - elaborately exploiting the superstitions of the time for their own personal gain.

It is when they run up against real supernatural forces that they find themselves in uncharted waters. Children have been disappearing from a small village and the military leader (Jonathan Pryce) of the French occupying army wants the Grimm Brothers to find out who (or what) is behind it.

Matt Damon, as the more pragmatic of the two brothers, gives an under-the-radar, traditional Hollywood leading man performance. Bland, bland, bland. Heath Ledger, as the more sensitive, artistic Grimm, takes more of a risk. His features are as disguised as his mannerisms. He's full of twitches, halting speech and strained inflections. It's an impressive job of acting. Too bad it doesn't really work in this film.

Gilliam, known in the industry for his excessiveness, fails to give his film the confines that would provide it with its shape and direction. It is a sprawling mess of a movie, exemplified by the over-the-top performance of Peter Stormare. His role of a sadistically stupid French/Italian officer is all over the map and so irritatingly bad that one wonders why Gilliam chose not to remove it from the film altogether.

The Brothers Grimm, rated PG-13, is too violent and bizarre for children; too poorly executed for adults. The only audience for it may be the die-hard Gilliam fans who will want to see it out of appreciation for his excessive style. Somehow, I think they too will be disappointed.

The Grimm Brothers travel the land and make their fortune by recognizing and exploiting the fears and superstitions of others. Contrast that with the way the apostles of the first century church responded when they faced a similar situation:

Then Paul stood in the midst of Mars' hill, and said, Ye men of Athens, I perceive that in all things ye are too superstitious. Acts 17:22 (KJV)

Instead of capitalizing on the ignorance of others, Paul confronted it and offered to teach them how to rid themselves of the fears and superstitions that were keeping them bound.

Superstitions continue to abound in our day and time. And disreputable people continue to make money by exploiting them. Just by speaking and living the truth can we expose the charlatans for what they are.

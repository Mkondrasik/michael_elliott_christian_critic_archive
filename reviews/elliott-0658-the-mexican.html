Author-verified transcription

There are films which run for 3 or 3½ hours which nevertheless seem to pass in the blink of an eye. The Mexican takes the opposite approach. Its two hour playing time feels more like three. That doesn't necessarily mean it's a bad film... just that there's so much going on we feel we're in the theater for a long time.

Brad Pitt (Fight Club) stars as Jerry Welbach, a decent, well-meaning guy who isn't quite the brightest bulb in the pack. Because of a coincidental accident, Jerry finds himself working for the mob. Though his five years of "servitude" is supposedly over, his imprisoned boss requires of him one last job. He must go to Mexico and retrieve an ancient pistol known as "The Mexican."

This news does not sit well with Samantha, his high maintenance girlfriend played by Julia Roberts (Erin Brockovich), who throws him out of the apartment and threatens to leave him if he reneges on his promise to extract himself from his criminal commitments. Faced with death if he doesn't go and a cold shoulder if he does, Jerry leaves for Mexico and the troubles begin.

With so many people interested in acquiring this pistol and concerned that Jerry might get ideas of his own, the mob hires Leroy (James Gandolfini, The Sopranos) to kidnap Samantha thus ensuring Jerry's cooperation.

The movie continues, tracking simultaneously with a number of story lines, some humorous... some romantic. We follow Jerry and his misadventures while finding, losing, finding, losing, and finding the cursed gun. We also follow the unusual relationship being formed between Leroy and Samantha as they exchange their opinions and experiences regarding love and relationships. A third story line explains the history of the gun in a series of "flashbacks" which helps to build a contextual theme in which to view all these stories.

Brad Pitt, in one of his most enjoyable performances, plays Jerry as a good-looking dweeb. Just a little short of being smart, he makes up for it with a charming if clumsy naiveté. Julia Roberts plays Samantha as a high-strung, highly emotional woman who gets her vocabulary and unusual viewpoints from a long line of therapy sessions. James Gandolfini acts as the calming influence between these two energetically emotional characters in his role as the sympathetic hit man, Leroy.

J.H. Wyman's (Mr. Rice's Secret) script includes so many plot twists and turns that it would be easy to get lost but the direction by Gore Verbinski (Mouse Hunt) is competent enough that we are able, for the most part, to follow the action without too much confusion.

Be advised, the film's R rating is well deserved as the film contains a great deal of profanity, violence and sexual situations, including a homosexual subplot involving one of the major characters.

Though essentially an occasionally humorous crime drama, The Mexican is about relationships, especially ones that are in danger of failing. The question that repeatedly gets asked and answered during the course of the movie is: "When two people really love each other, but cannot seem to make their relationship work, at what point do they stop trying?" The answer the movie provides is: "never."

It is an answer that can be supported by scripture:

Love bears up under anything and everything that comes, is ever ready to believe the best of every person. Its hopes are fadeless under all circumstances, and it endures everything (without weakening). Love never fails (never fades out or becomes obsolete or comes to an end). 1 Corinthians 13:7-8 (Amplified)

Perfect love knows no limitations. While it is a rare individual who rises to the level of manifesting this kind of perfect love, it should be the goal of every man, woman, and child. We know that it is available for us to achieve because God, who is love, dwelleth within us, making all things possible.

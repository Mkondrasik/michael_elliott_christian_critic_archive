Adaptation

Michael Elliott

December 6, 2002

3 stars

Original Author Copy

Author-verified transcription

Adaptation may be the most self-aware screenplay ever to have been penned by a fat, bald, and repulsive writer. (That's his oft-repeated description of himself, not mine.) If nothing else, the film gives further credence to the widely held opinion that Charlie Kaufman (Being John Malkovich) is one of the most inventive (and, if he is to believed, most insecure) voices working in Hollywood today.

Whether it is the most ingenious face-saving moves in movie history or simply the product of a facile imagination, Adaptation investigates the so-called creative process while getting in its fair share of jabs directed towards the film industry at large.

Following the success of his outlandish screenplay for Being John Malkovich, Charlie Kaufman was hired to write the screen adaptation for The Orchid Thief. This nonfiction book, written by Susan Orleans, is a kind of rambling account with an odd central character, very little narrative, and a lot of philosophical musings about flowers. It's a book which proves impossible to adapt. So what is Kaufman to do?

How about write a movie about how difficult it is to write a movie about The Orchid Thief? Inserting himself as the lead character is a stoke of maniacal genius and Kaufman does it twice, by also concocting a role for his fictional screenwriting twin brother, Donald, who serves as a counterpoint for some of Charlie's idiosyncratic ways.

Where Charlie is a struggling artist, Donald is an opportunistic hack. Where Charlie agonizes over each word in his screenplay, Donald spews out words with thoughtless abandon. Of course, we all know whose film is going to be most embraced by the producers.

Nicholas Cage is wonderful in his dual role as the Kaufman brothers. Delightfully clueless as Donald and hopelessly neurotic as Charlie, Cage manages to portray two very distinct and different characters simultaneously. They are similar but they are not the same.

Meryl Streep (The Hours) plays author Susan Orleans (at least Kaufman's interpretation of her) as a frustrated, unhappy woman who finds, in Florida nurseryman John Larouche (Chris Cooper, American Beauty), not only the subject of a magazine article and inspiration for her book, but also the passion she feels is missing from her life.

The acting is excellent across the board and director Spike Jonze does a competent job in bringing the story to visual life. But the focus remains (deservedly so) upon Kaufman's screenplay and the unusual way it has of both deconstructing and constructing simultaneously.

Much has been already written about the disappointing third act, but I found it to be a bold and brave statement. Kaufman makes a concession to his artistic integrity which fails in comparison to the rest of his film but by failing, it succeeds brilliantly. The irony will undoubtedly be lost on some but for those who get the joke, it will be impossible not to appreciate what Kaufman has done.

As one of the character says in the film, "Adaptation is a profound process," and indeed it is. The act of changing in order to thrive and prosper in the midst of one's environment is something that is commonplace in all of nature. It is no less necessary for us. As natural men and women, we become accustomed to a certain way of behavior. When we are filled with God's spirit and a new awareness and understanding is become known to us, we must learn to adapt or change if we are to thrive and prosper as spiritual men and women. Our "old man" or natural man ways will not cause us to succeed as God's people on this earth.

That ye put off concerning the former conversation the old man, which is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts; And be renewed in the spirit of your mind. Ephesians 4:22-23 (KJV)

If we ever compromise our spiritual integrity by conforming to the world's expectations or desires, our end will be as disappointing as Kaufman's film.

The Bourne Supremacy

Michael Elliott

July 23, 2004

Sermon / spiritual topic: Truth; lies; freedom; healing; stability

Author-verified transcription

Matt Damon returns to the screen as Jason Bourne, the super spy suffering from amnesia. The Bourne Supremacy is a sequel which is not that concerned with furthering the development of the characters. Its goal seems to be to mirror the success of the original film by copying the elements of the story as closely as possible.

The movie opens with Jason and Marie (Franka Potente) hiding "off the grid" in a little town in India. At the same time, a CIA sting operation goes bust when a mysterious intruder slips in, kills the targets and escapes with the cash and some files that the CIA wanted to retrieve. Evidence at the scene implicates Jason Bourne.

When an assassin is sent to India to terminate Bourne, it spurs the retired spy into action as he must piece together who is doing what to whom and why they all seem to be intent on killing him.

Pamela Landy (Joan Allen), the CIA agent in charge of the blown operation decides to reopen the classified Treadstone file and assembles the original team who ran it. Ward Abbott (Brian Cox) is a year away from retirement and is none too happy about rehashing the details of a dirty operation he'd just as soon forget. Nicky (Julia Stiles) is also brought into the loop as she was once Bourne's contact in Paris.

Bourne, even though he still has gaps in his memory, is able to always stay a step or two ahead of them. It's not much of an advantage but it does ensure that there will be plenty of chase scenes.

Original director Doug Liman did not return for the sequel so the reins were handed off to Paul Greengrass who vaulted his career into a higher bracket a few years back with the film Bloody Sunday. Greengrass does his best to copy Liman by relying on live action stunts rather than computer effects however his handheld "shaky cam" approach quickly becomes tiresome and annoying.

Regardless of the directorial interference, Matt Damon's characterization remains solid and stoic - a picture of humorless professionalism. The rest of the cast comfortably fit into stereotypical roles - from Joan Allen's authoritative agent with a Type-A personality to Brian Cox's duplicitous, politically driven schemer.

The script by Tony Gilroy, who also helped to pen the original, is again an intelligent treatment of a formulaic spy thriller. Instead of spoon-feeding us with every single plot twist or clue, he allows us to connect the dots on our own.

Without revealing the ending of the film, I will say that one of Bourne's final acts addresses a spiritual truth that is valuable to learn. As he begins to piece together the fragments of his memory, he is also able to present the truth to a person who grew up believing a lie. The truth was not pleasant to hear nor was it pleasant for Bourne to deliver but the bottom line is that living in truth is always preferable to living a lie.

And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free. John 8:32 (KJV)

A lie never delivered anybody from a life of fear or bondage. All it can ever do is to keep us from the deliverance that comes with knowing the truth. Truth may not always be easy to hear but it will always lead one to a healing wholeness that builds strength and stability.

How then comfort ye me in vain, seeing in your answers there remaineth falsehood? Job 21:34 (KJV)

A Dog of Flanders

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
August 27, 1999
Rating
1 star
Source
Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Learning from the past; faith; perseverance; biblical examples

Author-verified transcription

A lot of improvements have been made in the art of cinematic storytelling over the past 40 years. For some reason, the makers of A Dog of Flanders have chosen to ignore them. As a result, their film bears an amazing resemblance to the pastry-thin, emotionally-manipulative movies that Disney’s Wonderful World of Color used to show back in the early 60’s. In capturing the essence of this bygone era, A Dog of Flanders succeeds in being a family film that will bore old and young alike.

Even the story belongs to a different age. Based on a 1872 children’s tale by Ouida, this filmed version marks the fourth time it has been adapted for the screen. Considered to be a classic by many, it holds the distinction of being the first “modern-day dog story.”

Nello Daas, a young orphan, who has inherited his late mother’s artistic talent, lives with his grandfather, helping him deliver milk to the neighboring townspeople. One day he finds a bear of a dog, (breed: Bouvier des Flanders... which translated I think means “large hairy beast.”) The dog is lying by the side of the road, beaten to near death by his owner. Nello helps it to heal and the two become the best of friends.

This is a good thing seeing as Nello has few other friends. The difference between the “haves” and the “have-nots” in his small 19th century village is quite distinguishable and grows larger with every passing year. Nello’s only possible escape from the bleakness of his life is to win the Young Artist’s award which is granted to the most promising young talent in the city.

As the deadline for the art contest approaches, young Nello’s hardships increase. His grandfather dies, he is unjustly accused of arson, his only human friend (and puppy love recipient) is banned from seeing him, he loses the family home for nonpayment of the mortgage... and the bleakness goes on and on.

Most of the adult actors do well enough with the material they are handed. Jon Voight (Varsity Blues) is strong as Michel La Grande, the art master who encourages Nello to keep practicing. Steven Hartley (Split Second) is also effective as Carl Cogez, who does Nello a grave disservice and lives to regret it. Bruce McGill (Courage Under Fire) has some good moments as the affable William the Blacksmith. Jack Warden (Bulworth) as Jehan Daas has little to do other than to speak in a variation of a Flemish accent and to look old and tired.

But A Dog of Flanders is a movie centered around the kids and this is where the weakest casting can be found. Jeremy James Kissner (Great Expectations) and Jesse James (Message in a Bottle) share the credit for the role of Nello, each playing the character at a different age. Their performances are consistent... both being wooden and stiff. Farren Monet and Madylin Sweeten do not fare any better in their responsibilities as Nello’s female friend, Aloise.

As far as Patrasche, the dog, is concerned... If he had any expressiveness, it was covered by hair and could not be seen by the audience.

The lack of artistic quality is disappointing because there is an abundance of biblical lessons. The faith (or believing) demonstrated is heartwarming. While the filmmakers do utilize the spiritualistic error of dead people watching over us, they also present some great biblical principles such as: “The first step to winning is believing you can win,” and “Great men and their deeds always live on.”

The latter is especially true. The men who lived during the biblical times have since died. Yet the truth upon which they stood lives on. The records of their lives and the examples of their believing continue to live on for us via the Word of God.

For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope. Romans 15:4 (KJV)

We can learn from the past. But unlike these filmmakers we must make sure that we package it in a such way that it will appeal to the present.

Charlie's Angels

Author-verified transcription

Something of a TV phenomenon in the 1970s, "Charlie's Angels" has finally been given the big screen, high budget treatment. The familiar trio of Charlie's smart and sexy detectives, whose undercover work often required less cover than seen on most beaches, has been modernized for the 21st century. But whatever else this might be, it certainly isn't progress.

Jill, Kelly, and Sabrina have become a thing of TV's past. The movie's version introduces us to Natalie, Dylan, and Alex, quickly establishing that while these newer "angels" may excel at kung-fu, skydiving, and various other physical activities, their mental acumen is a bit less impressive.

Drew Barrymore ("Never Been Kissed") stars as Dylan and also serves as one of the film's producers. Dylan and her two cohorts, Natalie (Cameron Diaz, "Any Given Sunday") and Alex (Lucy Liu, "Shanghai Noon") have been hired to rescue a kidnapped computer software developer and recover his stolen program before the entire world is held hostage by evil men. To accomplish this task our intrepid (or should that be insipid?) heroines must become geisha girls, race car drivers, German bar maids, and corporate executives. No alias is too improbable and no neckline is too low for our gals.

However, as skimpy as Cameron Diaz's outfits might be, the script is even skimpier in terms of providing rational explanations for the actions and decisions taken by its characters. Logic takes a long, long vacation as the "angels" miraculously solve each piece of the puzzle without the benefit of clues, confessions, or traces of any kind of evidence.

Perhaps we shouldn't expect much from a movie based on a series which helped make ABC known as the "T & A network." Stil, the sheer production cost of the film is immediately evident and the word "waste" is automatically brought to mind.

The director, McG, whose work up to this point has been in the commercial and music video field, simply wasn't up to the challenge of pulling the various components together to form a cogent or entertaining 92 minute product. Instead, he presents to us an illogical, often absurd film which despite its many explosions and wire-enhanced fight scenes, is far less interesting than any episode of the simpler and smaller scaled TV version.

The three female leads offer little in the way of characterization other than to play off each other in a giggling schoolgirl kind of camaraderie.

Bill Murray ("Cradle Will Rock") has an amusing turn as Bosley, the man who stands between the angels and the disembodied voice of Charlie (John Forsythe, whose voice was also used in the original TV series). His scenes, however, give the impression of being tacked on rather than integrated into the story line itself.

Other supporting characters include The Thin Man (another quirky performance by Crispen Glover), Chad (a rather bland appearance by Tom Green), and Roger Corwin (played by an underutilized and visually uncomfortable Tim Curry).

Absent from the film's cast list are any of the six actresses who at various times played in the original TV series. In taking note of this, we might be tempted to say that "fools rush in where angels fear to retread."

Despite the angelic reference of the title, there is little in the film to spiritually edify or interest us. Like Charlie's employees, God's angels do indeed have the ability to "kick butt" when the occasion warrants, but they also know how to be reverent, respectful, observant, or diligently watchful.

Upon further reflection, perhaps there is something of an biblical analogy to be made seeing as the film's "angels" are religiously faithful to an unseen boss, carrying out his orders without question or doubt, battling evil forces on his behalf. Then again, such an analogy is probably stretching the spiritual point more than credibility allows.

Sorry, Charlie... But when all is said and done, these angels are less than divine.

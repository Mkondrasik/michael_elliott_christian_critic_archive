RV

Author-verified transcription

If you ever want to find out about yourself, put your family in an RV and drive. Such is the life lesson learned by Bob Munro (Robin Williams) who did just that in the new road trip comedy RV, with the results being funny enough to justify the price of admission.

Bob is a marketing rep for an environmentally unfriendly company run by a silly twit (Will Arnett) with a germ phobia. Though Bob is pushing the wrong side of 50 and a younger executive is being groomed to replace him, he is apparently the only one in the company who can still command the respect and attention of potential clients - or in this case, a prospective merger possibility.

Unfortunately for Bob, who sees his once close family drifting away from him, the possibility of a new merger comes at an inopportune time. He had arranged an all-important family vacation to Hawaii but when the merger deal presents itself he is given a choice - to cancel the trip and disappoint his family... or defy his boss, who has ordered him to give a song and dance presentation in front of the planned acquisition, and lose his job.

Bob tries a compromise and therein we find the humor. The ridiculously mechanically inept Bob rents an atrociously decorated RV and persuades his reluctant family to take a cross country trip to Colorado (where, unknown to them, the merger meeting is to be held). Instead of lounging on beaches basking in the Hawaiian sun, they'll be huddled together in RV parks getting to know the Bobbie Anns and the Billy Bobs of America. It's a tough sell.

They do meet a perpetually perky family called the Gornickes, led by Travis (Jeff Daniels) and Mary Jo (Kristen Chenoweth). They are career RV'ers, with no address to their name. They and their three home-schooled children just travel up and down the nation's highways looking for people to annoy... I mean meet.

Barry Sonnenfeld (Lemony Snicket's A Series of Unfortunate Events) is at the steering wheel and manages to avoid most potholes along a road that is well traveled (National Lampoon's Vacation series, anyone?). What saves the project is a PG friendly and naturalistic feel to the film. Williams is toned down - he's very funny without being over the top; Daniels and Chenoweth infuse warmth to their stereotypical characters; and love of family is vaulted to the top of what is viewed as being important to these characters.

There's a heavy reliance on crude humor. Much of the earliest RV scenes involve dealing with a stopped up septic system. Yuk, yuk, yuk. But as we begin to warm to the film, we can easily overlook some of the excesses of the filmmakers.

At the heart of all of his deception and subterfuge, Bob simply wants to find a way to reconnect with a family that over the years has grown apart. What he forgets is that actions speak louder than words and the greatest influence he has over his wife and children is the example of how he lives his life.

Ye are witnesses, and God also, how holily and justly and unblamably we behaved ourselves among you that believe: As ye know how we exhorted and comforted and charged every one of you, as a father doth his children. 1 Thessalonians 2:10-11 (KJV)

The greatest gift a father could give his children is the example of how to live a life pleasing to God and man. It is a lesson he need not teach. They will learn it simply by watching and loving him.

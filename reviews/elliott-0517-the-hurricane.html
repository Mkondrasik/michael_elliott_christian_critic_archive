Author-verified transcription

There are few physical forces on this earth as powerful as that of a hurricane. It is therefore fitting that a movie which carries its name is as impacting and devastatingly effective as the meteorological event.

Based upon a true story, the "hurricane" of the title is one Rubin "Hurricane" Carter, a black man contending for the middleweight championship title who is unjustly imprisoned to serve to three life sentences for murders he did not commit.

The screenplay by Armyan Bernstein (Cross My Heart) and Dan Gordon (The Assignment) uses, as its source information, two books: The 16th Round, an autobiography which Mr. Carter wrote from his prison cell; and Lazarus and the Hurricane, written by two of the Canadians who were instrumental in procuring his release.

The film contains the best of both books, painting Mr. Carter as an articulate, intelligent, extraordinary individual who learns to shut out the world in order to survive in the harsh and loveless environment of prison. It also focuses on Rubin's relationship with a young man named Lesra (Vicellous Reon Shannon, Can't Hardly Wait), which is short for Lazarus, who having read his book, is inspired to write and then meet him.

It is Lesra's infectious optimism and unwavering belief in Carter's innocence that gives the Hurricane new hope and an opportunity to reclaim the life that was wrongly wrested from him because of racial prejudice and the hatred that man is somehow able to generate toward his fellow men.

Denzel Washington (The Bone Collector) gives the performance of his career as Rubin "Hurricane" Carter. Words only serve to diminish the superlative work of Mr. Washington. He is absolutely mesmerizing. In a scene where Carter is sent to solitary confinement for refusing to don the uniform of a guilty man, Mr. Washington is afforded an opportunity to simultaneously play both sides of a complex personality: the tough fighter, full of hate and violence; and the scared, innocent man overwhelmed by the conditions in which he finds himself. What captures our attention is the third character he plays in that scene... the real Carter who is a combination of both. His is an award-winning caliber performance.

Director Norman Jewison (A Soldier's Story, Moonstruck) steers the film with an assured hand. His use of flashbacks to fill in the gaps of the story is both judicious and highly effective. Clearly Mr. Jewison knows how to tell a story and communicate its salient points to an audience.

If there is a weakness to the film, it is in the shallowness of the other characters. Lt. Vincent Della Pesca (Dan Hedaya, Dick) who persecutes Carter unmercifully and without reason is drawn too two-dimensionally to be fully believable. Likewise, the three Canadian do-gooders (Deborah Unger, Payback; Liev Schreiber, Jakob the Liar; John Hannah, The Mummy) who devote their lives and their careers in order to secure Carter's release are so sketchily drawn that their actions simply appear to be too good to be true.

And yet it is the absolute commitment demonstrated by these Canadian social activists which is required if one is to achieve one's desired goals.

And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. Galatians 6:9 (KJV)

Rubin "Hurricane" Carter had many people rooting for him. Bob Dylan wrote a hit song about him. Actress Ellen Burstyn, boxer Mohammed Ali, and other celebrities appeared at protest rallies and spoke on his behalf. But at the end of the day, it was the unflagging efforts of a group of normal, average people who, standing by their conviction and refusing to faint in the face of a stronger adversary, won Mr. Carter his freedom.

If our hearts are sound and our motives pure we can do the same for those who are still imprisoned spiritually. All it takes is commitment, unwavering belief, and a willingness to not give up or give in.

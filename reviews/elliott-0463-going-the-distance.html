Author-verified transcription

Every so often, in between reviewing the major releases of Hollywood's studios, I'll be granted an opportunity to screen a small budget video produced and promoted by an independent Christian filmmaker. While the production quality of these labors of love may not equal the multi-million dollar budgeted movies that are playing in our multiplexes, the heart behind these projects is undeniable.

The first product of Oregon-based Justice Pictures, Going The Distance, is a film which deals with an important and timely topic: teen sexuality. What makes it worthy of our notice is that it honestly and responsibly presents the sexual urges which our teens experience as something not to be ignored but rather controlled.

Chris Bresky stars as Phil Glass, leader and keyboard player of a high school garage band called "Murphy's Law." His best friend Jason (Jacob Atilio) is the lead singer. Typically late for their gig, Jason finally arrives with a pair of girls in tow. Kate (Stephanie Smith) and Nicole (Shelbi Wescotttt) are attractive and available. It isn't long before Jason and Kate are double dating with Phil and Nicole.

The course of their relationships takes a divided road however when the issue of sex arises. Phil and Nicole struggle against their natural urges, deciding that they both would prefer to remain virgins until they got married. Kate and Jason make a different choice and find that their relationship suffers as a result of the consequences which they face because of that decision.

Director and co-writer Shawn Justice can be credited with developing characters with whom the audience can empathize and for whom they can root without embarrassment. Going The Distance will not be mistaken for a polished professional production, but as a direct to video release targeted for a specific audience... it is worth a look. Some of the best scenes are the ones which include elongated speeches: Phil's dad (Tony Oliva), during a fishing trip, talking to his son about waiting to have sex until after marriage; Nicole relating to Phil the story of the first "date" that she had with her father where he impressed upon her how she should expect to be treated by the boys she dates.

As might be expected in a small independently produced film using unknown and relatively inexperienced talent, the acting is uneven. Many of the young teens are allowed to overreact or "mug" noticeably during their scenes to the point that they often foil their own efforts to believably communicate what their characters are feeling.

The music by Bart Hafeman is uniformly good and does provide a much needed spark when the film's pacing begins to lag.

Overall, Going The Distance is a responsible treatment of a subject that, because of the undue emphasis that the world places upon sex, is becoming increasingly difficult to rationalize to our teens... that abstaining from sexual relations until after marriage is a valid and rewarding choice. The film doesn't try to make the claim that such a decision will be an easy one to keep. But it does effectively make the point that waiting has its own rewards.

Blessed is the man that endureth temptation: for when he is tried, he shall receive the crown of life, which the Lord hath promised to them that love him. James 1:12 (KJV)

Open and honest communication, the support of family and friends, and a clear understanding of the benefits of waiting to have sex... all of this is needed to help lead our teens into strengthening their resolve to make the decisions that will help build for them the strong foundation upon which a healthy and joyous life may be built.

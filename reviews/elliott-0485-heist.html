Author-verified transcription

So many films habitually "dumb down" for today's audience that when a screenwriter/director makes a film where the audience is not spoon-fed by explaining to them every plot twist or turn, it tends to stand out from the pack. David Mamet (State & Main) is such a talent and his newest film, Heist, does indeed stand out as an intelligent and well-crafted crime drama.

Joe Moore (Gene Hackman, Heartbreakers) is a meticulous thief. A careful planner and strategist ("I wouldn't tie my shoes without a back up plan," he insists), he is nonetheless vulnerable to the risks of the job. During a jewel heist, he is cornered into exposing his face to the security camera, thus "burning" himself and prompting an early retirement.

Bergman (Danny DeVito, What's The Worst That Could Happen?), the fence and financier for his high level criminal activities, has got another "thing" lined up and isn't about to let Joe just walk away from it. By withholding the proceeds of the jewel heist, Bergman forces Joe and his crew to pull off a gold heist. As insurance, Bergman sends his nephew, Jimmy Silk (Sam Rockwell, Galaxy Quest) on the job to keep an eye on his "investment."

Heist soon becomes a story of who's doing what to whom as the characters once again disprove the adage, "honor among thieves."

Hackman is excellent as the old thief still at the top of his game. As said of his character, "he is so cool that when he goes to bed, sheep count him," Hackman is the picture of assurance and professionalism.

His crew consists of his longtime associates Bobby Blaine (Delroy Lindo, The One) and Pinky (Ricky Jay, Mystery Men); as well as his young wife Fran (Rebecca Pidgeon, State & Main). These players give to their characters the impression of their having worked as a team for a long period of time. Much of their communication is nonverbal variety, conveyed through a hand signal, tilt of the head or narrowing of the eyes.

As the outsiders, Danny DeVito brings a familiar bluster to his portrayal and Sam Rockwell, a youthful cockiness which is sharply contrasted to the calm and controlled exteriors provided by Hackman and company.

As competent as the acting is, and it is uniformly good, the real star of this film is the writing of David Mamet. His uncanny ear for dialogue resulted in a naturalistic staccato rhythm that helped to define each of the characters. He deftly avoided the all-too-recognizable clichés now associated with the genre without losing the atmosphere those clichés engender.

For my taste, I felt the film included one plot twist too many and the gunplay which takes place in the latter scenes seemed out of place for what preceded it. But these are minor complaints to a film which I found both engrossing and entertaining. All in all, Heist is the film that The Score (with Robert DeNiro, Marlon Brando, and Edward Norton) could have been.

Both films do ask us to condone and root for characters which unrepentantly engage in criminal activities. In Heist, Joe and Bobby even have a brief discussion about what makes "the world go round," love or money? Joe ends the discussion by concluding that both are correct. He asserts that it is the love of money (or gold) that makes the world go round. If Joe's statement is correct then the world is truly an evil place.

For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows. 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV)

While there are far higher motivations than love of money, none of them rank higher than love for God. If we keep our love for Him as the root cause of all that we do, the treasures we will receive are far greater all the money we could ever "heist" for ourselves.

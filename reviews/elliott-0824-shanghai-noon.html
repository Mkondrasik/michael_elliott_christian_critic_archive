Author-verified transcription

The wild, wild west meets east in Touchstone Pictures' newest offering, Shanghai Noon. As the clever title suggests, this film is a comedy with a western flair. With Jackie Chan (Rush Hour) as the central figure, the martial arts action is fast and furious and the laughs come just as quickly.

Mr. Chan plays Chon Wang (pronounced John Wayne) a member of the Imperial Guards of the Forbidden City in China. When the emperor's daughter, Princess Pei Pei (Lucy Liu, Play it to the Bone) is shanghaied in reverse (kidnapped and put on board a ship leaving China), the emperor handpicks three of his bravest guards to go to America and retrieve the princess. Chon Wang wasn't one of the brave guards... He just tags along to carry the luggage.

When the train taking the Chinese rescue party to Carson City is robbed by the not yet infamous Roy O'Bannon gang, Chon Wang interferes with the holdup and gets himself thrown off the train. O'Bannon (Owen Wilson, The Haunting) is abandoned by his own gang and left to die in the desert. This unlikely pair eventually team up to complete the journey to Carson City, putting cultural differences aside for the common goal of rescuing the princess.

It wouldn't be much of a western without bad guys and Shanghai Noon has plenty to go around. Bad guy number one is Lo Fong (Roger Yuan, Red Corner), the ex-Imperial guard turned traitor who arranged for Pei Pei's kidnapping in order to get his hands on the emperor's gold. Bad guy number two is Van Cleef (Xander Berkeley, Amistad), the ruthless law enforcer who is relentlessly tracking O'Bannon and the Shanghai Kid. Bad guy number three is Wallace (Walton Goggins, The Apostle), the newest and craziest member of the O'Bannon gang who wrests the leadership of the gang and vows revenge on its former leader.

Jackie Chan does not disappoint his many fans. The fight sequences, which take place on a train, as well as in the Indian woods, a western saloon, and a 19th century mission, are a fast blend of choreographed moves and humor. But as plentiful as they may be, the fight scenes are not the focus of the movie. At the core of this buddy picture is the relationship between the eastern Chan and the western Wilson. The developing friendship of their characters is what gives the picture its warmth and affability.

Owen Wilson comes into his own with this picture. A cross between Robert Redford and Paul Newman, it's as if he is playing both parts in Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid. The persona he presents is all American cowboy (Hollywood style) and immensely enjoyable.

Tom Dey, in his feature film directorial debut, rises to the occasion in a big way. Combining eastern locales with western ones, and introducing many different cultures (Chinese, American cowboy, American Indian, etc.), Mr. Dey keeps the interest level high and the action moving crisply.

The film has been rated PG-13 and appropriately so. The violence is cartoon in nature but it is abundant. There is also a fair amount of language and sexuality present.

As an Imperial guard, Chon Wang may not have been near the top of his class. He may not have been the bravest, or most skilled. He certainly wasn't the smartest. What set him apart from the other guards was his faithfulness to those he served. In an English fairy tale book which the princess carried was this phrase: "Once a promise is given, it can never be broken." That is a standard for life. Our heavenly Father has always lived by them as well.

And being fully persuaded that, what he (God) had promised, he (God) was able also to perform. Romans 4:21 (KJV)

Because of God's faithfulness to perform what He has promised, we can trust with our whole heart the Word which contains those promises... And by believing, claim them.

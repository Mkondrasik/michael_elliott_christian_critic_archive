Author-verified transcription

Every so often a film comes along that shows so much promise in its premise that you ache for it to be a better film. The Siege is such an experience.

After the United States kidnaps suspected terrorist leader Sheik Ahmed Bin Talal, illegally nabbing him on foreign soil, his Islamic followers begin to stage a series of increasingly vicious attacks against American citizens in order to force his release.

New York City reels under these terrorist reprisals as three US agencies (all with different agendas) work to counter this new and frightening threat.

Denzel Washington (Fallen) is Anthony "Hub" Hubbard, head of the FBI's task force. His partner is Frank Haddad (Tony Shalhoub, Big Night), an Arab-American who gets caught up in the growing distrust of all things Arabic. Their work to track down and stop the various terrorist "cells" that are operating in conjunction with but independently of each other is being compromised by Elise Kraft, AKA Sharon Bridger (Annette Bening, An American President), a CIA operative who once lived and worked among the Arabs.

"Hub" is aptly nicknamed, serving as he does as the moral center of the film. Trained to do a job within ethical bounds, he finds himself at odds with Kraft, who is more surreptitiously manipulative and not above using sex and seduction as a means for political persuasion.

As the terrorist attacks continue, the President calls for the Army to protect US citizens. Under the command of General William Devereaux (Bruce Willis, Armageddon), martial law is invoked. The army begins to round up all Arab-Americans and herd them into temporary holding centers (similar to the Japanese-American internment camps used during World War II).

The army's Gestapo-like mindset (ably represented by the uncharacteristically stoic Bruce Willis) which justifies the use of torture and violence as a means to an end is one of the more disturbing aspects of the film.

Director Edward Zwick (Courage Under Fire) does well to present the tension and terror generated by the waves of cowardly attacks. Where the movie falters is in its execution beyond that.

Miss Bening's character in particular was too enigmatic to be effective. Obviously she knew more than she shared, but to keep what she knew a secret from the audience only muddied the waters of comprehension and kept us at an emotional distance rather than drawing us into the story.

The film tries to operate on two levels. The first is the depiction of the terrorist attacks and the impact those attacks have on the American "way of life." On another level, it is an unsettling look at governmental policies and the agencies that are asked to carry them out.

The movie asks the intellectual question, "What price freedom?" Is our freedom worth the taking of freedom from another? Can we truly be free when we enslave others? For such an explosively emotional subject matter, it is surprising that we don't have more of a "gut reaction" to this film.

Spiritually, the lesson of the film screams out: "Fear!!!" Fear is a tool of the devil. It binds, restricts, hampers, immobilizes, hinders and otherwise stops the believer in his tracks. In other words, it is not a good thing.

There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love. 1 John 4:18 (KJV)

Fear is the base ingredient to terrorism (hence the name "terror"ism??) While we are human and subject to fears, as we grow in our spiritual maturity, we find that we can lose our fears more quickly and more easily, replacing them with the assured promises of God, keeping the peace of God in our hearts instead of the dread of what might come.

Being made perfect in love is a worthy goal to have. We may still be subject to fear, but it is a promise that the love of God in manifestation is more powerful than the root cause of any fear this world has to offer.

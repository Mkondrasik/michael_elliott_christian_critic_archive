Todd Haynes' Far From Heaven is the kind of cinematic offering that will usually leave critics glowing with praise while the average filmgoer stays rather cold with indifference. A concept film, Far From Heaven is shot in the style of a 1950's domestic melodrama, the kind that director Douglas Sirk used to make. Underscoring that fact is Haynes' title which is a reference to one of Sirk's films, All That Heaven Allows starring Rock Hudson and Jane Wyman.

While the similarities between the two films are mostly on the surface, it is the reality which churns beneath the veneer that has captured Haynes' attention. Sirk and others who operated in this genre were bound by the moral conventions of the time in which they worked but Todd Haynes has no such restriction. As Haynes reveals, it is apparent that home life in the 1950s was not entirely comprised of scenes from "Leave It To Beaver" and "Father Knows Best."

Set in 1957, Far From Heaven stars Julianne Moore (Hannibal) as Cathy Whitaker, a suburban Connecticut housewife and mother of two who works her. She's quite successful at it, even to the point of serving as the prototypical wife for her husband's Magnatech TV ad campaign.

As we get to know her (and the characters around her) better, we discover that appearances can be deceiving. Her "ideal" husband (Dennis Quaid, The Rookie) is battling with his own sexuality, unable to resist the homosexual urges which are repugnant even to him. At the same time, she is attracted to the friendship offered to her by her black gardener (Dennis Haysbert, 24). It is a relationship which causes her close society friends to shudder and recoil in horror.

Artistically, Far From Heaven risks audience alienation by staying true to a genre (domestic melodrama) that is so stylized and dated that it serves to distance us from its characters rather than draw us closer. Still, the performances that Todd Haynes elicits from his cast are impressive.

Julianne Moore, in particular, is award-worthy as a woman trapped by the conventions of the time who is dealing with situations that were not taught or spoken of in polite society. Dennis Quaid shows considerable restraint and emotional depth as a man failing to resist strong homosexual temptations. Dennis Haysbert plays his character with a naiveté that, to me, seems unrealistic for the times in which the film is set. It is a fine, easygoing performance which I feel could have shown more internal conflict.

Haynes seems to be commenting on a single theme by using three different controversial fronts: racism, feminism, and homosexuality. The message that Haynes sends is a valid one (that people would be better off living honestly rather than behind self-constructed facades.) However, the implication that is conveyed by linking sexual proclivity with race and gender skews that message, leading the audience to perhaps draw a conclusion that is false by God's standards.

Race is something that is decided at birth. Same too with gender. But sexual preference is and always will be a choice. There is nothing genetic about it.

They exchanged the truth of God for a lie, and worshiped and served created things rather than the Creator -- who is forever praised. Amen. Because of this, God gave them over to shameful lusts. Even their women exchanged natural relations for unnatural ones. In the same way the men also abandoned natural relations with women and were inflamed with lust for one another. Romans 1:25-27a (NIV)

Living honestly and openly is certainly to be encouraged; as is our free will right to choose which course we will follow. As Christians, we should bear no animosity nor ill-will toward our fellow man regardless of which course he chooses. But neither should we pretend that any course is a good one. God has set the standard of truth. All we must do is follow it for ourselves and teach it to others so that they have the same opportunity. In its implication, Far From Heaven does not stay true to the Christian standard and may mislead many who are in need.

Author-verified transcription

Will he or won't he (turn to the dark side)? It is a question that need not be asked because, thanks to George Lucas and his decision to shoot his decades-spanning episodic Star Wars movie out of sequence, everybody already knows the answer.

Seeking the answer to that question cannot be the reason that we (along with millions of other sci-fi fans) are flocking to the final installment of the Star Wars franchise. The reason must be that Star Wars has ceased being merely a movie - it is a true cultural phenomenon that transcends critical observation. It is more than a film - it is an event that a generation (or two) has long been anticipating.

Chronologically speaking, Episode III: Revenge of the Sith follows two films and precedes three others. Because it is the missing middle chapter to a story set in a galaxy far, far away, there's going to be a lot of loose ends that will need to be tied up by the end of its 2 1/2 hour running time. Chancellor Palpatine (Ian McDiarmid) must be revealed to be the evil Sith Lord Sidious; Padme (Natalie Portman) must become pregnant and give birth to twins (Luke and Leia of Episodes 4-6); the cloned storm-trooping army of the Federation must somehow be turned against the Jedi knights; and of course, the light-saber wielding Anakin Skywalker (Hayden Christensen) must "become" Darth Vader - the baddest bad guy that movies have ever produced.

Lucas, to his credit, continues to add to his greatest legacy - the boldest, most eye-catching six-part sci-fi spectacle to hit the screens. To our disappointment, he also continues to prove himself incapable of writing or directing scenes requiring intimacy or heartfelt emotion without resorting to the worst kind of cinematic cheesiness.

The film's opening is filled with the unexpected promise of a return to the action-packed fun of the original movie. When sending Obi-Wan (Ewan McGregor) and Anakin to rescue Chancellor Palpatine from the clutches of Lord Dooku (Christopher Lee), Lucas, in addition to providing the brilliant special effects that has been the standard of the franchise, gives us what the first two prequels have sorely lacked - humor.

Unfortunately, this light-hearted attitude doesn't last beyond the opening scenes. Lucas loses himself in the space soap opera quagmire that he has constructed and allows himself and the talented cast he has assembled to be sucked down into a humorless dark place.

Parents, be advised that this is no G or PG rated movie. The violence in this film, though no where near Tarantino levels, far exceeds anything we've previously seen from the Star Wars world.

What Lucas has provided to us is valuable in ways that transcend art. He mirrors truth in how he depicts evil - specifically, how man retains the free will ability to choose whether or not he succumbs to it.

The Scriptures are clear - no one is born "evil." Anakin had the ability to choose to follow a good and righteous path. For reasons of his own, he decided to go a different way - fully believing that his cause was just. By deconstructing his story, we gain insight to how a man can deceive himself and fall away from what is right in order to follow what he erroneously thinks is right.

Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall. Proverbs 16:18 (KJV)

Anakin suffered from a malady that afflicts many people. He lacked meekness. This deficiency inevitably resulted in a vulnerability upon which evil forces were able to capitalize. Had he continued to trust and believe in the true power of "the force," instead of turning to the seductive dark side, he could have achieved all his desires without the consequences that eventually robbed him of that which he desired most.

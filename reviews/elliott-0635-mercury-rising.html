Author-verified transcription

Put away the brain cells... Mercury Rising is not that kind of film. Believe me, it's for the best that we don't dwell too long on the plausibility of this story line. What we have here is your basic cop story - lone good guy protecting the innocent child from the heartless government agents who are not beneath killing a few average citizens in order to protect what they consider to be the “greater good.”

When kept on that level, Mercury Rising can be enjoyed as the action packed thriller that it is designed to be. Looking more deeply into the film will only expose its many flaws. Why go to that trouble? If we are truly interested in seeing a fine piece of cinematic artistry, would we not be kidding ourselves to think that we would find it in the latest Bruce Willis action hero vehicle?

On the other hand, if what we are looking for is a lot of gunplay, explosions, and tense situations from which our hero may emerge, battered, bruised, and bloodied, Mercury Rising may very well be up that proverbial alley.

Bruce Willis (Die Hard) plays Art Jeffries, an FBI undercover agent, who we first see trying to defuse a volatile hostage situation. When that situation goes sour, ending in bloodshed, Art publicly blames those responsible for the mismanaged operation. His outspokenness puts him on the "black list" of the FBI operatives.

Meanwhile, Simon, a nine year old autistic savant, has innocently cracked what was thought to be an impenetrable code. A code so highly classified that, in the wisdom found only in action movies and certain governmental agencies, it was printed in a newsstand puzzle magazine.

Simon's ability to read the code is considered by the National Security Agency (NSA) to be unacceptable. They will either have to a) change or retire the code; b) stop publishing the code in puzzle magazines; or c) kill the boy and everyone connected with him. As you might expect, options A and B were never discussed.

After Simon's parents are murdered by an NSA hit man, Art finds the boy, makes it his personal mission to protect him, and the chase, as they say, is on. A chase which is made more difficult because the autistic Simon does not like being held or grabbed and will tend to wander off at every opportunity. This of course allows for director Harold Becker (City Hall, Sea of Love) to place the child in even more danger. For instance, immediately after Art and the boy escape from a hospital assassin and narrowly evade the killers in a car chase involving a stolen ambulance, Simon decides to take a leisurely stroll down the train tracks straight into the path of an oncoming locomotive.

Mr. Willis is at home in the kind of role upon which he has built his movie career. Alec Baldwin (The Edge) turns in another smarmy performance as the NSA head honcho, Lieutenant Colonel Kudrow. Miko Hughes (Kindergarten Cop) is consistent as Simon. It is a tall order to ask a child actor to pull off a believable "autistic" performance. While not completely satisfying, his autonomic presence does not overtly detract from the rest of the film.

Mercury Rising is not a work that immediately lends itself to social or spiritual commentary. It's a shoot 'em up kind of flick that is eminently watch-able but soon forgotten 20 minutes after you leave the theater. Still, we at The Christian Critic are not easily dissuaded. We are always on the lookout for spiritual comparisons. We couldn't help but notice that as the body count was rising, most of those killed were the ones trying to get information to Art. They were aware of the risk they were taking but believed that the information they carried was more important than their own lives.

Most of us have never been put into a position where our ideals were tested. What is of primary importance to us? For what are we willing to risk our lives? It is easy to forget the many sacrifices that have been made in the name of God. Bibles are now sold in grocery stores. They can be found in every hotel room. But as early as a few centuries ago, to carry even just one printed page of the scriptures was a crime, punishable by death.

Those who lived during those dark days could be killed just for reading, writing or speaking God's Word. Many of them paid that price. Because they did, the Word of God continues to live for us.

History doesn't always record the names of those who pay this ultimate sacrifice. Just as most reviews won't mention the names of the secondary characters who got killed just as they delivered the next clue to the puzzle Art is trying to solve. Regardless of the acclaim that they may or may not get, the truth is - they are the real heroes.

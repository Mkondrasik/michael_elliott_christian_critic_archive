Author-verified transcription

“What fools these mortals be?” What fools indeed? Surely, many of those involved in the newest rendition of A Midsummer Night's Dream may be asking themselves that very question.

Not that the film is all bad. There are two performances in particular that, being outstanding, almost redeem the picture completely. But “almost” is most probably not what the filmmakers had in mind.

Shakespeare's story is not as much simple as it is serviceable. Demetrius loves Hermia. Helena loves Demetrius. Hermia loves Lysander who loves her right back. Italy's 19th century social convention being what it was, Hermia's father insists upon her marrying the man she does not love. This convoluted love quadrangle is brought before Duke Theseus and his bride-to-be Hippolyta who do little to resolve it.

Meanwhile, in the woods, the fairy kingdom is aflutter with activity. King Oberon and his lady Titania are having a lovers' spat. Pity the poor fool who stumbles in the midst of it. Enter Nick Bottom.

Bottom is part of an amateur theatrical “company” preparing a dreadful presentation in honor of Duke Theseus' wedding. He is magically transformed by Puck, the minion of Oberon, into having the visage of an ass.

Because of a potion placed upon her sleeping eyelids, Titania becomes enamored with this foolish looking creature. But the potion is working overtime... It also graces the lids of both Lysander and Demetrius thereby transferring the object of their adoration from Hermia to Helena. The frivolity reaches its first climax when all parties converge in the Italian woods one midsummer's night.

When in the right hands, A Midsummer Night's Dream can be one of Shakespeare's more delightful comedies. In this production, only two sets of hands rise to the challenge. The director and the rest of the cast allow the richness of this material to slip through their fingers. Of the vast array of talent assembled for the film, only Calista Flockhart (Ally McBeal) and Kevin Kline (In & Out) really shine, appearing to be well-suited for the Shakespearean stage.

Miss Flockhart is remarkably adept at breathing life into the love-sick, Demetrius-stalking Helena. Desperately amusing as the woman spurned, she is doubly amusing as she misunderstands the gentlemen's turn of affection as one that is born of mockery.

Kevin Kline brings a surprising depth of character to the role of Bottom. Usually played as a blustery buffoon, Mr. Kline's Bottom is instead imbued with a deep sensibility. Here is a vain man, in whose vanity we see a cry for attention and a search for self-worth. In his mouth, Shakespeare's words seem as fresh and modern as they are poetic. And his climactic presentation of the “tragical mirth” of Pyramus and Thisbe somehow manages to live up to its descriptive oxymoron.

Rupert Everett (My Best Friend's Wedding) and Michelle Pfeiffer (The Deep End of the Ocean) are simply disappointing as elfin royalty. They may look the part, but their words, which carry no weight, are soon as lost in the woods as the mismatched mortal lovers.

Biblically, we can draw a parallel between the fairy kingdom and the spiritual realities of life. We, like the mortals of the play, live and work on a multi-planed existence. First, we have the physical reality of which everyone is familiar. Our five senses provide us with the proper stimuli from which we learn to recognize the world around us. But there is also an invisible plane that is just as real... The spiritual plane where an unseen competition is continually underway.

Our awareness of this spiritual level of existence will only be as acute as the level of understanding we have achieved through our study of the Word of God. We need not be unwitting pawns of unseen beings. Neither do we need to resort to dreams to explain away unexplainable things such as Puck suggests. Instead, we can have the ability to see through the physical facade to recognize the spiritual truths which lie beneath the surface. To use Shakespeare's own words, it is a “most rare vision.”

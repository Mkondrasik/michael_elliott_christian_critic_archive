Finding Neverland

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 12, 2004
Rating
4 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Mark 10:15Mark 9:23John 11:40

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Childlike faith; believing; trust; imagination

Author-verified transcription

Before the advent of video games and round-the-clock TV programming, children were forced to entertain themselves through the age old practice of make-believe. No one has ever manifested this imaginative art form more endearingly than James M. Barrie, the man who gave the world Peter Pan. In Finding Neverland, we are told an embellished tale of how Barrie arrived at his fanciful story which is now hailed as a classic in children's literature.

Barrie (Johnny Depp) was a successful Scottish playwright living in London at the turn of the 20th century. As the film tells it, Peter Pan is an invention that was born from his imagination sparked by his association with Sylvia Llewelyn-Davies, a young widow (Kate Winslet) with four small boys.

Delighted by the playfulness of the youngsters, Barrie begins to spend all his free time in the company of the Llewelyn-Davies family. He neglects his wife and marriage; troubles Sylvia's authoritative mother who questions his motives; and, in general, gives proper English society a new scandal about which to gossip.

He also begins to germinate the idea of a place where children didn't have to grow up – where fairies, pirates, and Indians intermingled – and where believing something long enough and hard enough will make it come true. The summer he spends with George, Jack, Peter and Michael Llewelyn-Davies is the summer James M. Barrie “found” Neverland and the world is a richer place because of it.

Inspired by true events and based on a play by Allan Knee, the film takes many liberties with the facts, embellishing them to the point of sheer fantasy. Somehow, we just know that Barrie would approve.

He couldn't help but be pleased with the work of Johnny Depp who is utterly charming as Barrie, infusing the famed playwright with a dichotomous blend of personality traits ranging from playful impishness to cultured refinement. It is a winning performance in a most enjoyable film.

Kate Winslet as Sylvia, Julie Christie as her mother, and Dustin Hoffman, as the American producer who takes on the financial risk of mounting Barrie's strange new play, make up the ensemble of supporting players along with the quartet of boys who give Barrie his inspiration.

Freddie Highmore as the incorrigible Peter is a standout but all four have some wonderful moments, many coming by way of the “throwaway lines” that fall like pearls upon those attentive enough to be listening for them.

Marc Forster (Monster's Ball) has found the perfect touch for expressing the fancy and fantasy at play within Barrie's mind. To him believing and make-believing are often one and the same and as he plays with the children, we see him “go” to places in his mind that are not bound by the limitations of the real world.

Oh, to be able to recapture that rare ability to think as a child – to believe as purely and wholly as we once did before life's disappointments and responsibilities taught us how to become withdrawn, cautious, sober or cynical. What a gift that would be. It is how we are to receive the things that are of God – as children who completely and utterly trust and believe.

Verily I say unto you, Whosoever shall not receive the kingdom of God as a little child, he shall not enter therein. Mark 10:15 (KJV)

Whether we seek the way to heaven or to Neverland, the first step of the journey is the same. We must believe as a child believes... with our whole heart.

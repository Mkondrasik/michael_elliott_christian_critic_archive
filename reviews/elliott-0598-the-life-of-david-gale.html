Author-verified transcription

In trying to add more leverage to the argument against the death penalty, The Life of David Gale may have unintentionally provided ammunition for the other side. Most will agree - this is one project that should have been killed before it reached the screen.

David Gale (Kevin Spacey, K-Pax) is a popular university professor of philosophy and a leading activist for DeathWatch, an organization whose stand against the death penalty goes largely unheeded by the heartless right-wing politicians who control the Texas legislature.

David’s life takes a turn for the worse after one of his students falsely accuses him of rape. With his marriage breaking up, his alcoholism getting worse, and both his career and reputation destroyed, the one remaining friend in his life is Constance (Laura Linney, You Can Count On Me), a fellow DeathWatch activist. When her dead body is found (bound and gagged), Gale is accused and convicted of her murder.

After spending six years on death row, the time of his execution is now drawing near and so he reaches out to magazine reporter Bitsy Bloom (Kate Winslet, Iris) to tell his side of the story. At first skeptical, her investigation leads her to believe that the state of Texas is about to put an innocent man to death.

Irony is a delicate figure of speech. If one is too heavy-handed with it, the point is missed. The Life of David Gale delivers its irony with all the subtlety of an electric chair. The point is not only missed, it is dulled to absurdity. Contributing to this unsavory mess is a hand-wringing, overwrought performance by Winslet, more smug, self-satisfied posturing by Spacey, and a drab, unappealing portrayal by Linney.

The heavily-slanted screenplay was provided by first-timer Charles Randolph who has obviously placed credibility rather low on his priority list. Despite the self-important attitude it conveys, there are far too many lapses of logic for the film to be taken seriously.

Director Alan Parker (Mississippi Burning, Evita) is certainly no stranger to politically charged material. Thus, it is surprising for The Life of David Gale to misfire as badly as it does. Whatever political agenda he may have had in mind is not served well by the melodramatic treatment and faulty plot twists.

About the only thing the film has going for it is its timeliness. The national debate on capital punishment continues to inflame passions and stir ire on both sides. While this film will most likely not add a substantial voice to the debate, it does bring focus to a topic which has once again risen to gain media attention.

When considering the death penalty from a biblical perspective, we must bear in mind that the ultimate judge will one day rule for all. Until that day, man sits in judgment. The death penalty was instituted for this world, an imperfect and impure environment if ever there was one. Who among us would not prefer living in a Utopia? Who wouldn’t want to live in a place where crime and punishment were not daily realities? Sadly, that is not the world in which we currently live. And so, man has devised laws to govern his society because there are those among us who cannot govern themselves according to an acceptable standard.

Knowing this, that the law is not made for a righteous man, but for the lawless and disobedient, for the ungodly and for sinners, for unholy and profane, for murderers of fathers and murderers of mothers, for manslayers, For whoremongers, for them that defile themselves with mankind, for menstealers, for liars, for perjured persons, and if there be any other thing that is contrary to sound doctrine; 1 Timothy 1:9-10 (KJV)

Any law must include a reciprocal punishment for the violation of that law. Granted, the death penalty is a harsh punishment. This is undeniable. But because of the kind of evil which exists in this world, the only thing harsher that I can think of is not having it.

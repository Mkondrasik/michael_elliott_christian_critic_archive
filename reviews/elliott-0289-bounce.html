Bounce

Author-verified transcription

It appears Hollywood has rediscovered that the embers of love can be found even in the ashes of tragic despair. Last year brought us Random Hearts where the surviving spouses of airplane crash casualties fell for each other. Last spring we were treated to the surprisingly light Return To Me which featured the rather heavy sounding story of a man who falls for the transplant recipient of his late wife's heart.

Entering into theaters this weekend is Bounce, a film about a man who, on impulse, gives up his plane ticket to another man. When that flight crashes, he goes into an emotional, guilt-ridden tailspin which isn't helped when he finds himself falling in love with the dead man's widow.

What gives Bounce its bounce and lifts it high above the formulaic, sappy, or melodramatic B-movie premise are a pair of emotionally honest, solid performances and an intelligent screenplay. The grief of the characters is heartbreakingly real yet filmed in such a muted way that it doesn't become maudlin or feel invasive. The relationships may, on paper, appear to be forced or implausible, yet the actors provide so many nuances within their performances, that we accept and understand the attraction and the inner conflict each of them are experiencing.

Ben Affleck (Reindeer Games) is ad-executive Buddy Amaral who, having cheated death, fails to bounce back immediately. He turns to drink and depression during a self-destructive year, being unable to absorb the guilt he feels regarding giving his plane ticket to another man, thus causing his death. Mr. Affleck overplays the drunken scenes a bit, but after he sobers up and tries to set his life aright, he demonstrates an emotional subtlety that draws us towards his character.

As Abby, the grieving widow with two sons, who pretends she's divorced because she doesn't like the way society treats widows, Gwynth Paltrow (Duets) connects with the fragility of a woman reentering the dating world after a devastating loss. For her, bouncing back after her tragedy only means that she gets to crash over and over again. Her grief is painfully real and her character's emotional stability is balanced on a razor's edge. At the same time, she is able to demonstrate a quirky personality and much of the humor of the film comes from her actions and comments.

Writer/director Don Roos (The Opposite of Sex) is becoming well known and respected for the intelligence and wit reflected in his dialogue. This film is no exception. Whether it is the bantering between Amaral and his homosexual assistant (Johnny Galecki, The Opposite of Sex), a tender heart to heart moment between Amaral and the oldest son of Abby, or the casual interplay of strangers at an airport bar during a delay, the dialogue is always crisp, interesting, and operates on multiple levels.

So much of this film deals with the aftermath of a fatal tragedy and the grief, guilt, and other emotions which are felt during such times. The scriptures prepare us for the sting of death, because as long as we live in this world, it is a certainty that we will not escape it.

But the Word also tells us that we need not grieve as unbelievers do. We have a hope, a promise to hold in our hearts which will help us through the pain and grief that we shall feel when someone close to us dies.

But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep (dead), that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. 1 Thessalonians 4:13 (KJV)

We will certainly sorrow. But not as those who have no hope. The grief which we feel due to the loss of someone close to us is tempered and eventually healed by our understanding and remembrance of God's promise regarding the return of our Lord and the gathering together of all who have believed.

We can bounce back from a loss. How high we bounce is dependent upon how strong our believing is.

Dirty Dancing 2: Havana Nights

Author-verified transcription

It may have been better for this "Baby" to have been left in the corner. Dirty Dancing 2: Havana Nights has nothing to do with the 1987 hit that shares the first part of its name. That's not entirely true. There's sweaty, sensual dancing and a "forbidden" romance. There's also Patrick Swayze who shows up in a brief and unneeded cameo to show that he hasn't lost any of his steps.

Katey Vendetto (Romola Garai) is an intelligent, attractive high school senior who finds herself uprooted when her father is transferred to Havana, Cuba in November of 1958. Fulgencio Batista is still president but a growing unrest is developing among the people led by a revolutionary named Fidel Castro.

That may someday make for an interesting backdrop to a movie but it's not going to be this one. The political tension of the times is not well explained or handled. The focus of this film is instead upon young Katey and her interest in cabana boy Xavier Perez (Diego Luna).

When her friendship with Xavier leads to him losing his job at the hotel because of their zero tolerance regarding fraternizing with hotel guests, Katey tries to help him by offering to be his partner in an upcoming dance contest.

The conflict comes in the style difference between the two. She's been trained in the classical ballroom style of dance by her parents who were professional ballroom dance partners. He's learned his sensual style from the streets of Havana and knows no formal steps or structure. Together, they must blend their styles by teaching each other what they know of dance.

Of course, Katey's conventionally rigid parents (Sela Ward and John Slattery) disapprove of Xavier. Xavier's family don't understand why he's even bothering with the dance contest when there's a revolution brewing around the corner.

Of the cast, Diego Luna as Xavier is the standout. He's got a nice easygoing way about him and his dancing is excellent. Romola Garai works through her character arc as best she can but she is severely hampered by the script which is credited to no less than eight screenwriters. Some of her character's decisions make little sense or are not supported by other elements in the script.

There is a decent message regarding how the uppity American tourists wrongly treat the Cuban natives as inferiors although it is a bit obvious and heavy handed. A far more interesting comment was made by Xavier as he was explaining the difference between the Cuban dancing and the ballroom dancing that Katey studied. The Cuban style of dance isn't studied or learned. It is felt. As Xavier puts it, "It is the dance of slaves. When they dance, it was their only time to be free."

Dance is meant to be a form of celebration. That is how it is used throughout the scriptures. As the people were blessed by God, they danced as an expression of their joy and worship.

Let them praise his name in the dance: let them sing praises unto him with the timbrel and harp. Psalms 149:3 (KJV)

Perhaps it is why we, as a people, are so drawn to dancing. Because at its core, it is simply an expression of love and joy.

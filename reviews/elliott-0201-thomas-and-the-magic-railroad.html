Thomas and the Magic Railroad

Author-verified transcription

If I thought I was a tough critic, I discovered that I'm a pushover compared to a theater full of children ages 3 to 5. We're all gathered together to see the motion picture debut of a beloved children's character, Thomas the Tank Engine in his film, Thomas and the Magic Railroad.

Thomas, as many already know, is a talking blue train who, along with his friends, travel the tracks on the Island of Sodor. On the other side of Muffle Mountain is the storybook railroad town of Shining Time, home of Burnett Stone (Peter Fonda, The Limey.) Grandpa Stone, as he is known, has soured a bit because he feels responsible for a missing train named Lady which was the source of the magic which bridged the worlds of Sodor and Shining Time.

Meanwhile, Thomas and his steam driven friends are concerned because the evil Diesel Ten has returned and is up to no good. We can tell Diesel is a bad train because of his scowl and the steam shovel-like claw on his roof which he calls "Pinchy."

With the help of some magic gold dust, the eighteen inch Mr. Conductor (Alec Baldwin, Outside Providence) travels between the two worlds, keeping the trains on time and solving other problems, living by the code of the "three R's"... Responsible, Reliable, and Really Useful. He is joined by his cousin, Junior, a tiny surfer dude who, though he means well, has not yet developed those qualities.

With gold dust running out, Diesel Ten running amok, and Lady not running at all, Thomas, Grandpa Stone, his granddaughter Lily (Mara Wilson, Matilda) and Mr. Conductor must figure out the mystery of the missing magical train.

If all this seems to be a bit too convoluted to reach the understanding of the film's target audience, it is. Much of the film's plot escaped even me and it has been a long time since my age could be counted on one hand.

I will admit to being a bit envious as my diminutive audience partners vocalized their opinions which ranged from "I want to go home" to "I dropped my popcorn." While we should always try to exhibit adult decorum and proper etiquette, there's something to be said for the simple and direct expression of childlike honesty. Certainly there were times when the action on screen was able to hold their focus, but the meandering pace, the simplistic animation, and the overall length of the film (nearly 90 minutes) proved to be simply too much for the antsy preschoolers.

This is a disappointment because there are some redeemable elements. Both Mr. Fonda and Mr. Baldwin give warmly likable performances and Mr. Rodgers' antics are quite amusing. Just the concept of a G-rated film specifically aimed at the preschool market was enough to earn my appreciation for the filmmakers. This is a target group severely overlooked in the commercial marketplace and the parents of these children are forever looking for appropriate entertainment pastimes for them.

Britt Allcroft, the originator of the British TV series, Thomas the Tank Engine, wrote and directed Thomas and the Magic Railroad. A simpler story line would have helped her immensely. There is just too much happening at once and too many characters for the youngest members of the audience to absorb. On the other hand, the simplistically drawn trains and animation are just not of a special enough quality to interest older children who have grown accustomed to more sophisticated fare like Toy Story 2 or even Veggie Tales.

There are a number of moral lessons that can be derived from the film. Among them, the idea of stewarding that which one has been given. Grandpa Stone is sad because he did not properly steward the now missing train. Junior causes trouble because he did not properly steward the dwindling gold dust. We too have been given something precious to steward.

Let a man so account of us, as of the ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God. 1 Corinthians 4:1 (KJV)

God has entrusted to us His Word, which contains the secret to eternal life and everlasting salvation. It is up to us to be responsible, reliable and really useful in our handling of it.

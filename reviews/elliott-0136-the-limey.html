The Limey

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 2, 1999
Rating
1 star
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Romans 13:11-13Ephesians 5:15-161 Corinthians 15:34

Sermon / spiritual topic: Time; redeeming time; consequences; regret; wise living

Author-verified transcription

It has been said that "time flies like an arrow." Whoever thought up that adage obviously never had to try and make sense of Steven Soderbergh's newest film offering, The Limey, where time travels more like an abused pinball.

Terence Stamp (Star Wars I: The Phantom Menace) stars as Wilson, a career criminal who travels from England to the US in order to investigate and avenge his estranged daughter's death. Almost immediately, his investigation comes to rest upon Terry Valentine (Peter Fonda, Ulee's Gold).

Any number of hired henchmen, bodyguards and advisors attempt to dissuade Wilson from his plans of revenge. Most of them will regret their interference.

Had the screenplay been directed by John Woo or starred Charles Bronson and Chuck Norris, The Limey would have no doubt ended up in the middle of some studio's B-movie list of direct to video releases. It is a respectable but somewhat insubstantial tale.

However, in the hands of Steven Soderbergh (Out of Sight), the story that is told takes a back seat to the manner in which it is told.

Cutting and pasting like a mad Internet web master, Mr. Soderbergh has tossed aside the normal ABC structure of story development and linear narrative. The film's dialogue loops, repeats itself and overlaps into other scenes. Reaction shots from past or future events in the film work their way into seemingly unrelated moments. And interspersed throughout the film are clips of Poor Cow, a 1967 movie starring a much younger version of Terence Stamp.

Then as now, Mr. Stamp demonstrates that indefinable quality known as screen presence. His strong face, intense gaze and minimal gesturing all contribute to arrest our attention whenever the camera lingers upon him (which it does quite often in The Limey.)

Peter Fonda portrays the stereotype of the morally weak LA producer-type, complete with young girlfriend, spacious house with spectacular view, and expendable security men.

Leslie Ann Warren (Teaching Mrs. Tingle) is given relatively little to do as an actress/acting teacher who had Wilson's daughter in one of her classes, which apparently qualifies her to make psychological pronouncements on the nature of the relationship between the girl and her ex-con of a father.

Nicky Katt (Rules of Engagement) is given a few moments to shine as Stacy, a sarcastic hitman with something derisive to say about everything and everybody.

But this movie belongs (lock, stock, and two smoking barrels) to Mr. Stamp. Even though this film failed to captivate me, the reason was not due to his performance. I must, however, admit that his character's use of the Cockney rhyming slang is sometimes hard to follow. I didn't get it when it was used sparingly in To Sir With Love (also in 1967) and I find that I still haven't developed an ear for it during the years in between.

The movie does have points to make about time and about how we choose to use the time we are given. Choosing a life of crime cost Wilson in many ways, not least of all by taking his daughter away from him. Once spent, time cannot be regained. And as flawed humans, we tend to regret the time we've lost, which in turn causes us to lose even more.

In reality, time is unforgiving in its passing. It does not bounce from moment to moment. It does not rewind and give us second chances. It merely moves forward. Second by second; minute by minute; day by day. It is therefore up to us to make the most of each moment we have, spending time wisely and well.

Seeing as I have a little extra space, I'll run the risk of losing my audience and give you the rest of the quote I cited in the opening paragraph: "Time flies like an arrow but fruit flies like a banana." (Anonymous)

OK, so it's an old joke that you can appreciate and disdain at the same time... Come to think of it, in that way it is not unlike The Limey.

Author-verified transcription

When our elementary school primer encouraged us to "See Dick; See Dick Run," it was assumed that Dick was doing something worth watching. In the new comedy Fun with Dick and Jane, it is an assumption that proves false.

It is a remake of a 1977 satirical comedy with George Segal and Jane Fonda which took stabs at the over-inflated balloon known as consumerism. While the message is certainly as pertinent to today's life as it was thirty years ago, the new film's focus has shifted to the impact of corporate greed.

Dick Harper (Jim Carrey) finally gets the promotion that will take his career to penthouse heights. Unfortunately on its heels is a high profile corporate scandal (a la Enron) which ruins the company and Dick's credibility. Overnight, Dick becomes unemployed and, since all his investments were tied up in his company, financially destitute.

Dick and Jane (Téa Leoni) quickly run through their savings, sell all of their possessions, and face a future that only drives them to desperation. He takes a job as a Wal-Mart greeter. She signs up to be a subject for experimental cosmetic surgery. Pride and dignity take a back seat to basic human need. They are game for anything that might bring in a few dollars - even crime.

Carrey tries to thread the needle here, Looking for the elusive blend between pathos and comedy. His slips in and out of his once trademark physical style of broad, slapstick humor such as riding up to the executive floor in an elevator miming to the song "I Can Believe I Can Fly." but such moments are hardly seamless. They feel like editorial afterthoughts intended to improve a movie that doesn't quite work.

Téa Leoni does her best to hang on for the ride by changing her character's sails to match Carrey's ever changing winds. It is a performance that is all reaction with not much of an anchor to hold her in place.

As a social commentary, Fun with Dick and Jane loses its bite by morphing into an under-plotted comic revenge movie. intended to give a fictional comeuppance (and therein some sense of justice) to those responsible for the misuse of employee trust and pension plans, it fails to nail its subject to the wall of public opinion.

Money is a tool that enables us to do many things in this life. It is not an evil thing to be avoided. But neither is it to be worshipped, coveted, nor hoarded. In one of God's delicious dichotomies, the more one loves money, the less satisfaction it will bring. The worst thing we can do is to place our trust in our wealth or look to our riches for our salvation or deliverance.

Labour not to be rich: cease from thine own wisdom. Wilt thou set thine eyes upon that which is not? for riches certainly make themselves wings; they fly away as an eagle toward heaven. Proverbs 23:4-5 (KJV)

No matter our financial plight, we should never succumb to the temptation to stray from our righteousness, which has eternal rewards, in order to gain a few dollars which will provide only temporary relief. Such a decision will bring with it lasting repercussions.

He that trusteth in his riches shall fall: but the righteous shall flourish as a branch. Proverbs 11:28 (KJV)

Author-verified transcription

With no disrespect intended towards the rest of Hollywood's leading men, the coolest actor in movies today can arguably be said to be Samuel L. Jackson (The Red Violin). Thus, it is fitting that he be tapped to play what may be the coolest or "baddest" celluloid detective around, John Shaft, in the film which bears his name.

Originally played by Richard Roundtree in the 1971 version, SHAFT heralded a new genre some called "blaxploitation" and boasted one of the most effective movie theme songs ever (reprised by original artist Isaac Hayes for this film). The role made Mr. Roundtree's career and inspired an entire generation of black artists.

Shaft, as the movie opens, is a no nonsense police detective with a low tolerance level for racial injustice. When he arrives on the scene of a brutal hate crime, he wastes no time in identifying Walter Wade (Christian Bale, American Psycho) as the key suspect. The problem is that the victim is black, Wade is white AND rich, the eyewitness (Toni Collette, The Sixth Sense) is too scared to testify and the justice system can be bought. Bail is set and in no time at all, Wade has left the country and is off skiing in Switzerland.

Two years later, Shaft is involved in another case involving a third-rate Dominican drug lord (Jeffrey Wright, Basquiat) who yearns for the big-time. When Wade inexplicably returns to the US, these two cases become incredulously intertwined and Shaft must wrestle against both parties in order to see justice done.

Because of high level corruption on the police force, the only people John Shaft can trust are Carmen (Vanessa Williams, Light it Up), a fellow cop, and Rasaan (Busta Rhymes, Higher Learning), a street smart stoolie who has worked with Shaft in the past. Richard Roundtree also makes an appearance as J. Shaft, the private detective uncle of our hero.

The film takes its lead from the catchy theme music and presents its story with major attitude. All of the characters have more "bite" or "edge" than the normal Hollywood fare. But all the attitude in the world can't disguise the fact that logically, the script is full of holes and the dialogue full of uncomfortable clichés.

Still, many of the actors manage to rise above the material to present compelling characterizations. Mr. Jackson does not disappoint. Smiling or scowling, he stays in the moment and communicates so clearly what he would have us know of his character, even while spouting lines such as "Too black for the uniform and too blue for the brothers."

Jeffrey Wright is nothing short of amazing as drug dealer Peoples Hernandez. Taking a role that could have easily become a scenery chewing caricature, Mr. Wright breathes so much inner life into the man that he serves up a complex and complicated character which was riveting to watch.

The language of the dialogue is coarse, as harsh as I've heard in recent months. Whether such profanity is needed to communicate the ideas of the script, I'll leave for others to debate. Be advised that it is present (and present in great abundance). Violence too, can get a bit graphic although many of the gunplay scenes evoke a familiar feeling of deja vu, as if they were intentionally filmed to mimic the cheesy "mock fight scenes" that were prevalent in these kinds of films during the 1970's.

Of all the reasons man has concocted to hate one another, the color of one's skin has got to be the most ridiculous. Racial prejudice is indefensible. Not only is it unsupported by Scripture... it is actually contradicted by God's Word.

And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation; Acts 17:26 (KJV)

Scriptures also state that God looks not upon the outward countenance of a man, but upon the heart. If we as a people could just learn to emulate God in this matter, we would all be the stronger for it. As the song says, "Can you dig it?"

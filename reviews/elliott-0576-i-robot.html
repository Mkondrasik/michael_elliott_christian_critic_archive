Author-verified transcription

The credits of I, Robot inform us that it was “inspired by” the books of sci-fi visionary Isaac Asimov. What this means is that the filmmakers borrowed one of Asimov’s titles, the name of one of his central characters, and the three laws of robotics that govern his robot stories. They then ignored everything else that Asimov has to offer in order to make the same special-effects-happy action movie that the Hollywood machine churns out every summer.

First Law: A robot may not injure a human being, or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.

The year is 2035 and robots have been successfully introduced into the human experience. They serve as cooks, nannies, butlers, delivery men, etc. and, because of the first law that is hard wired into their circuitry, the world seems a safer and better place. Not so to Detective Del Spooner (Will Smith). For personal reasons, he has formed a prejudice against the humanform robots being mass-produced by US Robotics. He also rebels against futuristic technology by insisting on living in his own retro world filled with blatant 2004 corporate product placements.

Second Law: A robot must obey orders given it by human beings, except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.

When Spooner is called to investigate the apparent suicide of USR’s founder (James Cromwell), he immediately suspects robotic foul play. His suspicions are ignored or ridiculed by all which, according to the laws of Hollywood, means that he’s the sole voice of reason and must find a way to prove that everyone else is wrong.

Third law: A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Spooner soon finds himself on the short end of a big battle as the robots, or the one controlling them, seek to silence him and end his investigation through whatever means necessary – laws or no laws.

Smith, still showing the beefed-up muscle tone from Ali, seems a bit constrained as Spooner. But enough of his wise-cracking hero persona from Independence Day and Men in Black bleeds through to make his character work as an action hero. Bridget Moynahan costars as Susan Calvin, a robotics expert who slowly comes to accept the possibility of Spooner’s concerns. Alan Tudyk is the behind the scenes talent represented by the CGI robot character of Sonny who appears to hold the key to unlocking the mystery.

One of the greatest pleasures of reading Asimov’s robot stories is seeing how he so adeptly applies reason and logic to the unfolding of the mystery at hand. In I, Robot the logic is stretched thin and reduced to a less prominent role in favor of more popular movie elements like CGI effects, large explosions, and daring high-speed stunts. Those expecting an intelligent, thoughtful treatment on the moral and ethical questions Asimov explores in his stories will be disappointed. Those simply looking for a polished and exciting cinematic thrill ride should find the film to their liking.

The former group might care to consider a recurring theme which is briefly touched upon: the idea of purposeful creation. Sonny questions why he is different than the other robots of his kind. It is a question that each of us could ask. It is our similarities that draw us together to unite us but it is our differences that make us unique within the collective group. We function best when we utilize that which makes us unique for the benefit of all.

If the whole body were an eye, where were the hearing? If the whole were hearing, where were the smelling? 1 Corinthians 12:17 (KJV)

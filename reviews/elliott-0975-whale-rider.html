Whale Rider

Sermon / spiritual topic: Tradition; Purpose

Author-verified transcription

Whale Rider introduces us to an unfamiliar culture using the most familiar of issues... the generational schism that exists in all cultures. Progress butts heads with tradition in every community and so any story featuring this conflict is a universal one.

The Maori tribe of New Zealand exists as a patriarchal society. The chief passes the leadership of his people through his bloodline as his first born male child is groomed to one day be chief. Thus it has been for hundreds of years. Until now.

When the wife of the chief’s son, Porourangi (Cliff Curtis, Collateral Damage), dies during childbirth along with her newly born son, there is little consolation that the twin daughter survives. In a patriarchal world, it would seem that a daughter has little to offer.

Porourangi, partly in grief and partly in defiance, leaves behind the tribe, his responsibilities, and Koro, his stubbornly insensitive father, in order to start a new life in Europe. He also leaves his daughter to be raised by her traditionally minded grandparents.

The movie jumps forward to show the daughter, Pai (Keisha Castle-Hughes), now eleven, seeking ways to prove herself worthy of her grandfather’s love and respect. Koro (Rawiri Paratene, Rapa Nui) certainly loves her but absolutely refuses to entertain the notion that a girl might be the next one to lead the tribe.

Finally recognizing that his son will not be returning to assume his leadership role, he begins to train the available young Maori boys in the old ways, hoping that one will show the qualities of a ruler. Pai, who refuses to accept her role as a second class tribesman, secretly trains alongside them and patiently waits for her destiny to be revealed.

Director/screenwriter Niki Caro (Memory & Desire), adapted her movie from a novel by New Zealand author Witi Ihimaera. She allows us to emotionally connect with both the young girl and her grandfather, who is less a villain here than he is a stubborn and unenlightened man.

Much of the credit for the emotional impact of the film belongs to the outstanding cast who present real and understandable characters with whom we can bond. Keisha Castle-Hughes, in her film debut, is heartbreaking as she silently suffers the dismissal of her uncompromising grandfather. Rawiri Paratene as the elder chief lends a sense of authoritative dignity to the role without becoming stereotypical. Vicky Haughton (Her Majesty), as Pai’s grandmother, serves as an important bridge between Pai and Koro, as well as allowing us to see how women have thus far functioned in the Maori world.

We can’t be too hard on Koro. Men can become so locked into traditional ways of doing things that we are blind to the entrance of something new. Just as Koro refused to consider the idea of his granddaughter as chief heir apparent, the apostle Peter first rejected the notion that Gentiles could be fellow heirs with Christ.

And he said unto them, Ye know how that it is an unlawful thing for a man that is a [Judean] to keep company, or come unto one of another nation; but God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean. Acts 10:28 (KJV)

God, of course, is not bound by the limitations man will so often place upon himself. He has given his revelation to men and women alike, rich and poor, Judean and Gentile... He will call to service anyone with the meekness to recognize His voice and the boldness to carry out His will. While we must be firm in our beliefs, we must also be flexible in translating those beliefs into action. Tradition must never stand in the way of truth.

Author-verified transcription

General Douglas MacArthur, in his emotional farewell address to Congress, noted that "Old soldiers never die... They simply fade away." In the case of the new movie, Soldier, we can only pray that he was telling the truth. This Soldier can't fade fast enough for me.

Set in an unappealing future, this society's armed forces are selected upon birth. Babies identified to have the proper genetic qualities are removed from the general population and indoctrinated to become the ultimate fighting machines. Completely unemotional, ruthless in battle, totally committed to the missions assigned them, these soldiers have known no equal... Until now.

Thanks to genetic manipulation, soldiers of the future can be designed instead of being selected after birth. The old soldiers have become obsolete, being replaced by a superior, genetically engineered brand.

The superiority of this new breed was dramatically demonstrated during a training exercise that left the best of the old breed near death. This fine specimen of a warrior, who had served so well in the past, was discarded like a bag of trash, left for dead on a barren planet that serves as a waste disposal site. As MacArthur promised, this old soldier didn't die... But neither is he about to fade away.

A stony-faced Kurt Russell (Breakdown) is Sergeant Todd, the old soldier who refuses to die. I realize that he's playing a character who was trained to bury all his emotions, but still... A plastic GI Joe shows more personality than does Mr. Russell here. No wonder the other characters all thought Todd was dead... There were times, no doubt, when the audience entertained those same thoughts.

Sean Pertwee (Event Horizon) and Connie Nielsen (The Devil's Advocate) play a pair of peaceful inhabitants of the planet where Todd is so ignominiously dumped. Gary Busey (Under Siege) shows up (but does very little else) in the film as the captain of the old regiment. Jason Isaacs (Dragonheart) is the colonel for whom this new generation of fighting men is a pet project. Of course, it is he that causes all the problems, without which there isn't much of a movie. (Then again, even with all the problems, there isn't much of a movie.)

Jason Scott Lee (Dragon: The Bruce Lee Story) plays Caine 607, one of the new breed of soldiers (and the one that almost killed Todd during the training exercise.) This movie operates under a strange correlation principle. The bigger one's muscles, the less one has to put into building a character. Caine 607 has really big muscles.

The screenplay by David Webb Peoples (Unforgiven) could have been scripted by any number of writers. In fact, had they stopped the film halfway through, I'd venture to say that most of the audience could have pieced the rest of the movie together with a 95% accuracy rate, it was that predictably obvious.

Director Paul Anderson (Mortal Combat) keeps the explosions and fight sequences coming at a rapid clip, which is a good thing because he left little time for us to dwell on the movie's inconsistencies and flaws in logic.

The early scenes were the most intriguing as we see the indoctrination of the baby soldiers-to-be and follow their "training" from infancy to adulthood. The soldiers they became was a direct result of the continual teaching and reinforcement of that teaching that they received. The scripture that immediately comes to mind is:

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it. Proverbs 22:6 (KJV)

As parents, we have the responsibility to train, teach, and guide our children within the parameters that God has clearly set for us so that they may rise to be their best. We must be as relentless as the "teachers" in the movie, not so as to raise a family of trained killers, but to raise children that love and respect both God and His Word. Build their habits now so when they are of age, they will be prepared to stand on their own.

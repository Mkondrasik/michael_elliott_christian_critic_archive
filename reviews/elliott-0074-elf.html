Elf

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 7, 2003
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Believing; faith; spiritual power

Author-verified transcription

Holiday season must be fast upon us. The first sugary-sweet Christmas movie has already hit theaters. By saying Christmas, I’m referring to the secular, commercialized holiday where everyone drinks eggnog, exchanges presents, and tries to capture that special feeling called Christmas spirit. Elf does it as well as any movie of its kind.

The film tells the story of Buddy (Will Ferrell) who, as a baby, crawled into Santa’s bag one Christmas Eve and was mistakenly taken to the North Pole. Since he was an orphan, the elves decided to keep him and raise him as one of their own. When Buddy turned thirty, it became clear that he was more than an elf with a glandular problem.

Buddy was told the truth - that he was a human whose mother had died and whose father didn’t know he existed. Buddy decided to go in search of his birth father, who, as it turns out, lives and works in Manhattan. Much to Buddy’s shame, his dad has also been placed on Santa’s naughty list.

The rest of the film is a typical fish out of water tale. Buddy the Elf, full of irrepressible joy and delight, meets the human world filled with cynicism, greed, and distrust. Guess who changes whom?

Elf is a feel good holiday movie with charm to spare. Will Ferrell is perfectly cast as the ever cheerful, overly-excited, human elf. Even if a joke or gag is lacking, Ferrell’s consistency in his characterization sells it to us and makes it work. It’s a giddy performance.

Just as important to the film are the actors who must respond to this six foot three man dressed in green felt. James Caan plays the Scrooge figure as Walter, Buddy’s workaholic human dad, who doesn’t know what to make of this elfish addition to his life. Bob Newhart narrates the film and appears as Papa Elf, Buddy’s adopted father, with deadpan hilarity. Also in the cast are Zooey Deschandel as Buddy’s love interest, Ed Asner as the jolly old elf, Santa, and Mary Steenbergen as Walter’s wife.

Director Jon Favreau keeps Elf as light as a snowflake throughout its 95 minute running time, relying on Ferrell’s twinkle to carry the film. Holiday movie buffs will also be delighted to recognize that he’s included some animated characters from the beloved Rankin-Bass TV specials (Rudolph The Red-Nosed Reindeer and Santa Claus is Coming To Town).

Parents should note that there is behavior in the film that you will not want your young ones to emulate. Buddy’s diet consists of putting syrup on everything and he is delighted to discover that people will leave their only partially used gum for him to find. Still, Elf is remarkably inoffensive in its content.

The secret to Santa’s flying sleigh is the magical power of Christmas spirit, although over the years a turbo booster has been added. According to the “Claus-o-meter” fewer and fewer people are believing in Santa Claus. When asked why he doesn’t appear on TV, Santa replies simply, “It’s not about seeing... It’s about believing.” And with believing at an all time low, Santa’s sleigh doesn’t have enough power to get off the ground.

Perhaps it’s a silly analogy, but the biblical principle holds true. Believing is the key to power. It is believing that gets us born again of God’s spirit. It is believing that activates our prayers. It is believing that gives us the strength to endure difficulties and overcome obstacles. It is believing that empowers us to live a life that is more than abundant.

As we believe in God, in His son Jesus Christ, and in the Word that He has given us, the true spirit will live in our heart forever.

Jesus said unto him, If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth. Mark 9:23 (KJV)

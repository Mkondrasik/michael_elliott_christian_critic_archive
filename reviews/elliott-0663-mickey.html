Author-verified transcription

John Grisham is famous for his legal thrillers which have become as close as we get to a sure thing in the Hollywood adaptation sweepstakes. In his newest film, written directly for the screen, he breaks out of his traditional genre to explore one of his long-held passions: Little League Baseball.

In Mickey, a devoted father (Harry Connick, Jr.) spends long hours coaching, encouraging, and driving his son to become the best twelve-year-old pitcher in the Little League system. But when it comes time to prove his worth in the All-Star game, dear old Dad has a surprise for the young phenom. They have to leave town.

It seems dad has fudged a few figures on his tax returns. The IRS (described in the film as the "bad guys") are breathing down his neck and threatening jail time. The boy agrees to take it on the lam with his father, sacrificing his last year of Little League eligibility.

But wait... When dad arranges for their new identities, he discovers that the boy's new birth certificate shows him as being a year younger. When they arrive in their new home of Las Vegas, the first thing dad does is to scout which Little League ball teams show the best potential for having a winning season. He then strikes a deal with the coach of the Moose and has his boy, now called Mickey (Shawn Salinas), perform badly at the draft trials so no other team will choose him.

Everything is working like a charm until Mickey begins dominating the sport with his 80 mph fastball. He becomes a media sensation and takes his Vegas team all the way to Williamsport to compete in the Little League World Series.

Meanwhile, an IRS agent has been tracking down these scofflaws with the tenacity of Les Miserables' Inspector Javert. With the national press Mickey has been getting, it wasn't hard to find them.

What was hard was finding a distributor for this film. Grisham and director Hugh Wilson finally made the decision to release it themselves. In this case, the studios really did know best. There is much that is problematic about the film.

The protagonists are liars and cheaters. Their motivations are purely selfish. A father involves his son in a federal crime. A coach agrees to an immoral scheme to land a star pitcher. A school principal doesn't act upon the news a student at her school is purposely repeating a grade because she's dating his father who is wanted for tax fraud. Adults allow a 12-year-old boy with a bruised and possibly broken hand (from catching Mickey's "heat") to stay in the game. We are supposed to cheer for Mickey leading his team to the championship because they're facing the Cubans and everyone knows that they are cheating.

The moral implications of this film are indeed troubling which makes it difficult to recommend this PG rated film. Artistically, the film also suffers from inconsistency. Connick and Salinas give respectful if somewhat bland performances but most of the supporting roles are filled by less talented actors.

Still, were it not for the misleading messages of the script, Mickey would be an enjoyable family film if just for the scenes of the various ball games played during the championship season.

The lesson to remember is that though it is just a game, the disciplines used to play it well can continue to serve us in all areas of life. That is why how we play is more important than if we win. There will always be those who place winning above everything else and will be content to take "shortcuts" to ensure "victory." Such victories are meaningless and even damaging to one's life.

"What benefit did you reap at that time from the things you are now ashamed of? Those things result in death!" Romans 6:21 (NIV)

In the long run, it is far better to play honestly and by the rules regardless of the outcome than it is to cheat and win the game. Somehow, that message should have been slipped into Mickey.

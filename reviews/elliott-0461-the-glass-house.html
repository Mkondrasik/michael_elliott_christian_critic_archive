Author-verified transcription

The problem with glass houses is that one can see everything that is going on inside. The problem with The Glass House is that the audience also sees everything... long before it happens. For a "psychological thriller," that is not a good thing.

Leelee Sobieski (Here On Earth) stars as Ruby Baker, a moderately rebellious sixteen-year-old whose life is thrown into turmoil when her parents are killed in a car accident. After the funeral, Ruby and her brother Rhett (Trevor Morgan, Jurassic Park III) are turned over to the Glasses, family friends who have been chosen to be the guardians of the newly orphaned youngsters and their four million dollar trust fund.

Encased in their glass, postmodern Malibu home, Terry Glass (Stellan Skarsgård, Ronin) and his wife Erin (Diane Lane, Hardball) appear at first to be ideal guardians. She's a doctor and he's involved in California's glamorous entertainment industry. But faster than we can say 'plot twist,' Ruby starts suspecting that there's more to the Glasses than meets the eye.

Though the film shows a bit of promise in its early scenes, especially with its realistic depiction of the Baker family before the accident, it quickly dissolves into an incomprehensible mess. Exhibiting self-defeating behavioral characteristics that are simply unexplainable, the Glasses prove to be half-empty (i.e. they are a few french fries short of a happy meal).

Leelee Sobieski does little more than peer around glass corners and stare blankly with a nondescript expression practiced to perfection by most teenagers. Unfortunately, it does not lend itself to a compelling or interesting performance.

For that we must turn to the adults. Skarsgård and Lane are clearly talented performers but they are so badly hampered by the script and the course of action their characters take that neither one of them can pull this film out of its mediocrity.

With apologies to the human members of the cast, the most effective characterization belongs to the house itself. Cold, austere, and uninviting despite its opulence, it makes for a rather spooky setting.

Screenenwriter Wesley Strick (Cape Fear) and director Daniel Sackheim in his feature film debut add nothing original to this apparently tired genre. By the final reel, any hope of surprising developments coming forward were abandoned by the audience as they greeted the absurd ending with hoots of derision.

To try and salvage something of value from this experience, we must return to what was apparently a prominent theme of the film: Mainly, that we often don't see or appreciate the things that are right before our eyes. Ruby's parents were the bane of her teen aged existence... setting rules, offering unwanted advice, getting on her case about frivolous things like homework... She simply didn't understand how good she had it until it was gone.

God recognizes the tendencies of man and provides exhortation designed to strengthen areas where we might be weak. To help us keep our eyes open so we don't fail to appreciate what we have, He gives us this reminder:

In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you. 1 Thessalonians 5:18 (KJV)

The world's distractions, especially the obstacles and difficulties which we face, can consume us to the point that we are oblivious to everything else. Having a mindset whereby we consciously make it a point to recognize those things of which we should be thankful - an attitude of gratitude - will help keep us focused upon the positive things in our lives. Regret can be a painful thing to carry. It is an unnecessary burden.

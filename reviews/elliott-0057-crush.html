Crush

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
April 21, 2002
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Love; fear; secrecy; honesty; relationships

Author-verified transcription

Bridget Jones gets a peek into the future as three single, forty-something, English woman bond in Crush. Meeting regularly to exchange tears and laughter by relating their sad, pathetic stories about the sad pathetic men they have the misfortune to date, they swizzle gin and wine and down chocolate by the gross. At least they have each other. Or at least they do for the first 20 minutes of the picture.

Kate (Andie MacDowell, Harrison’s Flowers) is the never married head mistress of a private English school. Her gal pals include Janine (Imelda Staunton, Shakespeare in Love), the single-mom local constable and Molly (Anna Chancellor, Four Weddings and a Funeral), a money-loving physician and three time divorcee. Together they find “strength” by commiserating with each other in their weaknesses.

When the fortyish Kate begins a sexual relationship with an ex-student that is half her age, her friends, figuring that estrogen is stronger than any other bodily fluid that might be exchanged, make it their job to break up the “couple” before any real damage gets done. Their misguided attempts lead to unforeseen consequences that will forever change the way they look at life and each other. At least, we can hope that it will.

Written and directed by first timer John McKay, I doubt if Crush truly depicts an accurate look into the female psyche at age forty. Nevertheless, the trio of actresses share a nice chemistry and do provide moments of warmth and humor.

Andie MacDowell, as the unmarried, middle-aged headmistress who finds herself unexpectedly falling in love with a man twenty years her junior, balances the almost youthful giddiness she feels at her sexual reawakening with an understandable embarrassment over the social implications of her love affair. But McKay is not interested in exploring the nuances of a May/December romance. The emphasis of this film remains on the friendship between the ladies and pity the poor sod who may try to come between them.

The female camaraderie and ensemble work done by the three actresses is, at times, enjoyable to watch. Anna Chancellor as Molly, with a sharp wit and ready barb, is the dominant personality and sets a quick pace to all scenes in which she plays a part. Imelda Staunton is humorously expressive as Janine. Kenny Doughty (Titus), as Jed the love interest whose organ playing is the source of most of the film’s double entendres, smolders with sexual energy but lacks the depth of a more defined character.

Perhaps the love relationship in the film might have been more believable if we had seen it develop beyond the quick “slap and tickle” sexual stage. But we did not. Thus, all the professions of love, romance and matrimony that follow strike us as being foolish and naive.

Anyone who has lived into their forties should certainly know that sex and love are not interchangeable terms. If nothing else, the fact that Kate felt their “love” needed to be kept hidden from the rest of the town should have served as a warning that perhaps what she was feeling was not the pull of her heart but the stirrings of a region a bit further south.

There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love. 1 John 4:18 (KJV)

Love should make us fearless. It is never anything of which we need be ashamed. It certainly isn’t anything to keep hidden or secret... not if the love is genuine.

Open rebuke is better than secret love. Proverbs 27:5 (KJV)

Dealing honestly and openly in situations, even those uncomfortable to discuss, is a far better course of action than building a secret life that one must keep hidden from view.

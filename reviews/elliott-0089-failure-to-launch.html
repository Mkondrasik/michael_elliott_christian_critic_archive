Failure to Launch

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 10, 2006
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

2 Corinthians 1:3-41 Thessalonians 5:11Mark 10:7

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Comfort; strength; parenting; maturity; encouragement

Author-verified transcription

Some people just don't want to leave their comfort zone.

The romantic comedy, Failure to Launch, shows us that a comfort zone may not only be UN-comfortable. It can be an emotionally crippling thing.

Tripp (Matthew McConaughey) is a successful, attractive, and personable 35-year old man who might be considered the most eligible bachelor in his town but for one small detail – he's still living at home with his parents.

For Tripp, it's the perfect arrangement. A beautiful home, cheap living quarters, a built-in maid and housekeeper and an easy way out of any relationship that looks like it might be getting too serious. When a girl gives him “the look” that says she's ready for the next step, he brings her home to meet his roommates: Mom and Dad. It's a sure fire way to kill a budding relationship.

It's a perfect arrangement except for the fact that Mom (Kathy Bates) and Dad (Terry Bradshaw) are ready for their little bird to leave the nest and they aren't beyond shoving him out.

They hire a professional interventionist (Sarah Jessica Parker) whose theory is that adult men who still live with their parents must suffer from low self esteem. Her plan is to take them through a maturation process by having them fall in love with her. When she is done, they are ready to move on with their lives (and out of their parents' houses). Tripp becomes her latest client and her biggest challenge.

Romantic comedies live or die on the likability of the lead characters and in Failure to Launch, McConaughey and Parker pull out all the stops to present themselves in the most charming and flattering light possible. It can be a bit disingenuous at times but overall... it works.

They are sweet, funny and likable people. And so we are willing to overlook a few flaws or inconsistencies in the script because of them.

We need not overlook anything in the subplot between her moody roommate Zooey Deschanel and his sad sack friend Justin Bartha. Their unlikely relationship, cemented by the conspiratorial elimination of a troublesome mockingbird, is very amusing.

Bates and Bradshaw are a bit of an odd couple, with Bradshaw following Bates' (About Schmidt) lead by dropping his trousers and walking around naked for an uncomfortably extended period of time.

Tripp's “comfort zone” extends to the roof and four walls provided by his parents. What he fails to understand is that what he's receiving is not comfort at all.

The word “comfort,” as originally coined, actually meant to strengthen. It did not mean to pamper, to coddle, or to ease. Tripp's living at home is hardly strengthening his life. It has kept him in a holding pattern and prevented him from maturing or developing as an independent soul.

God, our heavenly father, is all about providing comfort which is strength.

Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort (strength); who comforteth (strengthens) us in all our tribulation that we may be able to comfort (strengthen) them which are in any trouble, by the comfort (strength) wherewith we ourselves are comforted (strengthened) of God. 2 Corinthians 1:3-4 (KJV)

As parents, our role is to strengthen our children so that they may be able to stand on their own and eventually provide strength to others.

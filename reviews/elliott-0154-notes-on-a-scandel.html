Notes on a Scandel

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
January 26, 2007
Rating
2 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Titus 2:11-121 John 2:15-17Colossians 3:5-10

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Selfishness

Author-verified transcription

Two masterful performances cannot make up for the disturbing and distasteful content in Richard Eyre's new film Notes on a Scandel. Based on the novel by Zoe Heller, it tells the story of two women, both of whom are teachers in a British secondary school and both of whom make perverse choices that are simply indefensible.

Barbara Covett (Judi Dench), an opinionated teacher who looks upon her peers and students alike with an obvious disdain, sees the new art teacher, Sheba Hart (Cate Blanchett) in a somewhat different light. "She's the one I've been waiting for," she writes in her diary as she sets out to forge an intimate relationship with the younger educator.

The intimacy is based upon Barbara's discovery of Sheba's indiscretion - a sexual affair that she is having with Steven Connolly (Andrew Simpson), one of her fifteen-year-old students. Barbara agrees to keep Sheba's secret on a number of conditions. And Sheba has little choice but to agree to them. But can she?

Judi Dench shows the depth of her talent in this starring role, stretching her skills far beyond what has been required of her in the many supporting characters she has played in recent memory. She never makes Covett an object of sympathy but neither does she paint her as a depiction of pure evil. She is a sexual predator to be sure but Dench focuses on the loneliness and social isolation of the character making her somewhat of a pathetic individual.

Blanchett has the equally difficult task of making her sexual attraction towards a fifteen year old boy somehow understandable. She doesn't completely succeed. Not even her character can explain why she has done what she did. But Blanchett does make us believe that such a relationship exists (in the context of the story). The male characters are neither as intriguing nor as fleshed out. Bill Nighy plays Sheba's older husband (who ironically was Sheba's instructor when she was a student) and Andrew Simpson plays the object of Sheba's desires. Both are adequate in their respective roles but take a back seat to the primary focus of the film which is the relationship between the two women and the actions they take.

But no matter how accomplished the actors may be in presenting their characters, we cannot escape the fact that the story being told is neither edifying nor particularly enjoyable. All we can do, if we are looking to come away with something for our 90 minute investment, is treat the film as an object lesson of what not to do.

Both Barbara and Sheba suffer from the same malady that warps their decision making process. They are selfish. Their choices revolve around their lusts, wants, and desires without their thinking about the irreparable harm they may be doing to others.

Love, which is woefully absent in this film, is the opposite. When we act out of love we act unselfishly, thinking of what might bless or please others despite the sacrifices we might have to make. Those "sacrifices" may include forsaking the "if it feels good, do it" philosophy that some in this world preach. God preaches a different message.

For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men, teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world; Titus 2:11-12 (KJV)

To do so will require us to put on an unselfish and giving mindset. The rewards to this are many... not the least of which will be the avoidance of the kinds of consequences seen in the movie.

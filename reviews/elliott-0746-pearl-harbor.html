Author-verified transcription

For Pearl Harbor it would appear that director Michael Bay (Armageddon) and producer Jerry Bruckheimer (Con Air) have decided to take the James Cameron (Titanic) approach to historical drama. The aerial attack on Pearl Harbor is given the pure Hollywood treatment... lock, schlock, and barrel roll.

The tale begins in Tennessee of all places, with two young boys who do little else than dream of becoming pilots. Best friends Rafe (Ben Affleck, Bounce) and Danny (Josh Harnett, Here On Earth) eventually grow up to join the US Army Air Corps.

With the war in Europe looming in the background, Rafe falls in love with Evelyn (Kate Beckinsale, Brokedown Palace). She's a Navy nurse, who after inoculating him, spends the next four weeks in a romantic tailspin during which they profess their undying love for one another.

Rafe, impatient to earn his wings as a fighter pilot, joins the Eagle Squadron, a division of the British RAF developed for US pilots who couldn't wait for their government to declare war like the rest of the world. He leaves, secure in the knowledge that Evelyn is being stationed in the Hawaiian "safe" zone of Pearl Harbor.

When news that Rafe's plane was shot down, Evelyn and Danny are devastated. In their grieving, they eventually find comfort in one another. Their relationship is allowed to develop and mature and they fall in love with each other. This is something that Rafe, who wasn't dead after all, has some difficulty accepting.

Naturally, this maudlin soap opera story takes a back seat once the bombs start falling. And fall they do. Pearl Harbor was reportedly granted the largest motion picture budget in history. Michael Bay and company did not scrimp when it comes to on-screen explosions.

Ben Affleck personifies the cocky, arrogant nature of a 1940's flyboy. Josh Harnett comes in a close second as a less confident, more awkward version of a military recruitment poster. Kate Beckinsale captures the perfect look of a WWII pinup. The best performance of the film comes from an unrecognizable Jon Voight (Varsity Blues) as FDR. Alec Baldwin (State and Main) is also on hand as Colonel James Doolittle who orchestrated the bombing raid on Tokyo, also pictured in this film.

All of the actors look the part. The attention to detail in picturing the WWII characters is incredible. Unfortunately, looks alone will not carry this picture and the actors are eventually done in by the weak and cliché-riddled script by Randall Wallace (Braveheart).

Still, the action sequences, when they come, are exciting and impressive. The three hour running time passes in what seems like two. Surprisingly enough, there are really no bad guys in the film. The Japanese attack is explained as being a regrettable course of action done by a nation in the face of economic ruin.

There are many aspects to this film which can be used to draw attention to spiritual truths. I suppose this is true because warfare lends itself to illustrating the spiritual battle which exists between good and evil. A quote which stayed in my mind, though in context it sounds admittedly "corny," was spoken by Alec Baldwin as Doolittle. During an inspirational moment, he told our two pilots who were facing a suicide mission (the Tokyo bomb run) that "victory belongs to those who believe in it the most and believe in it the longest."

For whatsoever is born of God overcometh the world: and this is the victory that overcometh the world, even our faith (believing). 1 John 5:4 (KJV)

We should never dismiss the power that can be exercised through our believing. Oftentimes, believing "the longest" is exactly what is needed to turn a situation from a defeat into a victory.

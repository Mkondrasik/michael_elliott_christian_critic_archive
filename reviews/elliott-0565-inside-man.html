Author-verified transcription

Spike Lee’s latest “joint” may very well be his most accessible to mainstream audiences. Inside Man is an above average heist movie with an intelligent plot and enough twists to keep an audience involved and guessing.

Denzel Washington stars as Keith Frazier, a police negotiator currently under investigation for some missing evidence. His automatic suspension is temporarily pushed aside when a group of armed robbers led by Dalton Russell (Clive Owen) take up residence in a bank, taking fifty innocent (and not so innocent) people hostage.

With partner Bill Mitchell (Chiwetel Ejiofor) in tow, Frazier begins the cat and mouse game with Russell where it slowly becomes evident to him that there’s more to this event that meets the eye.

Russell’s main ploy to dress robber and hostage alike in identical painter’s garb so that no one, including us, can be sure just who is who at any given moment. It is an effective gimmick that can be frustrating. It certainly is for Frazier who’s trying to piece this puzzle together.

Adding another element to the mix is the bank owner (Christopher Plummer) who hires a professional facilitator (Jodie Foster) to personally (and secretly) negotiate a side deal with Russell.

What Russell is really after and the means he employs to get it become clear (though not always obvious) as the film draws to a close. Lee doesn’t talk down to the audience but allows them the opportunity to exercise their minds to fill in the final pieces of the puzzle.

Denzel Washington adds another star performance to his burgeoning resume. Frazier is a good cop – but not so good that he isn’t above a little morality bending. Sharp, quick with a quip or two, Washington gives a relaxed and pleasant performance.

It is Clive Owen’s performance, however, that sets the tone of the film and keeps us involved in the narrative. He plays Russell as a grand chess master… or better yet… a champion poker player – one who keeps his cards close to his chest. He’s an enigma who draws us in as we try to guess exactly what he’s up to and what he’s capable of.

The remaining three “name” actors (Plummer, Foster, Dafoe) have relatively minor parts that don’t showcase the depth of their talents. Plummer plays a man with a secret. Foster plays a mysterious woman who uses her elitist charm to broker deals for high level clients. Dafoe plays the police officer in charge of the crime scene looking for the opportunity to storm the bank. Although these roles are small, having such high profile actors fill them make them look and feel bigger.

It is within Plummer’s character that we find our spiritual lesson. He is driven by a desire to keep his past hidden, justifying (and obscuring) it by a record of good works and philanthropy. Perhaps he never read Shakespeare: “The evil that men do lives after them; the good is oft interred with their bones.”

There is only one who can wipe away the past. Only one who can provide the forgiveness that lightens our minds and hearts. It is not something we can broker for ourselves. We needed a mediator by the name of Jesus Christ.

For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; Who gave himself a ransom for all, to be testified in due time. 1 Timothy 2:5-6 (KJV)

His blood washed our sins from God’s sight and memory. He gave us a clean slate with which to live the rest of our lives. Instead of hiding from the past, we can now fully embrace our futures.

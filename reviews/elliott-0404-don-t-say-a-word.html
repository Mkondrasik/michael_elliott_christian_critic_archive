Don't Say A Word

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
September 28, 2001
Rating
2 stars
Running time
1 hr: 50 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Sermon / spiritual topic: Giving; deliverance; helping others

Author-verified transcription

Don't Say A Word follows a basic Hollywood recipe for a tense crime/thriller. Take one conscientious man with a stable and loving home life, then threaten to take that away from him unless he solves a mystery within an impossible time limit. This timeworn recipe still works although the finished product now resembles fast food grub more than elegant cuisine. It'll do in a pinch but there's little reason to go back for seconds.

Michael Douglas (Traffic) plays Dr. Nathan Conrad, loving husband and father as well as a renown psychiatrist specializing in troubled teens. His life is disrupted one Thanksgiving Day when his eight year old daughter (Skye McCole Bartusiak, The Patriot) is abducted from their home. The kidnappers' demand? That he do his job... work with a troubled teen whose mind contains a six digit number that they want. The catch is that he's only got until 5 p.m. to discover what that number is.

Aware that his every move is being watched, Conrad walks a fine line to help the teen who is being terrorized by the same men while simultaneously working towards giving the kidnappers what they want in order to keep his daughter alive.

Director Gary Fleder (Kiss the Girls) seems to have a feel for this kind of film. Although it becomes unraveled in the latter reels, Don't Say A Word opens very strongly and keeps its audience involved and rooting for the central characters.

The most nerve-wracking sequence in the film has to do with Conrad's wife (Famke Janssen, X-Men) who is bedridden thanks to a ski accident which has left one leg in a hip to ankle cast... easy prey for unscrupulous felons who may wish pay her an unwelcome visit. As proved by Sorry, Wrong Number and Wait Until Dark, having an invalid as a potential victim adds to the suspense and tension because fleeing from the danger is removed as an option.

Michael Douglas looks great as the family man/psychiatrist although it is doubtful that "speed psychoanalysis" will ever catch on as a suggested treatment. Brittany Murphy plays Elisabeth, the troubled teen who witnessed her father's murder 10 years earlier and has carried a secret ever since. She does a passable job, complete with blank-eyed stares, singsong delivery and nervous twitches.

Skye McCole Bartusiak is precociously cute as Jesse, Conrad's eight year old daughter. Making her the same age as Elisabeth at the time her troubles began was an interesting choice which Fleder uses to build a rapport between Conrad and his patient. Sean Bean (Ronin) is appropriately sinister as a greedy thief turned kidnapper.

The holes in the plot are numerous, with some being large enough to swallow whole a less accomplished cast and crew. Thanks to their abilities, we are nicely distracted from dwelling upon the plot inconsistencies during the course of the action. It is as we reflect back upon the film that they become all too readily apparent. Still, Don't Say a Word pushes many of the right buttons for audiences seeking an uncomplicated traditional thriller.

It doesn't take Conrad long to determine that Elisabeth is feigning much of her mental illness to stay institutionalized and out of the clutches of the bad men.

The thing that breaks her from her self-imposed bondage of fear is her willingness to reach outside of herself to help save Conrad's daughter. Giving of one's self to help others is the best way I know to receive the deliverance one seeks.

And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends: also the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before. Job 42:10

I find it interesting to note that Job's deliverance came after he prayed.. not for himself but for the miserable comforters who were his friends. Having a heart of giving indicates meekness which is an attribute that God holds in highest esteem and will always reward.

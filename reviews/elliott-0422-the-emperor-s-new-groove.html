Author-verified transcription

Disney's The Emperor's New Groove is a film which refutes the oft argued preference of "style over substance." In an animated throwback to old style cartooning which is somehow similar to the work of the Warner Brothers' animator Chuck Jones (How The Grinch Stole Christmas), Disney's new film forgoes lush and computer-enhanced artwork for a simplistic yet expressive style, offsetting it with an intelligent and witty script along with voices that match. The end result is a film which may not be as polished as Disney's more recent films, but it is just as enjoyable if not more so.

Based on an original story, The Emperor's New Groove follows the exploits of Kuzco (voice by David Spade, TV's Just Shoot Me), the selfish and self-centered emperor of a mythical kingdom. Kuzco thinks nothing of having someone thrown out the window just because they interrupted his "groove." Such behavior elicits enemies and Kuzco has his share, most notably his evil advisor Yzma (Eartha Kitt, Harriet the Spy).

When Yzma attempts to assassinate Kuzco with a poisonous elixer, he is instead magically transformed into a llama. Kuzco then flees the castle and joins Pacha (voice by John Goodman, TV's Roseanne), a goodhearted peasant, whom he once tried to evict from his home in order to make way for Kuzcotopia, a royal hillside retreat. Together, they must evade Yzma and her dimwitted henchman Kronk (voice by Patrick Warburton, TV's Seinfeld) while making their way back into the castle to search for the antidote to the llama potion.

In the process, Cuzco learns about fairness, working together, and the feeling of community. His experience as a llama gives him the opportunity to become a better man.

The script's simplistic construction puts the emphasis not on story but on character. With this in mind, the vocal talent was chosen with great care and precision.

David Spade's acerbic personality fits well with the humorously sarcastic screenplay. His dry and casual delivery is contrasted by the ridiculous situations in which his character finds himself. Much of the humor is based in irony and may well escape the children, but it will certainly tickle the adults who have brought them.

John Goodman cashes in on his working class, "everyman" persona as Pacha, the peasant who, because of his wife, children, and the home they have built together, is a far richer man than the emperor could ever dream to be.

Eartha Kitt lends her voice to the kind of villainess we love to hate. she's obviously bad but not nightmare inducing. Deliciously evil and duplicitous, Yzma is well deserving of the comeuppance she is sure to get. Her simple-minded but affable sidekick Kronk is the source of much of the laughter in the film as his ineptitude continually thwarts Yzma's plans.

The Emperor's New Groove was originally conceived as a dramatic story inspired by an Incan legend. Initially titled Kingdom of the Sun, the project evolved 180 degrees during its developmental process to become this original and very funny comedy. The children will greatly enjoy the slapstick antics in the story which bring to mind some of the Bugs Bunny or Roadrunner vs. Wile E. Coyote cartoons of yesteryear. The 78 minute film is a slow starter but once the action is underway, it proceeds at a fast and steady pace.

The emperor Kuzco could be a poster boy for the "Me" generation. All his thoughts, and actions revolve around his wants and desires at the total exclusion of those around him. He will learn that having this mindset is a selfish and ultimately lonely way to go through life. The story of The Emperor's New Groove can be a gentle and easily entreated reminder to our children of the "Golden Rule" that we would all be well served to follow.

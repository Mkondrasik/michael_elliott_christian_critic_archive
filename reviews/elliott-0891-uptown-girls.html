Author-verified transcription

Party girl meets party pooper. Uptown Girls pits an irresponsible spoiled brat of an adult against an overly responsible and judgmental prig of a child. The result is mawkishly predictable and rather sloppily put together by director Boaz Yakin (Remember The Titans).

Brittany Murphy (8 Mile) stars as Molly Gunn, daughter of a legendary rock star who tragically died in a plane crash along with his wife when Molly was but a child. Now grown and the sole heir to his rock fortune, she lives in luxury with no responsibilities and no clue as to how the rest of the world functions. Her insulated life revolves around guys, gal pals, clubbing, and shopping.

All that comes to an end when she wakes up one day to find that her immense fortune is gone. Suddenly she is forced to contemplate the bizarre notion of actually having to work for a living. Ill-equipped for the workplace, the only position she is able to land is as a nanny for Ray, a music executive's precocious daughter (Dakota Fanning, I Am Sam). Whoever thinks that "girls just wanna have fun" has never met this child. Molly and Ray are, to put it mildly, completely incompatible.

But they somehow sense that each has something the other lacks. Molly has the fun-seeking, childlike spirit that is no where evident in Ray. Unlike Molly, Ray is centered, focused, disciplined and totally bland. Together, I suppose they make one well-rounded individual.

That's all supposition, of course. It's too much to hope that this film would make that much sense. And honestly, there's really no sense of character development taking place. The characters do change at some point, because the formulaic script calls for it, but we never really see why or how.

By breaking the film down and looking at its separate elements, it really had the potential to be a better film. The dewy-eyed Murphy can do pathos with the best of them and young Fanning is cute (even though she's working against it for most of the film). The premise isn't altogether a terrible one and director Yakin has successfully mounted a feel-good, sentimental tale in the past.

Still, putting those elements together proved troublesome here. Fanning's performance is flat and uninspired while Murphy's is all over the emotive map. The turning points of the film are so obviously contrived that they fail to move us or build our interest in the story's outcome. In the final analysis, there are too many continuity flaws, lapses of common sense logic, and character inconsistencies to overlook.

An iota of truth is spoken by Ray when Molly asks her why she spends so much time practicing her ballet moves and positions instead of just "rocking out" and having fun. The eight-year-old replies with wisdom beyond her years, "Fundamentals are the building blocks of fun."

True enough. People enjoy doing what they do well. Once we master the fundamentals of any given activity, we are free to grow and flourish within that activity, building upon the foundation which we have laid.

For every one that useth milk [is] unskilful in the word of righteousness: for he is a babe. But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, [even] those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil. Hebrews 5:13-14 (KJV)

This principle is sound and works in every category of life. We learn to crawl before we learn to walk. We learn to walk before we learn to run. All coaches will preach the fundamentals of their sport knowing that the game is more fun when played well. If it's true about dance and football... How about life itself? We must learn the fundamentals of life so we may enjoy it to its fullest.

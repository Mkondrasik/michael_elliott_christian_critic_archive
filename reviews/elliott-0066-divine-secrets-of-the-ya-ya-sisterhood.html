Divine Secrets of the Ya-Ya Sisterhood

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
June 7, 2002
Rating
3½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

1 John 1:9Proverbs 28:13Luke 6:37

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Forgiveness; confession; reconciliation; regret

Author-verified transcription

The secret has been known amongst women for some time now… Ya-Ya Sisterhood is indeed divine. Now, fans of the best selling Rebecca Wells novel will be pleased to revisit the world of Ya-Ya through director Callie Khouri’s film adaptation. Even those of us who do not qualify (by reason of gender) for sisterhood will find much to appreciate.

When playwright Sidda Walker (Sandra Bullock, 28 Days) grants an interview to Time Magazine, her published comments regarding her tumultuous Louisiana upbringing are not received well by her mother Vivi (Ellen Burstyn, Requiem For A Dream), who is admittedly tumultuous in her own right. Tensions escalate to the point where neither mother or daughter are speaking to the other.

Riding to the rescue is an unusual cavalry known as the Ya-Ya’s. They are a secret blood-oath society which, as the film states, was formed many, many, many, many moons ago by Vivi and her three closest friends: Teensy (Fionnula Flanagan, The Others), Necie (Shirley Knight, Angel Eyes) and Caro (Maggie Smith, Gosford Park) back when they were young, giggling girls.

The Ya-Ya’s know something that Sidda doesn’t, and they figure that before she completely throws away her relationship with her mother, the gaps in her understanding had best be filled. So they kidnap her and present her with the giant tome of Ya-Ya knowledge… a scrapbook containing a chronicle of their growing up and the friendship which has endured for over six decades.

Callie Khouri, who penned Thelma and Louise, makes her directorial debut and has assembled a dream cast of solid female talent who build a seamless ensemble. There isn’t a weak or careless performance amongst them. As the Ya-Ya’s, Ellen Burstyn, Fionnula Flanagan, Shirley Knight, and Maggie Smith convey to us the feeling that they’ve known and been around each other for ages.

Sandra Bullock does some fine work as Sidda, who bears the emotional scars of her childhood even though she isn’t aware of the full story behind those scars. Ashley Judd (High Crimes) succeeds in the very difficult and pivotal role of young Vivi who is grows to become the person Sidda describes as “the most charming, wounded person you ever met.” Judd shows us both the charm as well as the wounds.

Representing the male viewpoint, and wisely keeping in the background as the women strut their stuff is James Garner (Space Cowboys) as Vivi’s husband and Angus MacFadyen (Titus) as Sidda’s fiancé.

The constant flashbacks and the fact that multiple actresses play each character at various stages in the timeline can sometimes get a bit disorienting, but the strength of the characterizations and the richness of the relationships are such that is becomes easy to forgive any rough spots in the telling or structure of the story.

It is forgiveness, after all, which is the crux of the movie. Not only our willingness to forgive others for perceived wrongs they may have done to us, but also the need to forgive ourselves for the mistakes and weaknesses that are a part of our past.

Vivi, at one point, admits that there are some things she has done for which she does not expect forgiveness. She’s wrong. By burying those things and keeping them “secret,” instead of dealing with them openly and honestly, she has managed to perpetuate the pain of her past and pass that pain onto her daughter.

If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 John 1:9 (KJV)

Life is too short to spend it in a state of constant regret. If God is willing to forgive us for our transgressions, we owe it to ourselves to do the same.

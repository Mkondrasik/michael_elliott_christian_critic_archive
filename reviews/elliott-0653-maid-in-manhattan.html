Author-verified transcription

Formulaic films are predictable. They are also safe. When used by a competent craftsman, a good formula should result in a pleasant and enjoyable end product. Maid in Manhattan follows the Pretty Woman formula, which followed the My Fair Lady formula, which followed the Pygmalion formula... and so on.

Unlike Pretty Woman, our heroine comes from the world's second oldest profession... housekeeping. Jennifer Lopez (Enough) stars as Marisa Ventura, a single parent doing her best to raise a small child while cleaning up after the upper class in an upscale NY hotel. She, like her mother before her, is a maid.

Persuaded by a friendly coworker, Marisa indulges in a fit of fancy by trying on a guest's designer outfit. Wouldn't you just know that this would be the moment that she runs into Chris Marshall (Ralph Fiennes, Red Dragon), a bland but somehow charismatic politician running for office. He, of course, mistakes her for a hotel guest.

By letting him make that assumption, she allows him to fall in love with her. What follows is the typical Cinderella story where the working girl gets to be a princess for a day. Afterwards, she returns the glass slippers and takes her rightful place among the soot and ashes. But wait! Isn't that her Prince Charming searching the hotel for her?

As I said, it is predictable and mindless fluff which still somehow manages to elicit a smile from us. After all, no one would use these formulas if they didn't work.

Jennifer Lopez won't be winning any awards for her foray into Julia Roberts material but she is cute and spunky as Marisa and looks every inch the movie star when dolled up in thousand dollar threads.

Ralph Fiennes seems a bit stiff and awkward as the leading man. He may look the part of a Kennedy but lacks the personality that would put us in his corner.

Stanley Tucci (Road to Perdition) is amusing as Marshall's uptight campaign manager who unsuccessfully tries to run every aspect of his candidate's life. Bob Hoskins (Last Orders) is a commanding presence as Lionel, a hotel concierge.

Director Wayne Wang (The Joy Luck Club) seems an odd choice for such an unchallenging and rather fundamental exercise in storytelling but as mentioned earlier, even a proven formula requires a competent craftsman to properly mix the various ingredients. Wang has certainly proved himself to be competent and he mixes the elements of this film effectively.

Beneath the surface of this romantic comedy lies a relevant social and spiritual issue. Mainly, why do we judge people according to wealth, status, or position? Had Marisa not pretended to be a socialite, would Marshall have ever expressed interest in getting to know her better?

God continually warns us not to judge by appearances. He certainly doesn't.

But the LORD said unto Samuel, Look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; because I have refused him: for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart. 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV)

To be "no respecter of persons" means to treat and respect people by the same rule or measure. We are not to have different standards by which we consider people. Wealth, status, and social standing matter little in the larger context of life. We would all be better people if we would act as if we believed that.

Author-verified transcription

Perhaps it is due to the weight of audience expectation, but there seems to be a heaviness to the new film Unbreakable which dampens its overall impact. The high level of anticipation surrounding this film should come as no surprise. After all, Unbreakable was written, produced and directed by the same man who was the force behind last year's phenomenal hit The Sixth Sense. The two films also share the same leading man starring with a young boy amidst a plot which delves into the supernatural.

To be fair to Unbreakable, the similarities end there. This is not a sequel to The Sixth Sense. While they share some of the same elements, each film deserves the right to be judged on its own merits.

Security guard David Dunn (Bruce Willis, The Sixth Sense) is the sole survivor of a horrible train accident. Whether or not his marriage will survive is another story. Mysteriously distant from his wife Audrey (Robin Wright Penn, Message in a Bottle) and somewhat cold towards his son Joseph (Spencer Treat Clark, Gladiator) David simply doesn't have a clue as to why he's so sad and unfulfilled. Nor does he understand why he alone emerged from the train wreck completely unscathed.

Enter comic book collector Elijah Price (Samuel L. Jackson, Rules of Engagement) with an unusual answer. Born with a rare disease which makes his bones especially fragile, Elijah has long suspected that, somewhere in the world, there exists someone at the opposite end of that spectrum. Someone "unbreakable," A person who does not get sick or injured. Someone with "superhuman" capabilities.

He contends that David may be that someone. According to Elijah, who believes that comic books are merely an exaggerated look at real life, David is feeling unfulfilled because he has yet to embrace his "super hero" status. He is denying his true self.

And thus the film proceeds slowly, inexorably, towards the dénouement that leads us to the determination of whether David is indeed blessed with supernatural abilities or if Elijah is merely a crackpot who has read too many comic books.

It is a credit to M. Night Shyamalan that he not only makes this premise work as well as it does but that he makes it seem completely credible. By building into the script the complex and unresolved relationships between David and his family, he adds the extra spice to the screenplay, making Unbreakable a bit more than a trifling tale about the possible origin of a super hero legend.

Bruce Willis plays the reluctant hero in the same subdued manner that worked so well for him in The Sixth Sense. Here, however, instead of drawing us near and setting us up for the film's surprise ending, his almost zombie-like style keeps us at arm's length, impatiently waiting for the next plot revelation. Maybe it is a case of "Fool me once, shame on you. Fool me twice, shame on me," but when the unexpected plot twist at the end of Unbreakable finally came, we did not experience a jaw-dropping reaction of being blind-sided.

Still, Unbreakable manages to establish M. Night Shyamalan as being one of the more inventive and original storytellers in film today.

David's situation is one which many of us have experienced. How often in the Word of God are we reminded of the power that resides within us as God's children? How many times does the Word command us to live and act in a manner consistent with what God has called us to do, using the spiritual abilities God has given us? And yet how many times do we deny that power or ignore that calling?

Yet amid all these things we are more than conquerors and gain a surpassing victory through Him Who loved us. Romans 8:37 (Amplified)

We are more than super hero conquerors when we accept in our hearts the spiritual reality of our lives and the power which God through Christ has made available. It is the Word of God which defines who we are in Christ. And the truth of the Word is "unbreakable."

Author-verified transcription

What if they called a war and no one showed up to fight? Believe it or not, it almost happened. Joyeux Noel (Merry Christmas) tells the story of what has come to be known as the Christmas Truce of 1914. What was odd about this “truce” is that it wasn’t sanctioned by the leaders of any side.

Only five months into WWI and the men on the battlefield already knew what their commanders did not. This was not going to be a quick and easy war. As winter approached, German, French, and Scottish troops watched and waited for the weather to clear as they huddled in the trenches which constituted the front lines. They knew that, with the spring, the full assaults would once again begin. Until then, there was little to do but shiver in the cold and try to survive.

On Christmas Eve of 1914, an odd thing occurred. When a German tenor sang a Christmas carol as a reminder of life back home, the cheers and applause came from both sides of the battlefield. It wasn’t long before the men who, not too long ago were shooting at each other, were coming into the “no man’s land” which lay between them to exchange gifts, photos… anything at hand. The evening ended with a Latin mass attended by all sides.

This unusual armistice continued the next day as an agreement was reached enabling the men to retrieve the bodies of their dead so they might give them a proper burial. After they were done the soldiers thought a little football between the rival nations might be fun. If only all wars could be thus settled.

The phrase “insanity of war” has never been clearer than in Christian Carion’s Academy Award nominated film. While many of the characters are fictionalized composites, the film is based on actual events which took place up and down the front lines. It certainly depicts how the soldiers on both sides had more in common with each other than not.

Benno Furmann plays Sprink, the tenor whose voice drifted across enemy lines to enchant all who heard it. Diane Kruger plays his lover who arranged a special pass to visit her man and became part of and witness to the fraternization.

Guillaume Canet plays a sensitive French lieutenant whose wife may or may not have had a child. She is now behind enemy lines and contact has been impossible. Daniel Bruel plays his German counterpart with a similar compassionate air. Gary Lewis is the man of God called to deliver the strangest and most meaningful mass of his life.

The TV show M.A.S.H. had one of its characters offer this observation: “Sometimes I think it should be a rule of war that you have to see somebody up close and get to know him before you can shoot him.” Obviously if that were the case, there would be a lot less shooting.

And this is precisely the point that Carion makes and that the Christmas truce of 1914 illustrates. Wars may be waged by nations and powers but must be fought by individuals who, if left to their own devices, would more than likely have no quarrel at all with those on the other side of their gun barrel.

God reminds us of who our true enemy is:

For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places. Ephesians 6:12 (KJV)

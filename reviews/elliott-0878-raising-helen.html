Author-verified transcription

The gift that director Garry Marshall has is not that he's one of the great artistic visionaries of our time but rather that he's always been able to tap into the populist mindset of the average movie going public. His films from Pretty Woman to The Princess Diaries manage to strike an emotional chord in the audiences that flock to his films. That is why it is so disappointing to see him misfire the way he has in Raising Helen.

Helen (Kate Hudson) is a young party girl and executive assistant to the owner of an elite modeling agency. Her life consists of fashion, makeup, night life, and apparently being seen wherever Paris Hilton can be found.

That life changes when a car accident takes the life of her sister and brother-in-law, leaving their three children orphans. In the will, custody of the children goes to Helen instead of the supermom third sister Jenny (Joan Cusack). Helen is ill-equipped to function as an authority figure but she is willing to give it a try for the sake of the kids and the memory of her sister.

The screenplay follows a traditional pattern. After the initial shock of the premise wears off, the characters address the practical aspects of their new lives. First, Helen must find a new place to live as her studio apartment in Manhattan cannot accommodate her new responsibilities. Secondly, she must find a proper school in which to enroll her nieces and nephew, settling upon a Lutheran private school run by the hunky Pastor Dan (John Corbett).

Of course, her adjustment to her new role takes its toll and she loses her job at the prestigious modeling agency. And finally, all the kids have their own issues that must be addressed. Young Sarah was in the middle of learning how to tie her shoes and can't remember what her mother taught her. Henry refuses to play basketball any more because it was something he shared with his dad. And teenage Audrey has discovered boys and drinking.

It actually is the kind of film where Garry Marshall usually excels but what humor exists in this film plays flat and unfunny. The drama is of the maudlin variety. The tone is off, the pacing is slow, and the characters are uninteresting. Kate Hudson is certainly cute enough but isn't able to establish a sympathetic connection with the audience. The wonderful Joan Cusack gamely gives her best effort in playing a one dimensional character and John Corbett has trouble reaching even that one dimension.

The best performance in the film actually goes to 7 year old Abigail Breslin as Sarah. Of all the characters on screen, hers is the most emotionally honest.

The film is called Raising Helen because, like so many films of this kind (Uptown Girls, Baby Boom, etc) the focus is on the maturing of the grownup and how she learns to accept her role and responsibility. Though this movie isn't anything special, the message it contains is. Parents must be willing to continually put themselves in an unpopular position. They must be the disciplinarians, the authoritarians, the rule-makers and the rule enforcers. All must be done with love, of course, but those tasks are not always immediately appreciated by our children. And yet we must do them anyway.

Helen must go from being the "fun aunt" to being the one responsible for raising the children entrusted to her care. That's a serious responsibility that's not always fun to do... but it is always a joy to have.

Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby. Hebrews 12:11 (KJV)

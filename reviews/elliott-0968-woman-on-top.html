Author-verified transcription

The world has a saying: "The way to a man's heart is through his stomach." Watching how Penelope Cruz ("All About My Mother") operates in the kitchen, one could make a strong argument in favor of that theory. Then again, "Woman on Top" is only marginally about the food. In a badly written narrative which opens and closes the film, "Woman on Top" is described as a "story of love, motion sickness, and the art of cooking."

The Spanish-born Ms. Cruz, in her first English speaking film, is cast as Isabella Oliveira, a Brazilian beauty and culinary genius who specializes in hot Latin dishes. Her only problem is that she suffers from motion sickness so badly that the only way she can control her motion sickness is by controlling her motion. She can drive (poorly), but she cannot be a passenger, she can dance but only if she leads, and sexually speaking, she must be in the position from whence we get the title of the film.

The stereotypical Latin male is not particularly famous for his subservience, and Isabella's physical need to always be in control soon drives her husband Toninho (Murilo Benicio, "Orfeu") into the arms of another woman.

Upon discovering his infidelity, she leaves for California where her talents are eventually discovered and she is given a local cooking show called Passion Food which quickly replaces Rice-a-Roni as the newest San Francisco treat. Toninho tracks her to the US and tries to woo her back. He has competition from Cliff (Mark Feuerstein, "Rules of Engagement"), Isabella's clownish producer, who has been developing his own strong feelings for her.

All of this is rather trivial nonsense and the only thing which keeps us from tuning out completely is the incredible beauty of Ms. Cruz and the catchy Brazilian beat which is gently sprinkled throughout the film.

As one of the characters comments about Isabella's appearance on TV, "The camera loves her." The same is true about Ms. Cruz, who uses that knowledge to good advantage. It is just a shame that it could not be put to use in a finer film.

Besides the heavy sexuality and bad language, the filmmakers included a number of other factors of which we should take note. Isabella, following her failed marriage, retreats to her best friend in grade school, a transvestite named Monica (Harold Perrineau Jr., "The Best Man") who moved to San Francisco many years earlier. There is also a very strong element of spiritual error as Isabella and the residents of her Brazilian home of Bahia worship in the traditional ways of Candombl (the Bahian version of Santeria), relying on the whims of a fantasy sea goddess named Yemanja.

The film was directed by the award winning Venezuelan director Fina Torres ("Celestial Clockwork") and was written by Brazilian screenwriter Vera Blasi while she studied at the American Film Institute.

The hot Latin dishes being prepared by the hot Latin dish that is Ms. Cruz are supposed to represent the passion and energy for living and loving which resides in Isabella. It isn't the first time food has been used as a metaphor for a deeper meaning.

God has given us His Word not just to read, but to devour. To savour and enjoy as one would a seven course gourmet meal. With or without the Latin spices, the truth of God satiates all thirst and satisfies all hunger. It is the real "passion food."

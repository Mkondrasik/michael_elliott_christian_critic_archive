Author-verified transcription

In the beginning of Lord of War, when central character Yuri Orlov (Nicholas Cage) informs us that he plans on narrating the story without embellishing it to make himself look better, he wasn’t lying. Orlov is as cold hearted and disturbing a character as we’ve seen in theaters this year.

For much of his life, Yuri, the son of Ukranian immigrants, didn’t seem to appreciate the “land of opportunity” that America represents. He drifted from one meaningless endeavor to another… until he drifted into what would become the mechanism which propelled him to fame and fortune – arms dealing.

You’ve got to hand it to him. Once he settled upon his life’s dream, he dreamt big. Yuri wasn’t content with selling a gun here or there. He wanted to supply countries with enough weapons to start and wage a war. The morality of his life’s work never bothered him much. Of course, he would prefer those who used his weapons miss what they were shooting at. But the important thing is that they shoot. After all, bullets = dollars.

It doesn’t take long before Yuri is on top of the third world. He acquires enormous wealth and marries his fantasy girl (Bridget Moynahan), who asks next to no questions about his job. Being on top of the world isn’t always the most secure of positions. It is even less so when one’s clientele consists of ruthless dictators and despots.

Cage adopts a world weary tone of resignation in his narration. It may be an effective means of identifying his character but it got to be a bit monotonous. He shows more life as he acts out the story he’s telling – becoming a kind of used car salesman for advanced munitions.

Jared Leto plays Yuri’s troubled brother who, with Yuri’s help, falls prey to drug addiction. He also does the one thing of which Yuri seems incapable – he grows a conscience.

Ethan Hawke, as the straight-arrow federal agent trying to get the goods on Yuri, is a one-dimensional recruitment poster for the ATF (Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, and Firearms). Bridget Moynahan as a fashion model turned trophy wife wears the role well though it provides her with little to do.

As a provocation piece, Lord of War is effective. It raises our awareness of an ugly side of commerce that we might prefer didn’t exist. There are independently brokered (i.e. illegal) arms deals taking place around this world which places massively destructive weapons into the hands of people who have no moral compunction that would prevent them from using them unilaterally. The film closes with a coda that, if true, will a real eye opener for those inclined to stick their head in the ground to avoid unpleasant political realities.

As a piece of movie entertainment, Lord of War is not as successful. The film lacks a moral center which might have grounded the film for us. With the amoral Yuri as its heart, the film meanders and muddles its way from event to event without any real meaning or perspective.

That Yuri becomes wildly successful is no surprise. That kind of success always comes at a cost. God asks us to consider the following as we make decisions that will determine the course of our lives:

For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? Mark 8:36 (KJV)

For all his wealth, possessions, and notoriety, Yuri was a lost soul. And that is something that money can’t buy back.

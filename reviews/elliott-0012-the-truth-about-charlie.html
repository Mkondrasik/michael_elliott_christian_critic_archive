The Truth About Charlie

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
October 25, 2002
Rating
1½ stars

Original Author Copy

Author-verified transcription

The Truth About Charlie is a loose remake of the 1963 Stanley Donan film Charade. Loose because although the story and character names are similar, the styles couldn't be more different. Charade is a sophisticated, breezy gem of a film that delights even as it keeps audiences guessing. The sad truth about Charlie is that it's a jumbled, irritating mess of a movie.

Regina Lampert returns from a pampered vacation with plans to divorce her husband (the Charlie of the title). Divorce is never easy but in this case it proves impossible. His murder officially closed the door on any pending divorce.

Prior to his being killed, Charlie emptied out their spacious Parisian apartment as well as their bank account, leaving Regina with exactly nothing. Nothing except a police investigation and three mysterious strangers who want something they think Charlie has given her. On the bright side, she met a nice guy while on vacation who has followed her to Paris and might lend a helping hand. Or will he?

Thandie Newton (Mission Impossible II) assumes the Audrey Hepburn role of Regina while Mark Wahlberg (Rock Star) tries to fill the shoes of Cary Grant as Joshua Peters, the new man in Regina's life. Reprising the roles originally played by a pair of cinematic icons always carries with it a high risk of comparative failure. Newton and Wahlberg unwisely take that risk with predictable results.

Where Hepburn was alluringly capricious, Newton is bland. Where Grant played Peters with a comfortable and sexy assurance, Wahlberg plays him as a nervous goofball.

Lending support as a trio of suspicious characters are Joong-Hoon Park, Ted Levine, and Lisa Gay Hamilton. Little is known about these characters before, during, or after the film. Surprisingly, we find that we don't really care. Tim Robbins (Arlington Road) is a bit more successful taking on the Walter Mattheu role of a governmental bureaucrat who warns Regina that not everybody is who they claim to be.

The most pleasant surprise of the film is a brief cameo appearance of legendary singer/composer Charles Aznavour (Shoot the Piano Player). For an instant, life is breathed onto the screen. It's just long enough for us to realize how seriously dead the rest of the production is.

Often a director will have a concept around which he'll build his film. Such is the case with The Truth About Charlie. Director Jonathan Demme (The Silence of the Lambs) reportedly set out to remake Charade in the style of the French New Wave filmmakers who were at their zenith at the time Charade was originally filmed. The problem with concept films is that if the concept is a poor one, there's no saving the movie. Sorry, Charlie.

Central to the story of both films is the fact that there's a whole lot of deception going on. Regina is kept off balance because she isn't sure who she can trust. Our spiritual adversary tries to build confusion in our minds in much the same way and for much the same purpose.

For such are false apostles, deceitful workers, transforming themselves into the apostles of Christ. And no marvel; for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light. Therefore it is no great thing if his ministers also be transformed as the ministers of righteousness. 2 Corinthians 11:13-15a (KJV)

By being offered something that appears to be righteous, many are deceived and kept from true salvation. Our God, however, has committed His truth to a written form so we might have a standard by which we may prove all things. He has also provided for us His spirit which enables us to discern spiritual matters. Unlike Regina, we always know who we might trust for we put our trust in Him.

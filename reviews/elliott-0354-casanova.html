Casanova

Sermon / spiritual topic: Mistaken Identity

Author-verified transcription

One would think that a movie about a famed 18th century raconteur in the hands of an award winning director and starring an up and rising Oscar contender would be a hot ticket. Unfortunately, Casanova fails to generate any heat of any kind.

Giacomo Casanova (Heath Ledger), a Venetian man of many talents, gives himself wholly to the pursuit of the female flesh even though such game sets him at odds with the iron-fisted rule of the church. Sought for lasciviousness and fornication (offenses which were then punishable by death), Casanova is told by the local doge (Tim McInnerny) to marry a respectable girl or else suffer the consequences.

He proposes to an attractive virgin (Natalie Dormer) who has caught his eye before meeting the independently headstrong Francesca (Sienna Miller) who hates everything Casanova stands for. Since nothing is as desirable as the forbidden fruit, Casanova plots, schemes and masquerades his way into her heart... all the while evading capture by the church's leading Inquisitor, Pucci (Jeremy Irons) or discovery by his now unwanted fiancé.

The script, by Jeffrey Hatcher and Kimberly Simi, relies on an old playwright's contrivance - the case of "mistaken identity." Just about everyone in Casanova pretends to be someone they are not and much confusion/chaos occurs until all becomes known by the end of the story. It is a device used heavily in farces - often to great hilarity. Unfortunately, this is one farce that has the frenzy but none of the fun.

The Oscar buzz that Brokeback Mountain's Heath Ledger is hearing in his ears may turn into something more like a disappointed hum once this film is released. He delivers a bland, unenergetic performance that flattens out one of history's most charismatic personalities.

Sienna Miller is attractive in her co- starring role but completely uninspiring. Jeremy Irons opts to play a laughable caricature of a religious evil rather than a believable foe that can convey threat and provide dramatic tension.

Only Oliver Platt as Paprizzio, a wealthy seller of lard from Genoa who, much to her dismay, is betrothed to Francesca, provides a portrayal that reflects depth of character... though he is nearly undone by his make up artist.

For those who appreciate on location sets, the film was shot in Venice but the post production work done to replicate the past eradicates any and all Venetian charm from the screen. It might as well have been shot on a Hollywood back lot.

The gimmick of mistaken identity may be a playwright's/screenwriter's contrivance but it by no means is original. History is filled with examples - as is Scripture.

Abraham had his wife pretend to be his sister in Genesis 20. Joseph did not reveal himself to his own brothers in Genesis 42. King Saul disguised himself in 1 Samuel 28. King Ahab disguised himself in 1 Kings 22. Even the resurrected Jesus Christ did not identify himself to the men on the road to Emmaus in Luke 24.

Bottom line - disguises, misdirection, and masquerades have long been used by men for various reasons. But, as Adam discovered in Genesis 3, there's no hiding or fooling God. He sees all and certainly sees through any subterfuge we might use to pull the wool over the eyes of man.

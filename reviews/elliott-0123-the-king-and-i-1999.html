The King and I (1999)

Michael Elliott
Original publication date
March 19, 1999
Rating
2 stars

Original Author Copy

Sermon / spiritual topic: Fear; spiritual warfare; truth; trusting God; courage

Author-verified transcription

The best thing about the new animated adaptation of the Rogers and Hammerstein musical, The King and I, is the same as it was in the original... the delightfully uplifting music. From “I Whistle A Happy Tune” to “Getting To Know You” and “Shall We Dance”, the music is first rate. Regretfully, the rest of this animated tale does not rise to its level.

The filmmakers have taken some liberties with the text, adding a villain and imbecile of a sidekick for the benefit of the kiddies, as well as a smart aleck monkey, some action scenes (we first meet Anna on a ship beset by a sea creature conjured up by the evil villain), and a happy ending, but they have kept the basic framework of the plot.

Schoolteacher Anna Leonowens (speaking voice by Miranda Richardson, The Apostle) arrives in Siam from her English home to teach in the king’s palace. Cultures and personalities clash as East meets West. The king (drawn to resemble the young Yul Brenner, who originated the role on Broadway) is a proud and stubborn man who resists change even as he recognizes that it is inevitable.

Anna grows to become quite fond of the king, respecting his strength, though exasperated with his domineering manner.

As a secondary plot, the king’s oldest son, Prince Chululongkorn has fallen in love with the servant girl, Tuptim. This is a love that is forbidden and punishable by death, a law that Anna finds abhorrent and that the English protectorate finds barbaric.

The evil Kralahome (voice by Ian Richardson, Dark City) uses this prohibited relationship to his advantage, as he tries to usurp the throne.

The animation of The King and I is sub par, a step above the Saturday morning cartoon fare but well below the level of recent animated feature films. The character of Master Little, the stooge of a sidekick, is one that even small children might find too silly to be amusing.

The script does not successfully adapt the original story for the children’s audience the filmmakers sought. There are simply too many adult themes remaining in the film that our younger people will find boring. In the audience of the screening I attended were a handful of pre-schoolers who became increasingly restless, wandering the aisles searching for an entertainment they were not finding on the screen.

Still, there are spiritual lessons to be seen on that screen... The evil Kralahome uses magical powers to project illusions so people will act as he wants them to act. But his powers have limitations. Anna and the crew of her ship were able to defeat the illusionary sea monster as they fought against their fear (“Whistle A Happy Tune”). Later, as the king questions whether his decisions are the right ones, Kralahome says, “Good, he doubts himself... now my powers will work.”

The devil spirit kingdom operates in much the same way. They project illusions of truth in this senses world to deceive us and steer us away from the spiritual truth of God. But their illusionary powers are limited. Only our doubt, which leads to worry, culminating in fear opens the door for their powers to work against us. As we “stay our mind” upon God, driving out all fear and doubt, believing the truth of God’s Word, we are able to recognize the deceptions and reject the evil from whence they came.

For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind. 2 Timothy 1:7 (KJV)

Now there is a tune worth the whistle.

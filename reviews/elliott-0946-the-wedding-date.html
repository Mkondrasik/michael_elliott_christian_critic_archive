Author-verified transcription

Pretty Woman gets the old gender switcheroo when The Wedding Date, a new film based upon Elizabeth Young's novel Asking for Trouble, turns a "her" into a "ho."

When Kat Ellis (Debra Messing) is invited to her sister's wedding, she feels the need to have a date. After all, she'll be running into her ex-fiancé (Jeremy Sheffield) who dumped her two years ago and she can't have him thinking she's still pining (even though she is).

The problem is that she wants to bring the "perfect" date and she's not seeing anyone who would remotely fit the bill. But it's not a problem that $6,000 can't solve. That's the going rate for Nick Mercer (Dermot Mulroney), a male escort who promises Kat that her family will love him and her ex will rue the day he ever let her go.

What follows is insipidly predictable and less enjoyable than it should be. Kat is nervous and anxious around Nick. Nick is smooth, witty, and oh, so confident. Kat's family is dizzily dysfunctional and her friends are randier than Austin Powers on Viagra. All in all, everyone plays the stereotype they are assigned and no one is believable in doing so with the possible exception of Mulroney who represents a fantasy male.

First time screenwriter Dana Fox heads off in the wrong direction at the onset and never recovers. The attraction between Kat and Nick is evident from the beginning and Fox builds no obstacle of any import to keep them apart.

Director Claire Kilner (How to Deal) doesn't appear to have the artistic vision to do more than a straightforward approach to substandard material which inevitably results in a substandard film.

Mulroney simmers and shines but really isn't asked to do more than look good and refrain from fumbling his lines. Messing has the harder job in that her character arc swings from one extreme to the other. She gives a game effort but needed much more substantial support from the script.

No one in the supporting cast distinguishes himself/herself. The most noticeable of the lot is Sarah Parish who plays one of Kat's friends, TJ. She's the one who comments on Nick's tushy area saying they're like biscuits fresh from the oven. (Yeah, that's the level of writing that we're talking about.)

The movie hinges of the dramatic device of Kat having to concoct a lie to live during the 3 day wedding weekend with her family. She ends up believing the lie herself. Lies are like that. The ones most affected by them are the people who tell them.

He feedeth on ashes: a deceived heart hath turned him aside, that he cannot deliver his soul, nor say, Is there not a lie in my right hand? Isaiah 44:20 (KJV)

Maintaining a clear perspective that enables us to discern between truth and error requires the utilization of a rock solid standard upon which to rely. God has provided us with such a standard so that we need never be deceived (by others or even by ourselves). As we lean on and use God's standard of truth, our understanding becomes unclouded and lies are exposed for what they are - unprofitable wastes of time and energy to be avoided.

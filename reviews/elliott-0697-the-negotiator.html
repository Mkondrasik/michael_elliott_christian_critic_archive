Author-verified transcription

The Negotiator is not your typical cop shoot-em-up action adventure. Don't worry, you'll still get more than your share of explosions and gunfire, but what you'll also get is some intelligent dialogue, delivered by two of the smartest actors working in film today.

Samuel L. Jackson (187) is Danny Roman, a police negotiator who is called in during hostage situations to reason with the hostage taker. The job requires a cool head and a calm manner - qualities that Danny seems to have in abundance.

Danny soon finds himself on the opposite side of the negotiating table. After his partner tips him off to possible corruption on the police force, the partner is killed and Danny is made to look like the prime suspect. In a desperate attempt to prove his innocence, Danny appears at the offices of the police department's Internal Affairs. When it becomes apparent to him that they are somehow involved in the corruption and cover-up, Danny decides to hold them hostage until the guilty parties can be identified and caught.

Kevin Spacey (Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil) is Chris Sabin, a police negotiator from another precinct called in (at Danny's request) to try and talk Danny down. Both Mr. Spacey and Mr. Jackson are superb in their respective roles. Their scenes together - one negotiator pitted against another - are filled to the brim with tension, wit, and yes, intelligence.

It is amazing how little we have come to expect of this genre known as action/adventure. Most of the time we will be content as long as there's an appealing hero figure, some semblance of a plot, and a lot of explosions, narrow escapes, and thrilling car chases.

The Negotiator helps to show us how low our expectations have dropped. In this movie, we find ourselves waiting for the action scenes to end so we can get back to the dialogue and the interaction between the characters. Because we care about these characters, the action scenes actually have some meaning for us.

Director F. Gary Gray (Set It Off) keeps the movie taut almost until the very end. The last few scenes seem rushed and as a result the movie doesn't tie together as neatly as it should. Still, he utilizes his actors well, showcasing their strengths.

Mr. Jackson excels as the good guy who has to "turn bad" in order to prove his goodness. Mr. Spacey turns in still another polished performance as a negotiator who must learn to operate outside of the handbook because not only has his opponent read the same book, he's apparently written many of the chapters.

They have their humorous moments as well: Danny takes an inexperienced officer to task for bungling the initial negotiation with him by his repeated use of the word "no". The first time we see Chris is at his home where he is failing in his negotiation attempts between his wife and young daughter.

Also in the cast is David Morse (The Rock) as S.W.A.T. leader Adam Beck, J.T. Walsh (Breakdown) as Internal Affairs Chief Terence Niebaum, and Paul Giamatti (The Truman Show) as Rudy, an informer who happens to be in the wrong place at the wrong time.

Of course, Danny wouldn't be in the situation he is in if it weren't for the false witnesses who helped to frame him for something he did not do. These kind of false witnesses are nothing new for the Christian believer. The scriptures are full of records where believers were persecuted due to the lies spread by those who stood against them. The early prophets suffered stoning, imprisonment, crucifixion, whippings, expulsion, and more. And yet they continued. The punishment, even though it was wrong and severe, was not enough to stop the truth that burned in their souls.

Danny was completely convinced of his innocence and did all that he could to see that the truth was heard. We should all be as convinced of the promises of God.

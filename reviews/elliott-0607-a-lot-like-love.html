Author-verified transcription

A Lot Like Love is a lot like When Harry Met Sally... made for a Generation X audience. There are some differences: Director Nigel Cole is no Rob Reiner; screenwriter Colin Patrick Lynch is no Nora Ephron; and stars Ashton Kutcher and Amanda Peet can’t quite muster the neurotic electricity that added to the appeal of Billy Crystal and Meg Ryan. But it’s not all bad news.

A Lot Like Love tells a slow but rather sweet tale of a pair of young strangers who meet, have sex, and then spend the next seven years getting to know each other as they find their life paths occasionally crossing.

Oliver (Kutcher) is a shy, kind of goofy but amiable guy and Emily (Peet) is the tough-street-smart bad groupie who decides to introduce him to the mile high club during a flight to LA. After spending the day together, they part ways figuring their brief encounter would be just that... brief.

Three years later they reunite for a day having both been the recipient of life’s various bumps and bruises. Although an attraction between them remains, the timing of this and subsequent meetings does not invite a lasting commitment. But as they keep running into each other over the years they find more and more reasons to want to stay together even though circumstances within their control conspire to keep them apart.

Kutcher seems to be playing Oliver in a constant state of mild befuddlement, as if life is too complicated for him to figure out so he’s stopped thinking about it. He’s mapped out his plan: he’ll get his career on track, set up house and in five or six years he’ll begin to think about starting a family. And yet, for a character with such a singularly focused mind, he still comes off as being someone who is drifting through life.

Peet’s Emily has a more dramatically defined character arc and changes quite a bit during the seven year span of the film. She and Kutcher are cute together and share a number of scenes which had the audience swooning with romantic empathy.

What the film lacks is the crackling tension of overwhelming obstacles or barriers which the characters must struggle to overcome. Thus the film has an ambling feel to it. Instead of a mad rush to a passionate embrace we are given a leisurely stroll to a comfortable hug.

The filmmakers do avoid falling into many of the overblown romantic comedy clichés found in the genre but they do indulge themselves with a climatic plot twist that has been twisted so often that all the bend has been taken out of it.

I found Oliver’s approach to life to be an interesting aspect of the film. His desire to be settled financially and materialistically before asking a woman to share his life, while understandable, contains a major flaw in logic.

For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh. Ephesians 5:31 (KJV)

Our heads do not detach themselves to prepare a perfect life before asking our bodies to follow. One flesh means that all experiences, good and bad, are shared equally. By equating a marriage to a physical body, God defines a healthy marriage. It means two people who choose to live as one in every situation - not just when it is comfortable or convenient.

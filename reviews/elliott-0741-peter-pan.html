Author-verified transcription

It's hard to believe that J.M. Barrie's play about a boy who refuses to grow up is nearly 100 years old. Like the title character, Barrie's story has aged very well indeed as evidenced by its newest adaptation. This Peter Pan is not only the first live action feature film version made since the days of silent movies, it is the first major production to have cast a boy in the title role.

Wendy Darling (Rachel Hurd-Wood) and her brothers Michael and John, live in Victorian England with their loving parents. From all indications, they are having a perfectly lovely childhood. That is about to end as Father puts his fatherly foot down and insists that Wendy begin her "training" for womanhood.

Unbeknownst to the Darling family, a young boy dressed in leaves has been visiting Wendy's room at night in order to hear her tell stories of adventure to her brothers. Peter Pan (Jeremy Sumpter), as he is called, is the leader of the Lost Boys who live in a distant place called Neverland. He invites Wendy, Michael and John to visit and seeing as they have to fly (with the help of fairy dust) to get there, they excitedly agree.

Neverland is a magical place where children never grow old and life and death adventures seem to lurk around every corner. It is a place of fairies, pirates, and Indians, and it doesn't take long for the Lost Boys to take to Wendy and accept her as their honorary "mother."

But Captain Hook (Jason Isaacs) has other ideas. He's been after Peter Pan for as long as he can remember. It has certainly been since Peter cut off his hand and fed it to a crocodile. That croc has been following Hook ever since for a second course.

Hook sees in the Darling children an opportunity to lure Pan into a trap. And speaking of traps, Neverland has a way of making children forget their parents and their home. Already, for Wendy and her brothers, England is a foggy memory and their parents a forgotten dream.

While it may be a bit too intense for the very young, Peter Pan is a rousing good time for a family outing. This film is much more faithful to the original text than the Disney animated version. And with young Jeremy Sumpter as Pan, the psychological elements involved make more sense than did the 1960 TV version with 40 year old Mary Martin playing the lead.

Jeremy is all vim and vigor - full of the cockiness of youth without possessing the sure understanding of grownups.

What is especially delightful about this version is that, like Barrie's original work, it is as much Wendy's story as it is Pan's. And the filmmakers have made a treasure of a find in Rachel Hurd-Wood who simply illuminates the screen in her debut film performance. As Wendy, she properly straddles that nebulous line between child and adolescent.

And, of course, what would the hero be without an impressive villain? Jason Isaacs, as Hook, has a field day with his ingratiating style and presence. Humorous and threatening both, he is a perfect foil for Pan's youthful confidence.

The story itself is not just filled with childish dreams and desires. It is also infused with a healthy dose of an increasing awareness of change and the responsibilities that come with maturity.

Pan, for all his crowing and boasting, is still just a child... and a foolish one at that. His potential will never be realized until he enters the realm of adulthood. It is a realm into which all of us must enter. As we do, we can try to hang onto some of the childlike qualities that are so commendable but we must also embrace the growth and development that comes with maturing.

When I was a child, I spake as a child, I understood as a child, I thought as a child: but when I became a man, I put away childish things. 1 Corinthians 13:11 (KJV)

Peter Pan, leader of the Lost Boys, is stuck in a world that will not allow him to experience all that life has to offer. Wendy's example is a far better one to emulate.

Author-verified transcription

Stars of popular television sitcoms often have trouble breaking free of the roles that made them household names. It may be too early to tell if anyone from the six member cast of Friends will be able to parlay their small screen success into a large screen career. The Good Girl gives us an indication that at least one of them has the potential.

Jennifer Aniston (Rock Star) stars as Justine (Teeny) Last, a woman whose life has stalled in a small West Texas town. She's thirty years old, working as a clerk at the Retail Rodeo, as depressing a discount superstore that has ever been built. Her house painting husband (John C. Reilly, Magnolia) is an unexciting man who does little else besides watching TV and getting stoned with his buddy Bubba (Tim Blake Nelson, Minority Report). Her future is filled with the promise of more blandness and her past is filled with nothing but regrets.

It is no wonder that she perks up a bit with the appearance of Holden (Jake Gyllenhaal, October Sky), a 22-year-old coworker who is just as dissatisfied with life as she. Holden, who renamed himself after identifying with the lead character of Catcher in the Rye, is dark, moody and mysterious. He's anything but bland. Believing, as they do, that the world doesn't seem to understand them, they reach out to each other.

Not surprisingly, their not-so-secret sexual affair doesn't change anything. In fact, as Holden becomes more and more dependent upon her, Justine begins to realize that she's traded one dead-end road for another. But how to end it without hurting anyone?

The Good Girl was written and directed by the same team that was responsible for Chuck & Buck, Mike White and Miguel Arteta. Both films have a darkly satirical edge to them. Most of the humorous observations of this film can be found in the daily rituals at the Retail Rodeo.

There is a goofy security guard (Mike White) who tries to get Justine to come to his Bible study group. There's the young gothic chick (Zooey Deschanel, Almost Famous) who uses the store's PA system to insult the shoppers even though they are so desensitized they don't notice. She later displays more offbeat humor by giving garish makeup tips at the cosmetics counter. There's the manager (John Carroll Lynch, Bubble Boy) who gives tribute to deceased employees by broadcasting what he considers appropriately titled songs throughout the store.

What draws us to the film most of all are the performances by the lead actors. Aniston's wistful expression and resigned manner lends credence to the yearning Justine must have for a different kind of life. There isn't a single moment where she reverts to playing Rachel, her character on Friends. She is fully believable in this brand new persona.

Gyllenhaall plays Holden as a young man whose teen aged angst has followed him into his twenties. His intensity is captivating.

While both characters readily admit to feeling an emptiness in their lives, the actions they take to fill it only causes that void to deepen. When it is the world causing us misery, it simply makes no sense to turn to the world in search of a solution.

"Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths." Proverbs 3:5-6 (KJV)

It is disappointing that the one character in the film who should have been able to truly help Justine and her husband was portrayed as something of a goof ball. Even so, Justine still recognizes that the security guard's Bible studies might contain the answer she seeks. Her guilt regarding her infidelity is the only thing that stopped her from attending.

That is something we should bear in mind. When people come to us seeking guidance and deliverance, the last thing they need is to be made to feel guilty about what they've done. If they are seeking to change, they already know how to feel guilty. What they need to learn is how to receive God's peace and love.

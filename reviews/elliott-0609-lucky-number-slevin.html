Author-verified transcription

It would appear that, despite the title, the lead character of Lucky Number Slevin is anything but lucky. On one particularly bad day, he lost his job, was evicted from his apartment, and caught his girlfriend cheating on him. After he takes an old friend up on an offer to stay with him to sort things out, things turn decidedly worse.

Mistaken for his buddy Nick, Slevin (Josh Hartnett) becomes embroiled in a twenty year old feud between two mobsters. The Boss (Morgan Freeman), who believes Nick/Slevin owes him $96,000, wants him to settle the debt by killing the son of his rival, the Rabbi (Sir Ben Kingsley) who, it just so happens, also believes that Nick/Slevin owes him money.

Working with both sides is a professional assassin named Goodkat (Bruce Willis) who seems to be orchestrating matters for a purpose not immediately evident. But then again, that’s par for this course. There is much more to the film than appears at first glance.

Slevin is assisted by the well intentioned but amateurish sleuthing of Nick’s bubbly neighbor (Lucy Liu). He is being watched by a suspicious detective (Stanley Tucci). He is hampered by his own smart aleck mouth that can’t seem to stop making inappropriate comments at the worst possible times. Director Paul McGuigan (Wicker Park) adeptly tells an extremely violent crime story with a quirky “indie” voice. The depictions of various killings are blood-splatteringly graphic and numerous. Woven around the high body count are broad and often humorous characterizations that invite our continued inspection.

Josh Hartnett adds a lot of the humor through his character’s supposed medical condition that makes him immune to stress or worry. His quips lead to many of the biggest laughs and his demeanor is light and winsome which adds to our enjoyment.

Sir Ben Kingsley continues to impress with his chameleon-like aptitude to change. Here, he takes on the persona of a New York Jewish gangster with the ease of slipping on a trenchcoat. Morgan Freeman and Bruce Willis provide adequate support but seem to be coasting in roles that don’t stretch their talents.

The screenplay by Jason Smilovic has a few clever lines and enough twists to keep an audience surprised for three quarters of its running time. Unfortunately, the big reveal comes a bit too early, leaving a good half hour or more of wrap up without the built-in tension of trying to figure out what is really happening.

Slevin claims to have a medical condition that makes him impervious to worry or anxiety. Because he is physiologically incapable of grasping the seriousness of his situation, he is constantly saying the wrong thing to people who don’t much care for his flippancy.

It’s a new and interesting character trait that makes Slevin a character that is never boring to watch. Of course, worry and anxiety are realities that most of us must wrestle with from time to time. There is a way to rid ourselves of them. God tells us that as we cast our cares to Him, He will remove them from us.

“Have no anxiety about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which passes all understanding, will keep your hearts and minds in Christ Jesus.” Philippians 4:6-7 (RSV)

Big Fish

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 3, 2003
Rating
2½ stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Psalms 23:4-6Proverbs 14:32Luke 1:76-79

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Death; hope; fear; facing the future

Author-verified transcription

The odds are that when Ed Bloom begins a question with "Did I ever tell you about the time...?" the answer will be yes. Still, that won't stop him from telling the story again. In Big Fish, Ed Bloom (Albert Finney) is a teller of tales... tall tales. And he's been telling them for so long that it is difficult to distinguish between fiction and reality. Perhaps he's no longer sure himself.

It is a sore subject for his son, Will (Billy Crudup), who, because of all the fabrications he has been told from his youth, is beginning to think that he doesn't know who his father really is. When his father's health takes a turn for the worst, Will travels to his hospital bed where he hopes to finally learn the truth behind all the stories.

Director Tim Burton imaginatively tells the exaggerated story of Ed Bloom in flashbacks with Ewan McGregor as the younger version. Whether winning the heart of his true love by planting 10,000 daffodils outside her window, working odd jobs with a traveling circus, or volunteering for dangerous secret missions during the war, we get the sense that Bloom's stories are grounded in fact but embellished and exaggerated for no other reason than to entertain the listener.

Big Fish tries to be poignantly whimsical (or perhaps whimsically poignant) but has some trouble finding that delicate balance between the two. It doesn't really work as a fairy tale because the real world intrudes and comments upon the absurdity of what is being presented. It isn't that successful as an intimate family drama because of the larger than life fantasy elements that keep us (and Will) from getting to know the central character.

The final 10 or 15 minutes of the film, where Will proves that he is his father's son, do work well and helps to tie the various stories together to convey a meaning - but to this reviewer, the payoff wasn't enough to validate all that came before.

Ewan McGregor, playing what is essentially a figment of Ed Bloom's imagination, relies upon his considerable charm and gives us a character which is oddly similar to his role in this year's Down With Love.

The relationship between Albert Finney as Edward and Billy Crudup as Will could have been more deeply explored or expanded. As it is, the actors internalize their roles so much that it never reaches the surface where we might be clued into what is really happening between them. Jessica Lange, as Ed's loving and devoted wife, gives the most effective and heartfelt performance of the cast.

Early on, in one of Bloom's stories, Ed meets a witch whose glass eye will show anyone who dares to look into it the image of how he will die. Young Bloom decides to look because he figures that by knowing the cause of his death, he will be able to face all the other events of his life with no fear.

We may not know the specifics of how we will die, although we do know that it is an event that will surely come to us all should our Lord tarry. What we do know is what will happen afterwards. It is that knowledge that enables us to face the future with hope and thanksgiving rather than with fear and trepidation.

Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me. Thou preparest a table before me in the presence of mine enemies: thou anointest my head with oil; my cup runneth over. Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life: and I will dwell in the house of the LORD for ever. Psalms 23:4-6 (KJV)

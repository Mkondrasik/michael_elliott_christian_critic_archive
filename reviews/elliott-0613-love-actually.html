Author-verified transcription

It makes the world go round. It is a many splendored thing. It means never having to say you’re sorry. It is also the subject of the many unrelated but ultimately connected stories being told in the new British comedy, Love Actually.

Story #1: Hugh Grant is an unmarried prime minister being distracted from his ministerial duties by a growing attraction for a member of his staff.

Story #2: Colin Firth is a writer who heads for the country after discovering his wife has been unfaithful. He becomes smitten with his foreign housekeeper but they face a large language barrier.

Story #3: Alan Rickman is an office manager whose marriage to wife Emma Thompson is threatened by an increasingly overt office flirt (Heike Makatsch).

Story #4: Liam Neeson is a recently widowed man left to take care of a young stepson (Thomas Sangster) who he barely knows. When the lad confesses a puppy love fixation on the most popular girl in school, together they map out a strategy to win her affection.

Story #5: Laura Linney is an office employee with a deep crush on a coworker (Rodrigo Santoro) but also has an often conflicting obligation to take care of her troubled brother.

Story #6: Bill Nighy is an aging rock and roller trying to make a comeback with a cheesy Christmas song. By his side, in both good and bad times, has been his manager (Gregor Fisher).

Story #7: Best man Andrew Lincoln suppresses his desire for his best friend’s bride (Keira Knightley) so well that both bride and groom get the impression he doesn’t care for her at all.

Story #8: Kris Marshall has been so unlucky at love in England that he decides to move to America where he believes his accent will cause girls there to swoon.

Story #9: Martin Freeman and Joanna Page play the stars of an adult film who, during shooting, somehow manage to speak shyly and humorously in contrast with their physical actions.

Each of the stories are competently acted although no one overshadows the others. There’s just not enough time to explore any of them in depth. But depth doesn’t seem to be the motivating artistic goal here. Love Actually is a light, inconsequential (though adult oriented) treatise on the mystery of human relationships - particularly between male and female.

Writer/director Richard Curtis is only partially successful. There are humorous moments to be sure. There are also some genuinely sincere, heartfelt sentiments expressed. Certainly, some stories are more effective than others and some are funnier. But they all share one thing in common. Oddly enough - it isn’t love. At least not love as it is defined in the scriptures.

The Bible should be the definitive authority on the subject because, after all, God is love. From our studies of His Word, we learn that the primary characteristic of love is one of giving.

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. John 3:16 (KJV)

To love, biblically speaking, is to give, selflessly and unconditionally. The world has twisted the meaning of love in many different ways - none of which adds to God’s original intent. The “love” on display in Love Actually is but a mere shadow of the true love available between a man and a woman.

Perhaps the film should have been retitled Lust Actually. It might have been more accurate. The relationships that we see displayed for us are predominantly focused upon the physical manifestation of love. The physical attraction or sexual feelings between a man and woman is what takes center stage in this movie. As any successful married couple can confirm, that’s only one part of the puzzle. The full reality of love is far richer and much more rewarding than that.

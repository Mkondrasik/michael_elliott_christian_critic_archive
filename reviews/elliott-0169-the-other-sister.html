The Other Sister

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 26, 1999
Rating
3 stars
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Humility; childlike innocence; love; acceptance; independence

Author-verified transcription

In The Other Sister, Carla Tate does not like to be laughed at. Sadly, people have been laughing at her for most of her twenty-something years of life.

Carla is intellectually retarded (or mentally challenged, to use the current politically correct vernacular.) Having been sent away to a "special" school for the last ten years, she is now returning home to a family who is not quite sure what to expect.

That transition is not going to be an easy one... especially for Carla's mother, Elizabeth. Much of the movie deals with Carla's desire for more and more independence contrasted with her mother's fear that she will never be capable of being independent. There are a couple of unneeded sub-plots, such as the gratuitous lesbian relationship of one of the other Tate sisters, but the core and heart of this movie is found in the battle of wills and visions between Carla and her mother.

Carla sees and recognizes her limitations and is willing to experience the world within the boundaries of those limitations. She wants to go to a normal school. She wants to get her own apartment. Above all, she wants to be allowed to fall in love.

Elizabeth sees the same limitations, but wants to work on expanding Carla's capabilities before granting her independence. It is a not so simple matter of accepting who one is vs. holding onto a preconceived image of what one should be.

The Other Sister is blessed to have two outstanding performances at its center. Juliette Lewis (Cape Fear) wears her emotions so close to the surface that every thought, every feeling registers on her expressive face. She gives a completely honest and brave performance.

Giovanni Ribisi (Saving Private Ryan) as Daniel, a young man similarly challenged who falls in love with Carla, is just as convincing. Both portrayals manage to be believable without becoming condescending or patronizing. The credit for that belongs to these two fine performers.

Diane Keaton (The First Wives' Club) is cast in the thankless role of the smothering, over-protective Elizabeth. As a well-bred society woman, she struggles to accept the fact that none of her children are fitting into the mold that she has cast for them.

Tom Skerritt (A River Runs Through It) plays her husband with a quiet wisdom that belies an underlying strength and passion. All four performances are richly textured and infused with great heart.

Director Garry Marshall (Pretty Woman) walks a fine line in this unabashedly manipulative exercise. Much of the humor in the film comes at the expense of its two main leads. Whether it's Daniel's love for marching bands which causes him to play 76 Trombones during a romantically intimate moment, or Carla's physical inability to learn how to play tennis, or the pair of them studying a sex manual before they make the mutually agreeable decision to "do it," our laughter could be construed as insensitive. But the portrayals are so ingenuous and honest, and the characters so endearing that even as we are laughing at their inappropriate comments or actions, we are at the same time rooting for them to succeed.

The simple, complete and unreserved love these two characters express reminded me of the humility and childlike innocence we are to have in our relationship with God.

And said, Verily I say unto you, Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, the same is greatest in the kingdom of heaven. Matthew 18:3-4 [KJV]

We always know what Carla is feeling, for like a child, she does not bury her emotions under a facade of adult propriety. Her pain at being embarrassed during her sister's wedding reception is at once recognizably real and heartrending. There are times when we will laugh at things that Carla and Daniel say or do. I would like to think they know that even though we are laughing, it is not done out of maliciousness or cruelty. We're laughing because they have touched our hearts.

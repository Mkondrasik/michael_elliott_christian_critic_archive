Author-verified transcription

Remember that cute little alien named E.T.? Well he’s nowhere to be found in Steven Spielberg’s remake of the H.G. Wells’ classic novel War of the Worlds. The aliens in this film are big, menacing and hell bent on world domination. As one of the characters put it, “This ain’t a war. It’s an extermination.”

Among those scurrying on the ground to avoid death is blue collar worker Ray Ferrier (Tom Cruise) and his two children: sullen teen Robbie (Justin Chatwin) and ten year old Rachel (Dakota Fanning). Early on in the film Ray shows every sign of being a slacker dad. He’s emotionally disconnected from his kids and unpracticed in handling the responsibilities of fatherhood.

The alien’s death rays change all that as Ray transitions into high-speed survival mode. His sole motivation - indeed, his only thoughts become set on keeping his children alive. He has no time to think about revenge or even self-preservation.

Ray and the kids simply try to keep one step ahead of the devastation as they head from New Jersey to Boston where the kids’ mother is staying. Along the way, Ray manages to find and show the love for his children that up to this point he’d been unable to express.

In many ways, this is a small film. The focus is not on earth’s annihilation but rather on how one dysfunctional family reacts in the face of it. The tension and terror are real because director Steven Spielberg keeps us on the periphery of the action which is where Ray tries to stay. We see the attacks occur from Ray’s perspective and limited understanding. By establishing and maintaining this point-of-view, we gain a sense what the humans must be feeling - a sense of the overwhelming futility that comes with trying to resist a seemingly unconquerable foe.

Tom Cruise gets better as the film goes along. There’s not much to appreciate about him or his character early on. But as the film shifts into high CGI gear, he keeps the story solidly grounded in emotional truth. He’s ably assisted by young Dakota Fanning who not only seems to have a mature grasp of this art form, she also appears to know who she is and what she is capable of doing. She does an excellent job portraying a young girl living through an inconceivable terror.

This is a very dark side to Spielberg’s continuing fascination with extra-terrestrial life (E.T. and Close Encounters of the Third Kind). The images of violence and (somehow even more disturbingly) the aftermath of violence are graphic and most powerfully felt.

The comparisons to the terrible events of 9-11 are understandable and immediate. The dust coated survivors, the wall of photos as people search for information of their loved ones; the rubble and destruction of collapsed buildings; clothes floating from the skies as the final reminders of those who once wore them... I would think that, depending upon one’s personal experience during one of America’s darkest days, elements of this film might stir up highly emotional and unpleasant memories.

The one thing the film does quite well is subtly underscore something that is quite obvious to those who study the Bible. When you have an enemy who is not of this world, that’s where you have to look for your deliverance.

“For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places... Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day; and having done all, to stand.” Ephesians 6:12-13 (KJV)

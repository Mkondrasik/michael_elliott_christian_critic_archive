Titan AE

Author-verified transcription

The AE in the title of the animated TITAN AE must stand for "after Earth" -- appropriate because in the first 10 minutes of the film, our planet is destroyed beyond any possibility of restoration.

Set in the year 3028 AD, the film opens with the marauding aliens known as the Drej attacking the earth to the ends that mankind must sound the "abandon planet" alarm. The evil aliens then blow up the planet in an attempt to rid themselves of the dangerous and crafty humans who pose such a mysterious threat to them.

Cale's father was one such crafty individual. After securing safe passage for his five year old son, Sam Tucker (voice by Ron Perlman, Price of Glory) took the Titan spacecraft and secreted it from the evil Drej. This vessel contains a secret power that will stand as the remaining hope for the human species.

Sixteen years later, Cale (voice by Matt Damon, Dogma) is finding that there isn't much respect given to a life form without a home planet. His rather mundane life working aboard a salvage station is about to be interrupted as he learns that the future of the human race has been placed in his hands (more accurately, hand).

Aided by the stalwart Korso (voice by Bill Pullman, Brokedown Palace) and sexy Akima (voice by Drew Barrymore, Never Been Kissed), Cale must find the Titan before the Drej finds him. Other characters include the turtle-like Gune (voice by John Leguizamo, Summer of Sam), the kangaroo-like Stith (voice by Janeane Garofalo, Mystery Men), the scorpion-like cook (voice by Jim Breuer, Dick), and the lizard-like Preen (voice by Nathan Lane, Stuart Little). Who knew that alien life would be so animalistic?

The voices of TITAN AE did not blend with the animated drawings to which they were attached. I believe the problem was more due to the animators than the vocal talent. A combination of 3-D technology and 2-D animation was used to garner the various effects of the film - but the two styles seem more at odds with each other than they were complementary. For so much detail and precision to have gone into the drawing of the ships, guns and background settings, the characters themselves remain lifeless and rather one dimensional. Vocal inflection alone weren't able to bridge the gap and make them interesting to watch.

The film was at its best when the focus was on the action sequences of the ships and 3-D CGI crafted scenes were predominately featured. A "cat and mouse" chase scene through the ice rings of Tigrin had an interesting fun house mirror effect and was reminiscent of similar scenes found in The Hunt for Red October or Star Trek: The Wrath of Khan.

Midway through the film, Cale poses a question that made a lot of sense. The humans, who now had no home and were dispersed throughout the universe in weak colonies, were no threat to the powerful energy-based creatures known as the Drej. So why did the Drej so vehemently want to exterminate the human race? The answer given is very enlightening. "They don't fear us for what we are," Akima says, "They are afraid for what we might become."

The devil has the same fear. A clue to this is given in 1 Corinthians:

Which none of the princes of this world knew: for had they known it, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory. 1 Corinthians 2:8 (KJV)

The "princes of this world" is a figurative reference to the devil spirit kingdom. What they didn't know was God's redemptive plan for mankind. As the scripture states, had they known that the crucifixion of Jesus Christ would lead to man's ability to receive holy spirit, they would not have orchestrated his death. In other words, they would have preferred keeping Jesus alive rather than having an entire people able to exercise the power of God and do the works of Jesus Christ (indeed, greater works than these.)

The devil doesn't fear what we are. He fears what we may become... Power-filled born again Christians armed with the might and knowledge of God.

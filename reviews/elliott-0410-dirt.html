Author-verified transcription

Is there anything on this earth that is "dumber than dirt?" Perhaps not. That is probably why Dirt was selected as the title for a film where the lead characters leave no question as to their intelligence (or lack thereof).

The film opens with Junior (Michael Covert) and Scooter (Tracy Fraim) driving out into the West Texan desert to bury their recently deceased mama, who they thoughtfully and lovingly stuffed into a sleeping bag, duct-taping it sealed. These two mama's boys have never lived on their own before and after a few days without a woman's influence in their lives, they realize they had better find a replacement for her and pronto.

It is pure chance that leads them to the grocery store parking lot as Dede (Tara Chocol) is walking to her car. Head-butting her into submission, they take her home only to discover that she is quite happy to remain. Her husband (Patrick Warburton), a sadistically abusive Texas Ranger, has pushed her around for the last time. She is perfectly content to act as surrogate mama for the boys as long as they can support her, steal her baby from her husband and, most importantly, keep her husband away from them. The latter task proves more difficult than the others.

Complications ensue as the boys turn to bank robbery for their means of livelihood.

Their first attempt was so successful, they decide to stick with what works and just keep robbing the same bank over and over. Oddly enough, the employees rather like it... It seems banking can get a bit boring and the visits of Junior and Scooter kind of liven things up for a while.

We may feel the same. Dirt is certainly lively, borrowing freely from the irreverent style of humor made popular by the Coen Brothers (Raising Arizona). It may not be as polished as the work of the more accomplished filmmakers, but there are moments which are every bit as humorous and unexpected as we've seen from the Coen boys.

In addition to the aforementioned actors, Jennifer Tilly and Luke Perry both provide comic cameos.

Dirt is unquestionably violent, presumably for comic purposes (although for this reviewer, it crossed a line which made the scenes more uncomfortable than shockingly funny.) Still, violence plays an important part in the story as Junior has obvious problems managing his anger. If he's going to have any possibility of finding happiness, he's going to have to deal with that first.

"For wrath killeth the foolish man, and envy slayeth the silly one." Job 5:2 (KJV)

Anger, as in all emotions, has its place. The key is to loose it without losing it. No emotion, no matter how strongly felt, warrants the relinquishing of our mind's control over our body (and subsequently, our actions.) One doesn't have to be book smart to exercise a little common sense.

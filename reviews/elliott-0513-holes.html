Author-verified transcription

If you have a child in grade school, chances are you already know the story of Holes. Louis Sachar's award winning children's novel has been used by teachers to encourage reading since it first appeared in 1998. Now Walt Disney Pictures brings this imaginative tale of friendship and redemption to the big screen.

Stanley Yelnats IV (note the palindrome) is a kid who seems always to be in the wrong place at the wrong time. His family attributes it to an old curse which has afflicted the male Yelnats (all of whom are named Stanley) dating back to his "no good dirty rotten pig-stealing great-great-grandfather."

When Stanley IV (Shia LaBeouf, Even Stevens) is unjustly convicted of stealing a pair of shoes, he's given a choice of going to jail or attending Camp Green Lakes' character building program.

Run by a strict and somewhat scary disciplinarian simply known as The Warden (Sigourney Weaver, Tadpole), the program consists of sending the boys into the desert where they are to dig one hole each day - 5 feet deep by 5 feet round. Of course, they are to show the warden anything interesting that they might find.

In addition to following Stanley's progress in the camp, the film also takes us back in time to show how the Yelnats' family curse began and also to tell the story of Kissing Kate Burton (Patricia Arquette, Little Nicky), an Old West legendary outlaw. All these stories eventually come together as young Stanley learns how to stand up for himself while facing down the curse of the Yelnats.

Director Andrew Davis (The Fugitive) is best known for big budget action/adventure films and so the family friendly Holes is a bit of a departure for him. We can hope it is a detour which he will continue traveling. It also doesn't hurt that author Louis Sachar has written the screenplay adaptation of his popular book and so was able to keep much of the story's thematic integrity intact.

Disney Channel teen star Shia LaBeouf has a nice easygoing style about him with just the right amount of awkwardness befitting Stanley's age and maturity level. The other kids in the cast also perform their roles with assurance. But it is the adults who seem to be having the most fun.

Jon Voight (Pearl Harbor) nearly steals the movie with an outrageous performance as an ornery, beer-bellied Texan caretaker named Mr. Sir. Tim Blake Nelson (Minority Report) adds a touch of looniness as the camp "counselor" and Sigourney Weaver finds a way to get plenty of villainous sub-textual mileage just by saying "Excuse me."

Some of the intertwining stories are more effective than others. For instance, the interracial relationship which forms at the center of the wild west tale seems a bit hurried and undeveloped. It is a minor issue for most of the film and therefore the substantive messages we receive from the film come from the present day story involving the boys at the camp.

Not only does Stanley grow as an individual, gaining his own nickname ("Caveman") which elevates his status in the camp, but his presence also has a positive effect on the other boys. His giving and forgiving nature turns the "every boy for himself" camp into more of a cooperative. As Stanley demonstrates his willingness to extend himself for the others in the camp, they begin to respond. In the case of Zero (Khleo Thomas, Friday After Next), his best friend, they form a bond that makes them inseparable.

A man [that hath] friends must shew himself friendly: and there is a friend [that] sticketh closer than a brother. Proverbs 18:24 (KJV)

Holes is a rich, multi-layered story which adults and young adults will enjoy equally.

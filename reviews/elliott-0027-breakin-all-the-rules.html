Breakin’ All the Rules

Breakin’ All the Rules winds up breaking none of them. This formulaic and rather tepid romantic comedy follows the predictable path from beginning to end with mediocre results.

Quincy Watson (Jamie Foxx) works as an editor for Spoil Magazine which is going through a downsizing period. His latest assignment is to craft management procedures which detail the best way to fire employees and then employ them because his boss, Philip (Peter MacNichol) is too cowardly to do it himself. He quits rather than fire his co-workers.

When Quincy gets publicly dumped and humiliated by his girlfriend Helen (Bianca Lawson) he goes into an emotional tailspin. A bitter letter to his ex-girl grows to become a manifesto of sorts, is then published and turns him into a celebrity expert on the art of breaking up.

Naturally, his friends call him for advice whenever their relationships take a turn for the worse. First is Evan (Morris Chestnut) a “player” whose relationships never last longer than three months. He needs Quincy’s help because he’s trying to break up with his latest girl, Nicky (Gabrielle Union) before she can break up with him. Of course, before he can help Evan, he falls for her himself.

Then, his mousey boss needs help to get rid of a scheming gold-digger (Jennifer Esposito) but every move Quincy suggests gets his boss deeper and deeper into a relationship he doesn’t want.

The film then dissolves into a series of misunderstandings and mistaken identities which are neither believable nor funny. Some of the attempted humor includes running gags involving an alcoholic dog and an oversized geriatric.

Writer/Director Daniel Taplitz plays it safe by abandoning his initial premise early on in the film and relying on a traditional comedy of errors formula to drive the story. It may have been a far more interesting film had he stayed with the idea of a man who has figured out the best way to break up with a girl but doesn’t have a clue as to the best way to stay with one.

Most everyone in the cast is personable and appealing in a low key kind of way. No one really rises above the material although Peter MacNichol as a milquetoast publisher who fears confrontation does his best and manages to steal a scene or two. His eventual confrontation with Quincy is the movie’s one highlight.

The film bears little resemblance to reality or how people might react in an actual relationship. The games that these characters play in the name of love are sophomoric and shallow and would be soundly rejected by anyone with a shred of self-respect. One of the characters actually states his opinion that honesty is not an important element of love. He contends that it is an important element of a relationship but love only cares about one thing... itself.

That’s a direct contradiction to the one book on love that was written by the only true expert on the subject. God, who is love, defines love in this way:

“Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It is not rude, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no record of wrongs. Love does not delight in evil but rejoices with the truth. It always protects, always trusts, always hopes, always perseveres.” 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 (NIV)

There’s quite a difference between the “love” depicted in Breakin’ All the Rules and the love that God says is available. It doesn’t take an expert to know which is preferable.

The Good Shepherd

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 22, 2006
Rating
2 stars

Translation: KJV

Author-verified transcription

Espionage is a dirty business and those who are engaged in it are undeniably stained by their association. Robert DeNiro's The Good Shepherd takes a look at the formation of the CIA, its practices, uses and abuses of power, in what proves to be an inexorably slow, overlong

Matt Damon stars as Edward Wilson, a fictionalized character loosely based on the director of the counter-intelligence arm of the CIA James Angleton. Even at Yale, before being tapped by a secret society, Wilson was a cold and unemotional man. His subsequent years of dealing with global espionage pushes him further away from normal human development.

Though he tackles the tough moral questions with a solid integrity, it is an integrity born from a calculated, rationalized and ultimately flawed belief that puts country first.

Wife (Angelina Jolie) and son (Eddie Redmayne) both take a back seat to Wilson's patriotic duty. It is a duty that condones the use of misinformation, violence, and murder. Such acts are carried out with a dispassionate disregard for both human life and human rights.

DeNiro has attracted a stellar cast to his epic project though none of the big names, himself included, manages to break through the leaden atmosphere which weighs down the

DeNiro casts himself as Bill Sullivan, mastermind of the CIA; William Hurt plays Philip Allen, a US senator; Alec Baldwin plays an FBI agent; John Turturro plays Wilson's aide... the list continues but none of these A-list actors make much of an impression.

Tammy Blanchard, as the purest character on screen, is also one of the most effective. She plays an attractive deaf co-ed that Wilson meets and with whom he falls in love. Because he allows himself to be taken from her, she represents the life he could have had.

For Damon, he plays Wilson as a monotone. It is a multi-layered performance but each layer is coated with a sameness that gives a one-note impression. DeNiro's character speaks of his desire to make the CIA the "eyes and ears" of the nation and not the "heart and soul." He's succeeded with this movie. Damon, who should have been the heart and soul, conveys neither. Thus, there is no empathetic hook designed to grab and hold the audience's emotions.

The Good Shepherd does not spoon feed its audience. As a result, it is easy to get lost in the manipulations, double-crosses, and unspoken threats and innuendoes that make up this murky undercover world. Clarity of purpose is not a priority here as much as the overall atmosphere

The underlying moral question is this: in the fight against evil, is it permissible (or even advisable) to adopt the practices of the enemy in order to defeat it? Wilson and his like use the cover of national security to perform acts that (they hope) will never see the light of day.

Perhaps they should pick up a Bible and read.

For there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; neither hid, that shall not be known. Therefore whatsoever ye have spoken in darkness shall be heard in the light; and that which ye have spoken in the ear in closets shall be proclaimed upon the housetops. Luke 12:2-3 (KJV)

Perhaps we as a people and as a nation would be better off if we leave the cloak and dagger stuff to the fiction writers. Real power will always be found in the light, not in the shadows.

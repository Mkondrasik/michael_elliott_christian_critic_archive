Author-verified transcription

Based upon the autobiography by Jordan Belfort, The Wolf of Wall Street shows how important it is to have a moral compass and how easily greed and excess can lead one away from it. Unfortunately for Belfort, he was drawn to an industry at a time when greed and excess was the name of the game. And unfortunately for the world today, it doesn’t look like there has been much of a downside to playing it which is why things probably haven’t changed all that much. The rich get richer and the poor who want to get rich get duped.

Belfort, (Leonardo DiCaprio), was a penny stock trader who built a multi-million dollar empire through manipulative sales tactics and a charismatic personality. After being schooled by his mentor, Mark Hanna (Matthew McConaughey), who preached that the true nature of the deal was broker take all, Belfort launched out on his own, teaching his duplicitous techniques to those shady enough to follow him down a path of deception and trickery.

Soon, Belfort and his cronies were rolling in money (as well as in drugs, sex, depravity, and any other vice money can buy – much of which is enthusiastically depicted on the screen.) When a prominent magazine runs an article exposing Belfort as a fraud and charlatan, it only serves to lead a path to his front door where every wannabe millionaire is waiting, resume in hand, looking for a job.

As told by Martin Scorcese, Belfort’s story almost runs full circle. It starts with Belfort’s humble beginnings, shows him learning the basics of brokerage firms, and quickly brings him to the top of the Wall Street elite. From there it is merely a series of self-destructive actions, one after another, that threaten to cause Belfort’s fall from financial power.

What it doesn’t show is the other side of the coin. For every dollar that Belfort pocketed (and there were millions of them), there was an individual who could ill afford to lose it. We don’t see those people. We don’t even think about them save for one brief scene with Belfort’s first wife (later discarded for a trophy version).

Thus it is difficult to applaud the artistic elements of the film. While the film admittedly showcases incredible talents, it still leaves one with an empty, unfulfilled feeling. Lives were ruined, lives were lost, but no lessons were learned. Scorcese and his team were content to tell and show the story without including the moral compass necessary to put Belfort’s life and actions into context.

Certainly, the life that Jordan Belfort lived is nothing new. It has existed long before Wall Street was ever paved.

“For you have spent enough time in the past doing what pagans choose to do--living in debauchery, lust, drunkenness, orgies, carousing and detestable idolatry.” 1 Peter 4:3 (NIV)

The appeal of excessive living is a trick of our spiritual adversary to keep us from a simple truth. Peace, rest, or happiness is never found in excess. It is found in moderation. It is not found in hoarding or accumulating wealth. It is found in sharing and giving to others. There is an old axiom that gets bandied about in acting classes that affirms “Less is more.” More has a tendency to clutter things. Less brings focus and clarity.

Life is much the same. More has an unending appetite because no matter what we have received or accomplished in our lives, it is never enough… there is always going to be “more.” If we drive our mindset to achieving more, we launch ourselves on a path that has no end. At least no good end.

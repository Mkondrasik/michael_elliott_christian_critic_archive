Author-verified transcription

No, you're not seeing double... Well, you are, but it's perfectly fine - your vision is not impaired. You're watching Disney's remake of the 1960's The Parent Trap which originally starred Hayley Mills. In this updated version, Lindsay Lohan takes the dual role of Hallie Parker and Annie James, identical twins that were separated at birth by their divorcing parents in a bizarre agreement.

Eleven years later, attending the same summer camp, they meet, realize that they are twin sisters and devise a plan: They'll trade places so they can finally get to know the other parent.

The father, Nicholas Parker (Dennis Quaid, Switchback), runs a successful vineyard and winery in Napa Valley, California. The mother, Elizabeth James (Natasha Richardson, Nell), is a renowned fashion designer living in England. Once the girls meet the parent they have never known, nothing will stop them from getting those crazy adults back together.

One little hang-up: The father is about to get remarried to "Cruella Deville." That's Annie's description of Meredith Blake (Elaine Hendrix, Romy and Michelle's High School), naming her after the villain in Disney's 101 Dalmatians. Somehow the girls have to get her out of the picture while getting mother and father in the same picture.

Miss Lohan is a real find. Her expressions are precious. Acting opposite yourself (in a double role) must be a real challenge, but Miss Lohan does so well that most times, you'd think you were watching two different actresses performing a scene together.

Of course, a lot of that is a result of the technological improvements that have been made since the days of the first Parent Trap or The Patty Duke Show. Still, Miss Lohan's winning personality(ies) draw us into the story as we grab the premise hook, line, and sinker.

Mr. Quaid does the ruffled but charming American bit quite well, while Miss Richardson is the picture of English style, grace and sophistication. In fact everyone in this film is likable with the single exception of Meredith. Miss Hendrix, in a thankless role, makes this character all to easy to dislike.

Director Nancy Meyers (Father of the Bride) does a marvelous job of staying true to the original story while at the same time keeping the references fresh and current. She should be commended for bringing to the theaters a true PG movie that is entertaining for all generations. There are a few scenes of playful mischief that you wouldn't want your children to emulate, but the pureness of heart these girls demonstrate send a far stronger message than any trouble they cause.

Watching the burning desire that the twins share to fill a void left by an absent parent reminds me that God is the one who designed the family structure of a father, mother, and child. The number three, biblically speaking, indicates spiritual completeness. When one part of the whole is missing, there is an emptiness that is most definitely felt. Perhaps that is the reason we are so moved when we see Annie/Hallie get to hug their respectively estranged parents for the first time in their lives. It just makes us feel good. And isn't that about the highest compliment you can pay a movie?

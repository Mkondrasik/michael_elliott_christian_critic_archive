Author-verified transcription

True Crime opens in a bar with the married, sixty-something veteran reporter, Steve Everett, attempting to seduce a 23 year-old cub reporter by sloppily plying her with alcohol and cheesy pickup lines. Within five minutes she's dead, having lost control of her car on her way home. Amazingly, that's not the "true crime" of the title.

Clint Eastwood directs, stars, and produced this new mystery/thriller which disappoints by having few thrills and fewer mysteries. Its hackneyed plot is one that crops up in just about every freshman creative writing class, being soundly rejected by any professor worth his salt as being a cheap cliche.

A man on death row awaits execution. The clock is ticking... With only hours left, one man becomes convinced of his innocence. Can he find the necessary evidence in time? Can he get that evidence to the governor in time? Will the governor's call reach the warden in time??? Oh, please...

With so much emphasis on the urgency of time, it is amazing that this film moves as slowly as it does.

Clint Eastwood (The Bridges of Madison County) plays the veteran newspaperman who expects to accomplish in eight hours what the police and defense attorneys have failed to do during the past six years. His is a miserable character who is aware of his failings and makes no excuse for them. Unable to keep his hands off the ladies (most of whom are young enough to be his granddaughters), he is a lying husband, a neglectful father, and an alcoholic to boot... These are obviously attributes of a selfish man.

In contrast, the man on death row, Frank Beachum (Isaiah Washington, Out of Sight), is a born again Christian, a loving father, a devoted husband, and who remains adamant in professing his innocence. Mr. Washington does a wonderful job in balancing the character's emotions.

James Woods (Vampires) tries too hard to make something happen in his role of Alan Mann, managing editor of the paper where Everett works. He and Mr. Eastwood try and work the same kind of chemistry that Jack Lemmon and Walter Matthau or Cary Grant and Rosalind Russell shared in their respective versions of The Front Page and His Girl Friday. They just don't have the crispness or crackling wit of a well-written script to do it. In their movie, the scenes between reporter and editor are simply forced and artificial.

The weakly written script of True Crime is credited to Larry Gross (Another 48 Hours), Paul Brickman (Risky Business), and Stephen Schiff (The Deep End of the Ocean). Its blandness is only interrupted by some of the most laughably bad one-liners heard outside of a Swartzenegger film. (Examples: "Santa Claus rides alone," and "You trust in your faith... I trust in my nose.")

That last statement is particularly ludicrous and dangerously misleading. The character of Steve says it, referring of course to his "nose for news" or his ability to "feel" the truth. There is only one standard that discerns between truth and error, and it has nothing to do with one's proboscis.

But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil. Hebrews 5:14 [KJV]

Strong meat refers to the depth of the truth that can be understood by mature believers who have faithfully applied the "milk" or foundational principles of God's Word in their lives. Our spiritual growth is much like our physical growth. We would no more serve a new born baby a T-bone steak than we would serve bottles of milk to our dinner guests.

What we are able to digest physically is dependent upon how developed our bodies are. Likewise, how much truth we can discern is dependent upon how developed we have become spiritually. The only way to develop ourselves spiritually is to continue to read and act upon the scriptures.

Instead of depending upon our noses... we can put our trust in the One Who designed them.

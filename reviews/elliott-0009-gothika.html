Gothika

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
November 21, 2003
Rating
2 stars

Translation: RJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Demonic possession; spiritual warfare; free will; death and afterlife; evil

Author-verified transcription

Haley Joel Osment isn't the only one seeing dead people these days. Halle Berry decides to straddle the fence between the normal and the paranormal worlds in the creepy thriller, Gothika.

In it, she plays Dr. Miranda Grey, a criminal psychologist for a woman's penitentiary that looks like it was designed by Freddy Kruger. We first see her hard at work, in a session with Chloe, a seriously disturbed patient/inmate (Penelope Cruz). Chloe's claims of satanic visits and sexual encounters with demons fail to faze Grey who is looking for a rational explanation to what she assumes to be the psychotic ramblings of a confused mind.

The good doctor gets a taste of her own medicine when, after leaving the penitentiary one dark and stormy night, she encounters something no unexplainable. The very next thing she knows, she's back at the penitentiary - this time as a patient and accused murderess. One who has no recollection of the crime she supposedly committed.

Her ex-coworkers, including are now treating her with the same clinical dismissal that she used to employ with those whom she viewed as "crazy."

To make matters worse, she's beginning to receive a visitor - a dead girl who persistently appears to Grey for reasons she can't begin to imagine. Or is it her mind that is conjuring up these images?

Director Mathieu Kassovitz does a remarkable job in evoking a darkly gothic mood by giving the penitentiary a faulty electrical system. What with the lights flickering constantly, lightning flashing through the windows, the sound of thunder and electrical crackling punctuating every scene, it is no wonder that we're made uncomfortably edgy. What Kassovitz can't do is disguise the inherent weakness of the script.

Once it puts its protagonist in her predicament, it has no where to go. It goes anyway. And where is ends up is so far out in left field that it's almost like watching a different movie... a bad one at that.

Halle Berry does some pretty intensive emotional calisthenics but she doesn't have much of a character to work with. The rest of the cast, including Robert Downey Jr., Charles Dutton, Bernard Hill and Ms. Cruz, do the best they can with underwritten, one-dimensional roles.

The question at the heart of the movie deals with whether or not Grey is crazy, possessed, or somehow being framed.

We, who study the scriptures, can't completely dismiss the spiritual reality of demonic possession. There are too many biblical records that testify to the frightening reality of it. But we can dismiss the way it is all too often portrayed by Hollywood screenwriters.

Spiritual possession is a matter of control. God doesn't possess, nor do his angels, because it would violate free will - something He will not do. Dead people or ghosts cannot possess as there is no thought, awareness or power in the grave. The only beings who possess are devil spirits and they can only do so if the subject has willingly opened his or her mind to the idea of devilish influence to the point that all spiritual resistance is gone.

There is never an upside to spiritual possession. There is no benefit, no positive outcome, no greater good being performed. It is always a devilish act done for destructive purposes.

The thief (devil) cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: John 10:10a (RJV)

So, while the idea of people reaching out from beyond the grave to correct some kind of injustice in this world might make for some spooky stories and fright filled "entertainment," it is pure fantasy dreamed up by screenwriters who do not always concern themselves with such "trifles" as logic and credibility.

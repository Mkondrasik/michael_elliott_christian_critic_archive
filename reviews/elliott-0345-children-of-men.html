Children of Men

Author-verified transcription

Mexican director Alfonso Cuaron (Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban) depicts one of the most realistic and bleakest future worlds that cinema has ever seen in his new sci-fi thriller Children of Men.

It is the year 2027 and it has been eighteen years since there has been a human birth. For some unexplained reason, women have become infertile and mankind is facing its own eventual extinction. The world, without the hope of future generations to give it purpose, has gone to hell.

Most other countries have been destroyed but Great Britain "soldiers on" despite the rampant violence that is tearing it apart. In the midst of this is ex-activist Theo Faron (Clive Owen), now a disillusioned government worker who is just going through the motions of a pointless life. Whatever comfort he finds comes at the bottom of a bottle.

When his ex-wife, Julian (Julianne Moore), asks for his help, he is drawn into a scenario that has global implications. A young illegal alien (Claire-Hope Ashitey) is miraculously with child but because of the current socio-political upheaval has no safe haven. The possibility of a rumored "Human Project" is her last recourse and Julian asks Theo to help obtain the travel visas to escort the young mother-to-be to safety.

Children of Men is set in a depressing world where most men have turned their back on even the pretense of civility. It is a dog-eat-dog (or man-shoot-man) existence as the last vestiges of humanity live out what remains of their now meaningless time on this earth.

Cuaron's ability to depict such a world is remarkable. He does not spend time pointing fingers at what has caused the world to collapse into its current state. He simply asks us to accept it as the backdrop for what is, essentially, a long chase film. Cuaron's mastery of the camera leads to a number of remarkable technical achievements. He's known for including long tracking shots in his films and Children of Men has some brilliantly executed ones.

Clive Owen treads a fine and difficult line as the main protagonist who, at the beginning of the film is an empty suit - a man who has already given up. It is only until later we begin to see through the emotionless façade he presents to an uncaring world.

Michael Caine and Julianne Moore add energy and lightness in supporting roles and we would have liked to have seen more of them but Cuaron was right to keep the focus where he did. Claire-Hope Ashitey strikes the perfect balance between fear and determination as the young woman whose pregnancy may change the world as she knows it... if she can escape the various factions who want to use her baby to advance their own political goals. The theme of the film which resounded loudly to me was one of hope... or the absence of it. Life without hope is a life that is hardly worth living. And yet there was a time when mankind did live without having hope.

That at that time ye were without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world: Ephesians 2: 12 (KJV)

God responded to our hopelessness with a different pregnancy - one that delivered hope to all men and for all time.

Six Days, Seven Nights

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
December 6, 1998
Rating
4 stars
Running time
1 hr: 42 min
Source
Original Author Copy

Scripture references

1 Corinthians 10:13James 1:12Matthew 26:41

Sermon / spiritual topic: Temptation; Free Will; Fidelity

Author-verified transcription

With all the hoopla and hype surrounding actress Anne Heche's (Volcano) self-professed homosexuality coupled with her public (and undoubtably private) relationship with TV star Ellen Degeneres, it might be tempting to allow our reaction to her personal life to overshadow the fact that her new film, Six Days, Seven Nights, is actually a little gem of a romantic comedy.

Seeing as Miss Heche does not portray a homosexual in the film, we won't address that issue here. Instead we'll keep our attention focused on the movie itself and whatever nuggets of truth may be gleaned from watching this amusing offering from producer/director Ivan Reitman (Ghostbusters, Twins).

Miss Heche plays Robin Monroe, a driven, ambitious executive for a fashion magazine who is enjoying a vacation in the South Pacific with her new fiancee, Frank (David Schwimmer of TV's Friends). An emergency photo shoot arises (See there, I bet you didn't even realize there was such an animal as an emergency photo shoot) and she is quickly called into duty.

In order to fly to Tahiti where the photo shoot is to be done, she enlists the aid of Quinn Harris, a laid-back, booze hound of a charter pilot amusingly played by Harrison Ford (The Fugitive). On the way to Tahiti, a storm forces the plane to crash land on a seemingly deserted island. The rest of the film follows this odd couple as they attempt to get off the island, handle snakes, evade pirates, drive each other crazy and somehow still manage to fall in love during the process.

It is the comic touches throughout the film that make it such a delight. Miss Heche and Mr. Ford demonstrate a comic awareness and timing that serve the film so well that they enable it to be elevated above its formulaic story-line. Their on-screen chemistry is clearly evident and, as a result, their scenes together crackle with an energy reminiscent of Hepburn and Tracy. The short-lived flight to Tahiti is a wonderful example of two craftsmen blending suspense, comedy and romance into a compact, wonderfully funny scene.

Meanwhile Mr. Schwimmer, playing a derivative of his nebbish TV persona, waits back at the tropical resort along with Quinn's nubile assistant, Angelica (Jacqueline Obradors of The People), where they comfort each other in the face of the apparent loss of their respective companions. How far the "comforting" is allowed to go is just one biblical lesson we can draw from this movie.

Free will is one of God's greatest gifts to man. We have the ability to choose for ourselves the course our lives will take. God has given us the ability to rise above the temptations and lures we often face in life. Mr. Schwimmer's character is presented with one such temptation - a temptation that he chooses not to resist. Even though his partner in "sin" tries to assuage his guilt by saying, "You're a guy... you can't help it," we know that the truth of the matter is that he CAN help it... we all can, men and women alike... if we so choose.

Mr. Ford's and Miss Heche's characters are presented with a similar temptation. They, however, choose not to totally succumb to the physical temptations of the flesh and in so doing, are able to stand upon the moral high ground at the end of the film. Ask anyone who has freely given up that high ground (our President immediately comes to mind) -- it just isn't worth it.

From a pure movie making perspective, giving the characters the recognition of having a physical attraction to each other, without the freedom to investigate that attraction helps to build an underlying tension in every scene. A tension which, in this movie's case, makes the situations facing the characters that much more comedic. Mr. Ford and Miss Heche play it to the hilt.

Much has been broadcast in the media regarding the real life age difference that exists between Mr. Ford and Miss Heche. Quite frankly, because of the playful give and take of these two fine actors, the physical attraction we're asked to accept of them is fully believable.

Still, the screenwriters were wise not to ignore the disparity of age. Instead, they actually incorporated it into the dialogue. By doing so the filmmakers were inasmuch saying, "Hey, we know he's twenty-five years older than she is...but come on, it's Harrison Ford... what do you think... she's going to say no?" And who are we to argue? Let's face it, A fifty year old Harrison Ford beats a thirty-something David Schwimmer every time... island or no island.

Author-verified transcription

1999 has seen a number of cinematic turnabouts. First there was master of horror films Wes Craven, seen directing a sentimental and inspirational movie about a violin teacher in Harlem. Now, David Lynch of the weird and popular Twin Peaks series teams with Disney Studios to bring us a G-rated offering called The Straight Story.

Before you buckle the children into a car and rush to the theater. "G" in this instance might stand for "Grass Growing," (which is probably how visually interesting this film will be to young ones.) The movie has the pacing of a lawnmower traveling down an American highway which, appropriately, is the setting of the story.

Adults may be touched by this story of dogged determination but the children will sleep right through it, tired from the exertion of waiting for something to happen.

Richard Farnsworth (The Gray Fox) is Alvin Straight, a 73 year old man with failing vision, hips as fragile as bone china, and a stubborn streak in him that mules would envy. When he gets word that his estranged older brother has suffered a stroke, he decides to make the 350 mile journey from Iowa to Wisconsin in order to visit him. Since the only mode of transport available to him is a small riding lawnmower, he hitches it up to a trailer, packs a cooler with hot dogs, and begins to "John Deere" his way down the road. The journey will take him six weeks which, as an odd coincidence, could very well be the approximate running time of the film itself.

However you respond to the pacing of the film, there is no mistaking the brilliance demonstrated in the casting of the lead character. Mr. Farnsworth plays this simple man with a subdued earnestness that is remarkably appealing. Whether imparting wisdom, ("Learn to separate the wheat from the chaff and let the small stuff fall away,") commenting upon his journey, ("This trip is a hard swallow to my pride,") or trading unpleasant war memories with a fellow WWII vet over a glass of milk, he captures the essence of a character that we would like to know.

And get to know him we do. There isn't much of a compelling narrative to get in the way of this character study. Mr. Lynch, as the title of the movie would suggest, tells the story straight. Based on a true incident (The Straight Story was dedicated to Alvin Straight who died in 1996), the film focuses on Alvin and the people he meets rather than on any dramatic experiences which occurred during the journey.

Even the details of the fraternal estrangement are not considered important enough to mention. The sweetness of the tale is found solely in the character of Alvin and the folks he meets during his six week journey. He is able to help a teen-aged runaway realize the value of family, leave a poignant impression on a group of young and carefree bicyclists, and touch the hearts of a small community who come to admire the stranger's tenacity and willfulness.

The musical score by Angelo Badalamenti (Arlington Road), fits with the casual, almost lazy way the film unfolds. Just as with a walk in the park, The Straight Story is in no particular hurry to get to the end and the soundtrack certainly lends itself to that mood.

There are a mixture of spiritual concepts that could be discussed. The journey to Wisconsin is not without challenges, yet Alvin is determined to follow through and achieve the goal he set for himself. Perseverance and endurance are two qualities that God would have us develop in our own lives.

Also, when conversing with the teen-aged runaway, Alvin demonstrates the strength that comes from being a part of a family by showing her how easily one twig will break, but when bound together with other twigs, that one twig is reinforced to the point that it cannot be broken. Equating that to the strength of the family unit is a biblical principal straight out of Ecclesiastes:

And if one prevail against him, two shall withstand him, and a threefold cord is not quickly broken. Ecclesiastes 4:12 [KJV]

Perhaps understanding this scene and scripture brings us a little closer to understanding why Alvin felt compelled to undertake what appeared to be a foolhardy trip in order to become reunited with his brother.

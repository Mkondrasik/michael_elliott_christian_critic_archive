Blast From The Past

Byline
Michael Elliott
Original publication date
February 12, 1999
Rating
1 star
Running time
1 hr: 52 min
Source
Published Web Version

Scripture references

Ephesians 4:141 Peter 3:81 Corinthians 15:33

Translation: KJV
Sermon / spiritual topic: Standards; morality; manners; cultural change; God's Word

Author-verified transcription

You've heard of a "nuclear family?" Meet the Webbers, America's first "nuclear accident family."

In Blast From The Past, Calvin Webber (Christopher Walken, Suicide Kings) is a genius inventor, with a streak of paranoia running through him. In 1962, during the Cuban missile crisis, Calvin overreacts to the president's address to the nation and takes his pregnant wife (Sissy Spacek, Affliction) to the elaborate bomb shelter he built in his back yard. A freak plane crash convinces him that the Communists have dropped the bomb and so he seals the door... to be time released thirty-five years later, after the imagined radiation danger has passed.

And so they live "sheltered" lives, raising their son, Adam (Brendan Fraser, Still Breathing) according to their 1960's sensibilities. Thirty-five years later, the doors open and Adam ventures outside for the first time in search of supplies and a wife. Oh, what a difference thirty-five years can make!

Mr. Fraser plays the anachronistic Adam with his characteristic earnestness and quirky charm. He plays him with an extreme broadness to be sure, but it was well in keeping with the overall tenor of the film.

Alicia Silverstone (Clueless) is disappointing as Eve. (Adam and Eve, get it?) Demonstrating little depth or emotional range, she turns in a rather bland performance.

Similarly, Sissy Spacek, as Calvin's dutiful wife, tries to pull off what appeared to me to be a drunken June Cleaver impersonation. Whatever else it was, it was also a one joke characterization that soon became predictably average.

Dave Foley (Kids In The Hall) as Eve's sympathetic and compassionate homosexual friend, Troy, and Mr. Walken as Adam's father fare better, providing many moments of light-hearted humor.

Director Hugh Wilson (First Wives' Club) using an uneven script, directs an uneven cast resulting in (what else?) an uneven movie.

Even in what will surely be the movie's signature scene, where Adam shows off the results derived from 35 years of dance lessons by swinging with two girls simultaneously, Mr. Wilson allows whatever momentum or anticipation that was built during that entertaining sequence to quickly dissipate.

Many of the forced religious references the filmmakers use are simply embarrassing. Upon the initial visit to the outside world, the Webbers encounter a drugged out bum who mistakes them for (get this!) the Father, the Mother, and the Son. He then proceeds to build an altar and worship them. This contrived running gag was not funny the first time let alone the fifth.

What I found to be most surprising was the reaction that the 1990's characters had to the 1960's mentality of Adam. They were flabbergasted at many of the things he would say or do. Things like saying grace before a meal, holding a door open for a woman, asking permission instead of assuming it, and being offended at even mild profanity were met with incredulous looks of "modern day" superiority. I would like to think that common courtesies don't just belong to an earlier age. Have we really "progressed" past the need to have manners?

Life moves so gradually that oftentimes we don't recognize the change that we, as a people, have made until it is already over. Like a rudderless boat without an anchor, we are subject to the changing tides and winds of the sea. All the more reason to have a true standard to hold us in place. The winds of time will still blow, but with an anchor to keep us grounded, and a rudder to direct our course, we can keep an even keel to our life.

That we henceforth be no more children, tossed to and fro, and carried about with every wind of doctrine, by the sleight of men, and cunning craftiness, whereby they lie in wait to deceive; Ephesians 4:14 [KJV]

God's Word is the only standard strong enough to resist the winds of change being blown by our spiritual opponent. By using the Word as our anchor and our rudder, we can stay on course.
